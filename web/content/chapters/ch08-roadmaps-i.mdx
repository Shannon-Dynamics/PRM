Chapters 6 and 7 answered one query at a time. This chapter builds something to keep: a network of
one-dimensional curves in $\Qfree$ that captures everything a planner needs about the space, so
that every later query is "get on, ride, get off". The surprise is that *two very different
curves qualify*. The **visibility graph** strings obstacle vertices together along lines of sight,
and it contains the shortest path — one that grazes every corner it turns. The **generalized
Voronoi diagram** is the set of points equally far from the two nearest obstacles — the path that
stays as far from everything as it can. One optimizes length, the other clearance; both are
roadmaps because both satisfy the same three-word contract.

Proving the contract for the GVD brings in two ideas that run through the rest of Part II. A
**deformation retraction** melts the free space onto its skeleton without tearing, so every loop in
the free space survives as a loop on the skeleton — which is why the skeleton is connected
wherever the space is. The **preimage theorem** says that one equation in the plane cuts out a
curve wherever its gradient does not vanish, which is why the GVD is one-dimensional and also why
its definition needs a refinement that a range sensor would have insisted on anyway. The theorem
arrives with the Morse vocabulary — nondegenerate critical points, their index, the Euler
characteristic — introduced in a definition box here and used without ceremony in Chapters 9 and 10.

Everything on this page runs. The visibility graph is built by Choset's rotational sweep and
checked against the brute-force definition at sixteen hundred vertices; the exact Voronoi diagram
of the chapter's slab is traced by Chapter 3's predictor–corrector to $10^{-12}$ and its meet point
lands on $(2, 1.5)$ to nine digits; the Apartment's GVD is a brushfire ridge, accessed by gradient
ascent from a hundred and forty-five seeded points, every one of which arrives.
</Overview>

The lattice path is Chapter 6's answer and it is fine. The visibility path is shorter: $6.7$ m on
average over fifty seeded queries in the chapter's check, against $10.7$ m for the Voronoi path.
The Voronoi path is safer: its minimum clearance averages $0.48$ m against $0.22$ m — the
visibility path runs exactly at Rusty's radius from the walls, because that is what "shortest"
means for a disc. Rusty, being a disc with odometry error and a controller that overshoots, might
prefer the second — or might not, if the corridor is long and the battery is low. The point of the
chapter is not that one is right. It is that both are *roadmaps*: built once, searched many times,
with the same three guarantees.

## Building intuition

### The highway contract

<KeyIdea title="Definition 5.0.2 — roadmap">
A union of one-dimensional curves $RM$ is a **roadmap** if for every $\qstart$ and $\qgoal$ in
$\Qfree$ that can be connected by a path: (1) **accessibility** — there is a path from $\qstart$
to some $q'_{start} \in RM$; (2) **departability** — there is a path from some $q'_{goal} \in RM$
to $\qgoal$; (3) **connectivity** — there is a path *in* $RM$ from $q'_{start}$ to $q'_{goal}$.
</KeyIdea>

That is Los Angeles. You plan a path onto the 110, ride to the 105 and the 405, and get off at the
beach; the side streets are never searched. For a robot the payoff is that the search happens in a
one-dimensional set, however many dimensions $\Q$ has, and that building the roadmap can be done
once — or, with a range sensor, incrementally while exploring. The visibility graph satisfies (1)
and (2) by definition: every point of a polygonal free space sees some vertex. The GVD satisfies
them by *gradient ascent*: walk away from the nearest obstacle until a second one is equally near.
Connectivity is the theorem in each case.

### A lighthouse

</Deferred>

The obvious way to decide which vertices $v$ can see is to test the segment to each one against
every obstacle edge — $O(n^2)$ per vertex, $O(n^3)$ for the graph. The sweep is a different
idea: a beam of light turning about $v$ illuminates, at any moment, the nearest thing in its
direction, and the nearest thing changes only when the beam passes a vertex. So pause only at
vertex angles, keep the list $S$ of edges the beam currently crosses sorted by distance, and ask
of each vertex a single question: *is it nearer than the nearest edge in $S$?* The table beside
the canvas is Choset's Table 5.1 filling in as the beam turns; three vertices pass the test and the
other five are behind something. When the sweep finishes, the full graph appears with the A\* path
from $v$ to the goal, and the *reduced* toggle fades every edge that does not lie on a supporting
or separating line — the path length does not move. That is the misconception this widget kills:
visibility does not cost $O(n)$ tests per pair, and most of the edges it finds are never needed.

the Voronoi diagram of polygons is *not* made of straight lines.

### Melting the candy

Imagine a doughnut-shaped candy dissolving. What remains at the end is a ring, far smaller than
the candy but with the same hole. The Retract Candy does this to the Apartment: every free point

every $t$. Then flip to *retract to a point*. That map is also continuous and also fixes its image;
it is a retraction. But the orange loop has to cross the island to reach the point — the red marks

identity, and that is the property that keeps the holes.

### Visibility

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — visibility graph; supporting and separating lines; reduced graph (Choset §5.1.1)">
In a polygonal planar $\Qfree$, the **visibility graph** has as nodes $\qstart$, $\qgoal$ and every
vertex of every C-obstacle, and an edge $e_{ij}$ between $v_i$ and $v_j$ iff $s v_i + (1 - s) v_j
\in \mathrm{cl}(\Qfree)$ for all $s \in [0, 1]$. Obstacle edges are edges of the graph. A
**supporting line** is tangent to two obstacles with both on one side; a **separating line** is
tangent to two obstacles with them on opposite sides; for a polygon, tangency at a vertex $v_i$
is the nonsmooth kind, $B_\epsilon(v_i) \cap l \cap \QO_i = \{v_i\}$. The **reduced visibility
graph** keeps only edges on supporting or separating lines. A boundary is **locally convex** at
$\bar c$ if the tangent line there separates the nearby boundary points; at a reflex vertex no
line is tangent, so the reduction removes every edge at it.
</KeyIdea>

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-graph}{e_{ij} \ne \emptyset} \iff s\, v_i + (1 - s)\, v_j \in \mathrm{cl}(\Qfree)\ \ \forall s \in [0, 1],
\qquad
\htmlClass{term-path}{\text{shortest path}} = \text{A}^\star\ \text{on}\ \htmlClass{term-graph}{(V, E)}\ \text{from}\ \htmlClass{term-start}{\qstart}\ \text{to}\ \htmlClass{term-goal}{\qgoal}.
$$
</LinkedMath>

<Derivation title="The visibility graph contains a shortest path" result="\text{bends only at obstacle vertices} \Rightarrow \text{a path in the visibility graph}">
**Statement** (Choset §5.1.1). In a polygonal planar $\Qfree$ a Euclidean-shortest path from
$\qstart$ to $\qgoal$ is a polyline whose interior vertices are obstacle vertices; hence it is a
path in the visibility graph, and A\* with the Euclidean heuristic finds it.

**Step 1 — locally shortest means straight.** Any sub-arc of a shortest path is shortest between
its endpoints; in the open free space the shortest curve between two points is the segment, so the
path is straight wherever it is not touching an obstacle.

**Step 2 — bends live on the boundary.** A bend at a point interior to the free space could be
shortcut by a chord; so bends occur only on $\partial \Qfree$.

**Step 3 — not on an edge's interior.** A bend at a point interior to an obstacle edge could be
shortcut too: the chord between two nearby path points stays on the free side of the edge's line.
So bends occur only at vertices.

**Step 4 — consecutive bends see each other.** Between two bends the path is a segment in
$\mathrm{cl}(\Qfree)$, which is the definition of a visibility edge. $\blacksquare$

*Connectivity* is Choset's Problem 1 and this chapter's Exercise 1: within a component of $\Qfree$,
every vertex sees some vertex, and the obstacle edges close the chain. *What breaks in
$\mathbb{R}^3$:* a shortest path around a polyhedron bends on edges, not at vertices, so the
visibility graph of vertices misses it (Choset §5.1.1; Exercise 1). *Taut paths use only supporting
and separating lines:* at a bend the path wraps the vertex, so both polygon neighbours of the
vertex lie on one side of the path's line — the nonsmooth tangency above. That is why the
reduction loses no shortest path, and the check confirms it: on fifty seeded fields the full and
reduced graphs give the same start-to-goal length every time, with $3{,}875$ edges in place of
$8{,}386$. The same check samples $405$ random collision-free polylines from start to goal and
finds none shorter than A\* on the graph.
</Derivation>

<Derivation title="Rotational plane sweep is correct and runs in O(n² log n)" result="v_i \text{ visible} \iff |v v_i| < \text{dist to the nearest edge in } S \text{ and } l \text{ does not enter the obstacle at } v">
**Statement** (Choset §5.1.2, Algorithm 5). Sweep a half-line $l$ from $v$ through $[0, 2\pi)$,
pausing at the vertex angles $\alpha_i$ in increasing order, and maintain the set $S$ of edges
crossing $l$ sorted by distance from $v$. Then $v_i$ is visible iff the segment $v v_i$ does not
cross the nearest edge of $S$ and $l$ does not pass through the interior of the obstacle at $v$.

**Step 1 — nothing happens between vertices.** The set of edges crossing $l$ changes only when $l$
passes an endpoint of an edge, that is, a vertex. So pausing at the $\alpha_i$ loses nothing.

**Step 2 — only the nearest crossed edge can occlude.** Every edge in $S$ crosses $l$ at some
distance; if the nearest crossing lies beyond $v_i$ the segment $v v_i$ crosses none of them, and
no edge outside $S$ crosses $l$ at all. Two exceptions are handled by hand: edges incident to $v_i$
reach it exactly and do not occlude it, and when $v$ is itself a vertex, directions into its own
polygon are blocked whatever $S$ says.

**Step 3 — bookkeeping is logarithmic.** At $\alpha_i$ the two edges at $v_i$ either end (delete
from $S$) or begin (insert, by distance along $l$). Edges in $S$ do not cross one another, so their
order by distance is invariant while they are in $S$; with a balanced tree each operation is
$O(\log n)$.

**Step 4 — the count.** $n$ events per vertex, each $O(\log n)$, plus an $O(n \log n)$ sort:
$O(n \log n)$ per vertex and $O(n^2 \log n)$ for the graph. $\blacksquare$

*Table 5.1, reproduced.* For Fig. 5.8 — rectangle 1 on $[2, 4] \times [-1, 1]$, rectangle 2 on
$[6, 10] \times [-2, 4]$, $v$ at the origin — the initial list is $S = \{E_4, E_2, E_8, E_6\}$, the
events come in the order $\alpha_3, \alpha_7, \alpha_4, \alpha_8, \alpha_1, \alpha_5, \alpha_2,
\alpha_6$, and the edges added are exactly $(v, v_4)$, $(v, v_8)$, $(v, v_1)$. The port's row for
$\alpha_1$ reads $\{E_4, E_1\}$ where Choset prints $\{E_1, E_4\}$; his next row, $\{E_4, E_1,
E_8, E_5\}$, agrees with the port, and the two cannot both be sorted by distance — just past
$\alpha_1$ the beam meets $E_4$ (the line $x = 2$) before $E_1$ (the line $y = -1$). *Two
honesty items:* the TypeScript port keeps $S$ as a sorted array, $O(n)$ per update rather than
$O(\log n)$, which changes the constant and not the idea; and general position — no three
vertices collinear — is assumed, as Choset assumes it, with the perturbation he suggests as the
fix. The check runs the sweep and the brute-force definition at $1{,}598$ vertices over fifty
seeded convex fields and finds no disagreement.
</Derivation>

### The generalized Voronoi diagram

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — Voronoi region, two-equidistant sets, the GVD (Choset §5.2.1, §5.2.4)">
The **generalized Voronoi region** of $\QO_i$ is $F_i = \{q \in \Qfree : d_i(q) \le d_h(q)\
\forall h \ne i\}$ (eq. 5.1). The **two-equidistant surface** is $S_{ij} = \{q : d_i(q) - d_j(q) =
0\}$; its **surjective** subset is $SS_{ij} = \{q \in S_{ij} : \nabla d_i(q) \ne \nabla d_j(q)\}$;
the **two-equidistant face** is $F_{ij} = \{q \in SS_{ij} : d_i(q) \le d_h(q)\ \forall h\}$. The
**GVD** is $\bigcup_i \bigcup_j F_{ij}$. In the plane the $F_{ij}$ are **GVD edges**, ending at
**meet points** $F_{ijk}$ (equidistant to three closest obstacles) or **boundary points** (where
$D = 0$, the ends of the diagram's spokes).
</KeyIdea>

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — retraction, homotopy, deformation retraction, π₁ (Choset §5.2.3)">
For a manifold $X$ and $A \subset X$, a **retraction** is a continuous $f : X \to A$ with $f(a) =
a$ for all $a \in A$; $A$ is the **retract**. A **homotopy** between $f, g : U \to V$ is a
continuous $H : U \times [0, 1] \to V$ with $H(\cdot, 0) = f$ and $H(\cdot, 1) = g$; then $f \sim
g$. A **deformation retraction** is a homotopy $H : X \times [0, 1] \to X$ with $H(x, 0) = x$,
$H(x, 1) \in A$ and $H(a, t) = a$ for $a \in A$ — a retraction homotopic to the identity — and
$A$ is a **deformation retract**. The **first fundamental group** $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ is the group of
homotopy classes of loops through $x_0$ under concatenation; a deformation retraction preserves
it: $\pi_1(X, x_0) = \pi_1(A, f(x_0))$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Accessibility of the GVD" result="\dot c = \nabla D(c) \text{ reaches the GVD from any } q \in \Qfree">
**Statement** (Choset Lemma 5.2.1). In an obstacle-bounded environment, gradient ascent of the
distance function, $\dot c(t) = \nabla D(c(t))$ with $c(0) = \qstart$ (eq. 5.2), traces a path from
any free point to the GVD.

**Step 1 — the flow is $\nabla d_i$.** Let $\QO_i$ be the unique closest obstacle at $q$. Nearby,
$D = d_i$ and the ascent follows $\nabla d_i$, the unit vector away from the closest point
(Chapter 7, Derivation 2).

**Step 2 — $d_i$ grows, something else must stop it.** Along the flow $d_i$ increases at unit
rate. The environment is bounded, so $d_i$ cannot grow forever; by continuity of the distance
functions some $d_j$ must stop exceeding $d_i$.

**Step 3 — the tie.** There is a $\bar t$ with $d_i(c(\bar t)) = d_j(c(\bar t))$, a point of
$S_{ij}$. $\blacksquare$

*Two refinements the port needs.* First, the tie must be in $SS_{ij}$: two collinear wall pieces
sharing an endpoint tie at every point beyond the endpoint with $\nabla d_i = \nabla d_j$, and
that is not a Voronoi edge — the ascent skips such ties and continues with the new nearest
obstacle. Second, the crossing is detected as a change of nearest obstacle between two steps and
then bisected, so the landing point is on $S_{ij}$ to $10^{-15}$ rather than within a step of it.
*Departability* is accessibility run backward from the goal. Choset adds that every free point sees
some GVD point along a segment, so a robot that can sense the goal may simply drive at it once in
sight. *The numbers.* From $145$ seeded free points of the Apartment every ascent lands, with
$|d_i - d_j|$ at most $3 \times 10^{-15}$, and every landing point lies within $0.11$ m of the
$0.1$ m grid GVD below; on the open slab, $107$ of $120$ starts land within one trace step of the
traced diagram and the other $13$, below the pegs, walk out of the window — the lemma's
boundedness hypothesis is not decoration.
</Derivation>

<Derivation title="The GVD is a deformation retract, hence connected, hence a roadmap" result="RM \sim \mathrm{id},\quad \pi_1(\Qfree) = \pi_1(\mathrm{GVD})">
**Statement** (Choset §5.2.2–5.2.3). The map $RM : \Qfree \to \mathrm{GVD}$ that sends $q$ to the
landing point of its gradient ascent (and fixes the GVD) is a retraction homotopic to the
identity. Consequently each connected component of $\Qfree$ contains exactly one connected
component of the GVD, and the GVD has the same loop classes as the free space.

**Step 1 — it is a retraction.** $RM(q) = q$ on the GVD by definition; elsewhere it is the ascent
endpoint, which exists by Lemma 5.2.1.

**Step 2 — it is continuous.** Nearby starts follow nearby flow lines of the piecewise-smooth
field $\nabla D$ and land nearby (Choset cites [340] for the proof).

**Step 3 — it is homotopic to the identity.** $H(q, t) = c_q(t)$, the point at fraction $t$ of
$q$'s own ascent path, is continuous in $(q, t)$, equals $q$ at $t = 0$, equals $RM(q)$ at $t =
1$, and fixes the GVD throughout. That is a deformation retraction.

**Step 4 — connectivity.** The image of a connected set under a continuous map is connected, so
$RM$ carries each component of $\Qfree$ onto a connected piece of the GVD; and since the GVD lies
in $\Qfree$ and $RM$ fixes it, that piece is a whole component.

**Step 5 — loops survive.** Deformation retracts preserve $\pi_1$, so a loop in $\Qfree$ that
cannot be shrunk to a point — one around an island — retracts to a loop on the GVD that cannot
either. $\blacksquare$

*Retract versus deformation retract.* A point is a retract of the filled doughnut (send
everything to it) but not a deformation retract: the straight-line homotopy would have to pass
through the hole. The Retract Candy shows exactly this — the loop around the island keeps winding
number $1$ about it at $t = 0$, $\tfrac12$ and $1$ under the gradient-ascent homotopy, every one of
its $48$ points lands on the GVD and none enters the island, while the straight-line retraction to
a corridor point pierces an obstacle on $14$ of the $48$ rays. The $0.1$ m grid GVD of the
Apartment has one connected component, as the free space does.
</Derivation>

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — regular value, critical point, critical value (Choset §5.2.4)">
For smooth $G : M \to N$, a point $m$ is a **critical point** if the differential $DG(m)$ is not
surjective; its image $G(m)$ is a **critical value**; $\Sigma(G)$ is the set of critical points.
A value $n \in N$ is **regular** if $DG(m)$ is surjective for every $m \in G^{-1}(n)$. For a
real-valued $G$ the differential is the row vector $(\nabla G)^\top$, surjective iff $\nabla G
\ne 0$.
</KeyIdea>

<KeyIdea title="Definition box — Morse function (introduced here; imported by Chapters 9–10)">
A smooth real-valued $f$ is **Morse** if every critical point is **nondegenerate** (nonsingular
Hessian). Nondegenerate critical points are isolated, and each has an **index**: the number of
negative eigenvalues of the Hessian — $0$ a minimum, $\dim M$ a maximum, anything else a saddle.
The topology of the level sets $f^{-1}(\lambda)$ changes only as $\lambda$ crosses a critical
value, and $\sum (-1)^{\text{index}}$ over the critical points is the Euler characteristic of $M$.
*Example:* $h(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \cos\theta_1 + \cos\theta_2$ on Reach's torus has four critical
points, of index $2, 1, 1, 0$ — a maximum at $(0, 0)$, saddles at $(\pi, 0)$ and $(0, \pi)$, a
minimum at $(\pi, \pi)$ — and $1 - 2 + 1 = 0 = \chi(T^2)$; the check computes the indices from
Chapter 7's Hessian. Chapter 7's navigation functions are Morse by definition. The distance
function $D$ is *not* smooth, which is why Chapter 9 introduces the generalized gradient.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The planar GVD consists of one-dimensional manifolds" result="SS_{ij} \subset (d_i - d_j)^{-1}(0) \text{ is a 1-manifold},\quad T_q SS_{ij} = \ker (\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j)^\top">
**Statement** (Choset Theorem 5.2.2, the preimage theorem). Let $G : M \to N$ be smooth and $n$ a
regular value. Then $G^{-1}(n)$ is a closed submanifold of $M$ of dimension $\dim M - \dim N$, with
tangent space $T_m G^{-1}(n) = \ker DG(m)$. For $G = d_i - d_j : \Q \to \mathbb{R}$ the set
$SS_{ij}$ is one-dimensional in the plane.

**Step 1 — the differential.** $D(d_i - d_j) = (\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j)^\top$, a $1 \times 2$ row.

**Step 2 — surjective iff the gradients differ.** A $1 \times 2$ row is surjective onto
$\mathbb{R}$ iff it is nonzero, i.e. iff $\nabla d_i(q) \ne \nabla d_j(q)$.

**Step 3 — that is the definition of $SS_{ij}$.** The word *surjective* in "two-equidistant
surjective surface" is this condition. On $SS_{ij}$, $0$ is a regular value of $d_i - d_j$, so
$SS_{ij}$ is a submanifold of dimension $2 - 1 = 1$, with tangent perpendicular to $\nabla d_i -
\nabla d_j$.

**Step 4 — faces are submanifolds.** $F_{ij} \subset SS_{ij}$ is cut out by the closed conditions
$d_i \le d_h$; it is a submanifold with boundary, and the GVD is a finite union of them.
$\blacksquare$

*The warm-up.* $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ has $Df = [2x, 2y]$, nonzero everywhere on $f^{-1}(91{,}538)$
— the check finds $|Df| = 605.1$ there — so that preimage is a one-manifold: a circle. *Why
the refinement is needed.* Cut a nonconvex obstacle into two convex pieces in two different ways
(Choset Fig. 5.10) and you get two different $S_{ij}$ — but the same $SS_{ij}$, because the parts
that differ are exactly those where the two closest points coincide and the gradients line up.
A robot at $q_2$ *outside* the concavity sees one local minimum of $\rho$, not two (Fig. 5.11):
the sensor could never see the cut, and $SS_{ij}$ is the definition that agrees with the sensor.
*The tangent.* $\ker (\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j)^\top$ is perpendicular to the chord joining the two
closest points (Fig. 5.13): on the slab, at the meet point $(2, 1.5)$, $\nabla d_{P_1} = (0.8,
0.6)$ and $\nabla d_W = (0, -1)$, so the tangent along $P_1 | W$ is $\propto (1, -0.5)$ — slope
$-0.5$, and $(1, -0.5) \cdot (2, 4) = 0$ against the chord from $P_1$ to the wall's foot $(2, 4)$.
That tangent is also what Chapter 3's tracer follows.
</Derivation>

<Derivation title="Size and construction of the polygonal GVD" result="\tfrac{3(n+1)}{2} \le \#\text{edges} \le 6N + 3n - 3,\qquad \tfrac{n+5}{2} \le \#\text{nodes} \le 4N - n - 2">
**Statement** (Choset §5.2.5). In a polygonal world with $n$ obstacles and $N$ vertices, the GVD's
edges are straight (vertex–vertex, edge–edge) or parabolic (vertex–edge), and their number lies
between $\tfrac{3(n+1)}{2}$ and $6N + 3n - 3$; the nodes number between $\tfrac{n+5}{2}$ and $4N -
n - 2$ [Choset ref. 359].

**Step 1 — three bisectors.** Points equidistant from two points lie on a line; from two lines, on
their angle bisectors; from a point and a line, on a parabola with that focus and directrix.

**Step 2 — partition by feature pair.** The plane splits into regions by which pair of features
(vertex or edge) is closest (Fig. 5.14); on each region the GVD edge is one of the three curves.

**Step 3 — count.** The GVD is a planar graph; Euler's formula with the bounds on how many
feature pairs can be adjacent gives the counts. $\blacksquare$

*Brushfire.* Choset's third construction is Chapter 7's: a pixel the brushfire reaches by two fronts
with different back pointers is a GVD pixel, and `ridge_cells` returns them. The grid version of the
$SS_{ij}$ refinement matters here too: the Apartment's walls are one connected obstacle, so "two
different obstacles" finds nothing until the back pointer is the *obstacle pixel* of origin and a
pixel is called a ridge pixel where a neighbour's origin lies far from its own with a different
direction — the discrete form of two distinct closest points with distinct gradients. The algorithm
section says how the port does it and what it costs.
</Derivation>

## The algorithm

<Algorithm
  name="ROTATIONAL PLANE SWEEP  —  Choset Alg. 5"
  inputs="a set of vertices {v_i} whose edges do not intersect, and a vertex v"
  outputs="the subset of {v_i} within line of sight of v"
  complexity="O(n log n) per vertex with S a balanced tree (the port: a sorted array, O(n) per update); O(n² log n) for the graph"
>
1. **for** each vertex $v_i$: compute $\alpha_i$, the angle from the horizontal axis to $v v_i$
2. create the vertex list $E$, the $\alpha_i$ sorted in increasing order
3. create the active list $S$: the edges crossing the horizontal half-line from $v$, sorted by distance
4. **for all** $\alpha_i$ **do**
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $v_i$ is visible to $v$ — $|v v_i|$ is less than the distance to the nearest edge of $S$ not incident to $v_i$, and $l$ does not enter the obstacle at $v$ — **then** add $(v, v_i)$ to the graph
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $v_i$ is the beginning of an edge not in $S$ **then** insert it into $S$ by distance along $l$
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $v_i$ is the end of an edge in $S$ **then** delete it
8. **end for**

*Reduced graph:* keep $(v_i, v_j)$ iff the line through it is tangent at both ends — at a polygon
vertex, both polygon neighbours on one side of the line (free nodes impose nothing). *Brute
force*, for the check: $(v_i, v_j)$ is an edge iff no polygon edge not incident to either endpoint
properly crosses the segment and its midpoint is not strictly inside any polygon; the second
clause is what rejects a chord of a convex obstacle.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="GVD BY RETRACTION AND TRACING  —  Choset eq. (5.2), §5.2.5 (sensor-based construction)"
  inputs="a distance query giving d_i and ∇d_i for every obstacle (geometry here; Chapter 9 feeds it from range data); a window"
  outputs="landing points on SS_ij, traced edges F_ij with their meet and boundary points"
  complexity="retraction: O(#obstacles) per step; tracing: one predictor–corrector step per sample"
>
1. **access** (`retract`): from $q$, step along $\nabla d_i$ for the nearest $i$; when the nearest obstacle changes to $j$ and $\angle(\nabla d_i, \nabla d_j) > 0$, bisect $d_i - d_j$ on the last step to $10^{-12}$ — a point of $SS_{ij}$; a tie with equal gradients is skipped
2. **seed** (`projectToGvd`): for lattice points, Newton along $\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j$ onto $S_{ij}$ for the two nearest sites; keep the point only if they are still the two nearest there and the gradients differ
3. **trace** (`traceEdge`): Chapter 3's `traceCurve` on $G = d_i - d_j$ with tangent $(\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j)^\perp$, both ways from the seed, until a third site ties (polish the **meet point** by Newton on $(d_i - d_j,\ d_i - d_k)$), $D$ vanishes (a **boundary point**), or the window is left
4. **collect** (`exactGvd`): skip seeds within a step of an edge already traced for the same pair; each edge carries its samples, its ends, and its analytic type per sample — *line* when both closest features are of the same kind, *parabola* when one is a vertex and the other an edge
5. **ride**: `Roadmap` is a `Graph` for Chapter 6's A\*; a query is `retract(q_s)`, `retract(q_g)`, A\*

*What this is and is not.* The Rust crate delegates the exact Voronoi diagram of segments to a
library kernel (Boost.Polygon's algorithm); no such kernel exists in TypeScript, and the port does
not pretend to be one. Tracing is exact to the tracer's tolerance — $4.8 \times 10^{-13}$ against
the slab's parabolas — and comfortable for a dozen sites; it is Choset's sensor-based construction
with the sensor replaced by geometry, which is also why Chapter 9 can reuse it unchanged.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="GRID GVD  —  Choset §5.2.5 (brushfire method), with the SS_ij refinement in grid form"
  inputs="an occupancy grid; Chapter 7's brushfire; for connected obstacles, a Euclidean feature transform"
  outputs="ridge cells, a ridge graph for A*, an access map from any free cell"
  complexity="O(#cells) brushfire; O(#cells log #cells) feature transform; A* on the ridge"
>
1. **separate obstacles** (the slab, a room with furniture): Chapter 7's `brushfire` and its `ridge` — pixels two fronts from different obstacle components reach
2. **connected obstacles** (the Apartment's walls): propagate each pixel's nearest obstacle *pixel* under the Euclidean distance (Danielsson's vector distance transform, a Dijkstra over 8-neighbours keyed on the true distance to the propagated origin); pixel $p$ is a ridge pixel when a neighbour's origin lies at least $\gamma = 2.5$ cells from $p$'s, the two directions differ by more than $20°$, and $p$ is no nearer the obstacles than the neighbour
3. **ridge graph**: 8-adjacent ridge cells with Euclidean step costs; `thinRidge` (one Zhang–Suen pass, heuristic) when a one-pixel skeleton is wanted
4. **access**: climb the brushfire labels to a ridge cell; **ride**: A\* on the ridge graph; **depart**: the goal's climb, reversed

*Why not brushfire origins for step 2:* brushfire's labels are a grid metric and its origin pixels
are tie-broken by queue order, so along a long wall two neighbouring pixels can carry origins
many cells apart — a false ridge. The Euclidean features are the quantity Chapter 7 compared
brushfire against, and the ridge they give is within $0.11$ m of every exact landing point in the
Apartment check. *Grid ridges are jagged* (Choset Problem 14): on the slab at $0.25$ m the
8-point ridge passes $0.18$ m from the exact meet point and the 4-point ridge $0.40$ m, and the
grid meet cells sit at $y \approx 2$ (chessboard) and $y \approx 1.1$ (Manhattan), bracketing
$y = 1.5$.
</Algorithm>

## Implementation in Rust

The `roadmap` crate is introduced here; Chapter 9 adds `gvg` as a submodule. Its spine is a graph
embedded in the free space together with the access map of Def. 5.0.2.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/lib.rs"
use nalgebra::Point2;
use petgraph::graph::{NodeIndex, UnGraph};

/// A roadmap: a graph embedded in Q_free with the geometry of each edge sampled, plus the
/// access map of Def. 5.0.2. "Ride" is `search::astar` on `graph` — nothing is re-implemented.
pub struct Roadmap {
    pub graph: UnGraph<Point2<f64>, f64>,
    /// One polyline per edge, in edge-index order: a chord for the visibility graph, a traced curve for the GVD.
    pub polylines: Vec<Vec<Point2<f64>>>,
}

/// Accessibility as a trait: where does q get on, and along what path?
pub trait Access {
    fn access(&self, q: Point2<f64>) -> Option<(NodeIndex, Vec<Point2<f64>>)>;
}

pub struct QueryResult { pub path: Vec<Point2<f64>>, pub length: f64, pub min_clearance: f64 }

/// Get on, ride, get off. `None` when start and goal land in different roadmap components —
/// which, for a roadmap, means they are in different components of Q_free.
pub fn query(rm: &Roadmap, acc: &dyn Access, q_s: Point2<f64>, q_g: Point2<f64>, d: &dyn Fn(Point2<f64>) -> f64) -> Option<QueryResult> {
    let (ns, on) = acc.access(q_s)?;
    let (ng, off) = acc.access(q_g)?;
    let ride = search::astar_graph(&rm.graph, ns, ng, |n| (rm.graph[n] - rm.graph[ng]).norm())?;
    let mut path = on;
    for w in ride.path.windows(2) {
        let e = rm.graph.find_edge(w[0], w[1]).unwrap();
        path.extend(rm.polylines[e.index()].iter().skip(1));
    }
    path.extend(off.into_iter().rev().skip(1));
    let length = path.windows(2).map(|w| (w[1] - w[0]).norm()).sum();
    let min_clearance = path.iter().map(|p| d(*p)).fold(f64::INFINITY, f64::min);
    Some(QueryResult { path, length, min_clearance })
}
```

The sweep keeps $S$ in a `BTreeMap` keyed by distance along the current ray — the balanced tree
the $O(n \log n)$ bound assumes.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/visibility.rs" {22-31}
use std::collections::BTreeMap;
use ordered_float::OrderedFloat;

/// Choset Alg. 5 for one vertex `v`. `polys` are the C-obstacles (general position assumed).
pub fn rotational_sweep(polys: &[cspace::Polygon], v: Point2<f64>) -> Vec<Point2<f64>> {
    let (nodes, edges) = index_vertices_and_edges(polys);
    // Step 1–2: angles α_i in [0, 2π), sorted (ties broken by distance).
    let mut events: Vec<(f64, usize)> = nodes.iter().enumerate()
        .filter(|(_, n)| n.p != v)
        .map(|(i, n)| (angle_from(v, n.p), i)).collect();
    events.sort_by(|a, b| a.0.partial_cmp(&b.0).unwrap());
    // Step 3: S = edges crossing the horizontal half-line, keyed by crossing distance.
    let mut s: BTreeMap<OrderedFloat<f64>, usize> = edges.iter().enumerate()
        .filter(|(_, e)| !incident(e, v) && crosses_half_line(e, v))
        .map(|(i, e)| (OrderedFloat(ray_distance(v, 0.0, e)), i)).collect();
    let mut visible = Vec::new();
    for (alpha, i) in events {
        let vi = nodes[i].p;
        let d = (vi - v).norm();
        // Step 5: nearest crossed edge not incident to v_i; the sweep line must not enter the obstacle at v.
        let nearest = s.iter().filter(|(_, &e)| !incident_to(&edges[e], i))
            .map(|(_, &e)| ray_distance(v, alpha, &edges[e])).fold(f64::INFINITY, f64::min);
        if d < nearest - 1e-9 && !enters_obstacle_at(polys, v, vi - v) && !midpoint_buried(polys, v, vi) {
            visible.push(vi);
        }
        // Steps 6–7: an incident edge already in S ends here; otherwise it begins. Key by the
        // distance just past α so two edges starting at v_i are ordered the way the beam meets them.
        for &e in &nodes[i].incident {
            if let Some(k) = s.iter().find(|(_, &x)| x == e).map(|(k, _)| *k) { s.remove(&k); }
            else { s.insert(OrderedFloat(ray_distance(v, alpha + 1e-7, &edges[e])), e); }
        }
    }
    visible
}

/// Keep only edges on supporting or separating lines: tangent (both polygon neighbours on one
/// side) at both ends. At a reflex vertex nothing is tangent — the local-convexity test of §5.1.1.
pub fn reduced(polys: &[cspace::Polygon], g: &Roadmap) -> Roadmap { /* filter edges by tangent_at(a, b) && tangent_at(b, a) */ filter_tangent(polys, g) }
```

Tracing the GVD is Chapter 3's tracer on $G = d_i - d_j$; a meet point is two equations in two
unknowns.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvd/retract.rs · src/gvd/mod.rs" {6-18}
/// Eq. (5.2): follow ∇D until two obstacles tie with distinct gradients (SS_ij). A tie with
/// equal gradients — collinear wall pieces beyond their shared endpoint — is not the GVD and is
/// skipped. The crossing is bisected so the landing point is on S_ij to 1e-12.
pub fn retract(dist: &dyn cspace::DistanceQuery<2>, q0: Point2<f64>, step: f64, bounds: Aabb) -> Retraction {
    let mut q = q0; let mut path = vec![q0];
    let mut ds = sorted(dist.distances(&q));
    for _ in 0..MAX_STEPS {
        let (i, g) = (ds[0].index, ds[0].grad);
        let next = q + step * g;
        if !bounds.contains(next) { return Retraction::left_window(path); }
        let ds_next = sorted(dist.distances(&next));
        let j = ds_next[0].index;
        if j != i && angle(dist.grad_of(i, &q), dist.grad_of(j, &q)) > GRAD_TOL {
            let land = bisect(|t| { let p = q.lerp(&next, t); dist.d_of(i, &p) - dist.d_of(j, &p) }, q, next, 60);
            path.push(land);
            return Retraction::landed(path, [i, j]);
        }
        q = next; ds = ds_next; path.push(q);
    }
    Retraction::budget(path)
}

/// A meet point F_ijk: Newton on F(q) = (d_i − d_j, d_i − d_k) with J = [∇d_i − ∇d_j; ∇d_i − ∇d_k].
/// Rejected unless i, j, k are the three *closest* sites at the solution.
pub fn meet_point(sites: &[Site], i: usize, j: usize, k: usize, seed: Point2<f64>) -> Option<MeetPoint> { newton_2x2(sites, [i, j, k], seed, 60, 1e-12) }

/// Sensor-based edge following (§5.2.5): Chapter 3's predictor–corrector on G = d_i − d_j, tangent
/// (∇d_i − ∇d_j)^⊥ — perpendicular to the chord between the two closest points (Fig. 5.13).
pub fn trace_edge(sites: &[Site], i: usize, j: usize, q0: Point2<f64>, turn: Turn, step: f64, bounds: Aabb) -> (Vec<Point2<f64>>, EdgeEnd) {
    let g = |q: Point2<f64>| sites[i].distance(q).d - sites[j].distance(q).d;
    let dg = |q: Point2<f64>| sites[i].distance(q).grad - sites[j].distance(q).grad;
    let stop = |q: Point2<f64>| !bounds.contains(q) || clearance(sites, q) < step || third_site_ties(sites, i, j, q);
    let tr = bugs::trace_curve(&g, &dg, q0, turn, bugs::TraceParams { step, newton_iters: 4, tol: 1e-12, ..Default::default() }, Some(&stop));
    (tr.points.clone(), classify_end(sites, i, j, &tr))
}
```

The grid GVD reuses Chapter 7's brushfire for separate obstacles and adds the Euclidean feature
transform for connected walls.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvd/grid.rs"
/// Pixel p is on the grid GVD when a neighbour's nearest obstacle pixel lies ≥ γ cells from p's,
/// in a direction differing by more than `min_angle`, and p is no nearer the obstacles than the
/// neighbour. Features come from a Euclidean vector distance transform (Dijkstra keyed on the
/// true distance to the propagated origin pixel); brushfire's grid-metric origins are tie-broken
/// by queue order and would call long straight walls ridges.
pub fn ridge_from_features(occ: &cspace::Raster, ft: &FeatureTransform, gamma: f64, min_angle: f64) -> Vec<bool> {
    let mut mask = vec![false; occ.len()];
    for p in occ.free_pixels() {
        let o = ft.origin[p];
        for q in occ.neighbors8(p).filter(|&q| !occ.is_occupied(q) && ft.origin[q] != o && ft.dist2[q] <= ft.dist2[p]) {
            let oq = ft.origin[q];
            if occ.pixel_distance(o, oq) < gamma { continue; }
            if occ.direction_angle(p, o, oq) < min_angle { continue; }
            mask[p] = true; break;
        }
    }
    mask
}
```

<Callout type="info">
Two things the port had to learn about grids. First, a wall lying exactly on the window's edge —
the Apartment's shell — vanishes from a raster whose occupancy test is "centre within half a cell",
because the centres are exactly half a cell away; with the shell gone the free space is unbounded,
the feature transform is off by metres along the border, and the "GVD" is nonsense. The inflation is
now a hair over half a cell. Second, the brushfire ridge of Chapter 7 names *obstacle components*,
and the Apartment is one component; the GVD of a single nonconvex obstacle is empty until $SS_{ij}$
asks for two distinct closest points, and the grid needs the Euclidean feature transform to ask it
reliably. Neither is a bug in Choset; both are places where the discrete object needs the same
refinement the continuous definition did.
</Callout>

## Putting it together: fifty queries, two highways

The integration lab is the hook run to statistics. Both roadmaps are built once for disc-Rusty in
the Apartment: the visibility graph on the walls inflated by $0.22$ m — $150$ nodes, built by the
sweep — and the GVD from the $0.1$ m raster's feature ridge, $1{,}592$ cells in one connected
component. Fifty seeded start–goal pairs at least two metres apart are answered on each: retract,
ride, depart. Every pair is answered on both. The visibility path is shorter in every case and
$6.68$ m on average; the GVD path is $10.70$ m on average and its minimum clearance, $0.48$ m, is
more than twice the visibility path's $0.22$ m — which is Rusty's radius, exactly, because the
shortest path touches the inflated walls. The check pins the means to two decimals and asserts
the two inequalities pair by pair.

Reach's half of the lab is the GVD of its configuration space: the Chapter 4 raster of the
Workbench C-obstacle on $T^2$, run through the same feature transform with wrap-around adjacency,
gives the skeleton of the free torus — the curves along which the arm stays as far from the block,
the post and the shelf as it can in *joint* space. Exercise 6 builds it; Chapter 10 uses it.

Three pointers close the chapter. The planar GVD is one-dimensional because one equation in two
unknowns cuts out a curve; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ the same equation cuts out two-dimensional *sheets*,
and the roadmap must be the intersection of sheets — the generalized Voronoi graph of Chapter 9,
which also builds the GVD from range data with the tracer this chapter already used. Chapter 10's
Morse decomposition takes the definition box literally: the cells of a coverage plan are the
pieces between critical values of a sweep function. And Chapter 11 will call "sample near the GVD"
medial-axis sampling, and will state the roadmap contract one more time, for a graph built by
throwing darts.
