[Chapter 1](/chapters/ch01-robots-that-plan) promised that the piano is a point. This chapter pays.
It answers the four questions Choset opens his Chapter 3 with — how much information specifies a
robot's position, how to represent it, what its mathematical properties are, and how obstacles in
the workspace become constraints on it — for every case that can be computed exactly, and it opens
the collision box that [Chapter 2](/chapters/ch02-the-lab) deliberately sealed.

The idea the whole chapter turns on is one sentence long and easy to say wrong. A configuration
space obstacle is **not a picture of the workspace obstacle**. It is the set of configurations $q$ at
which a *function* of the configuration, $R(q)$, meets the obstacle. For a disc that set happens to
look like the obstacle, grown by the radius — and Choset warns on his page 44 that this is the one
case where it does. For a polygon that rotates, it is a Minkowski difference computed one vertex at
a time, and the vertices re-pair every time the robot turns. For a two-joint arm it is a blob on a
torus that nobody can draw by hand and that a planner must nonetheless search. The widget at the top
of this page draws it live; the mathematics below says why it has the shape it has; the Rust at the
end computes it, and the tests hold every printed number to the code.

Two things from Choset's §3.8 ride along, because every later planner for Reach — the potential
fields of [Chapter 7](/chapters/ch07-potential-functions), the dynamics of
[Chapter 17](/chapters/ch17-robot-dynamics), the steering of
[Chapter 21](/chapters/ch21-nonholonomic-ii) — needs them already: the Jacobian
$J(q) = \partial\varphi/\partial q$ that turns joint velocities into tip velocities, and the
configurations $\sin\theta_2 = 0$ where it loses rank. The topology that makes the torus wrap, and
why the square is only a chart of it, waits for [Chapter 5](/chapters/ch05-configuration-space-ii).
</Overview>

## The problem: a block, and its shadow

Below, a slate block slides across the Workbench on a seeded path while Reach, the two-joint arm,
sweeps through its configurations. Beside it, the square $[-\pi, \pi]^2$ of joint angles — the torus
$T^2$ cut open and flattened — with an amber region that stretches, splits, and rejoins as the block
moves. Before reading on: what is the amber region?

<Deferred>
  <CSpaceMorph />
</Deferred>

It is the set of joint-angle pairs $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ at which some point of the arm lies inside
the block. Choset calls it the **configuration space obstacle** $\QO$, and three things about it are
the whole chapter.

**It is not the block.** The block is a square in the plane. Its shadow on the torus is a curved blob
whose shape depends on where the block is, how long the links are, and which link can reach it. Slide
the block toward the base and a vertical band appears — link 1 now hits it for *every* $\theta_2$, so
a whole column of the square is forbidden. Slide it the other way and the blob shrinks toward a point.
Drag it in one direction and the blob can move in the other. The misconception this widget exists to
kill is that the C-obstacle "looks like" the obstacle. It is a different shape in a different space.

**It is the preimage of a function.** Write $R(q)$ for the set of workspace points the arm occupies
at configuration $q$. The amber region is $\{q : R(q) \cap \text{block} \ne \emptyset\}$, the set of
inputs for which a function of $q$ meets a fixed set. Every property of the blob — its convexity or
lack of it, its connectedness, whether it wraps across the seam — is a property of that function.
This is also why the same code draws the disc's C-space in the next section: nothing about the
rasterizer knows what an arm is. It asks a `Collision` checker one question per cell.

**It is a picture, not a certificate.** The amber cells are the configurations the raster *tested*.
A path through the white cells passes through configurations no one tested. Choset's §3.2.2 says this
plainly — the grid "only encodes collision information for the discrete set of points lying on the
grid" — and offers the remedy the widget's *link thickness* slider implements: thicken the robot so
that a free sample certifies a free neighborhood. The planner is then conservative and only
*resolution complete*. We will come back to this.

The readout under the torus counts cells and components. Load the *micro-example square* preset and
the $\theta_1 = 0°$ row shows exactly $29$ occupied cells. That number is the chapter's handshake
between hand, raster and code: the next section derives it with a protractor, the Rust at the end
prints it, and a test asserts it.

<KeyIdea title="The one sentence version">
A configuration space obstacle is the set of configurations at which the robot's footprint
$\Rq$ meets the workspace obstacle. For a disc that is the obstacle grown by $r$; for a convex
polygon at fixed heading it is a Minkowski difference, built vertex by vertex from the two bodies'
edge normals; for an arm it is a region of the torus that only a collision checker can draw. In every
case the obstacle lives in the space of configurations, and planning happens there.
</KeyIdea>

## Building intuition

### A disc only grows

Start where the picture *is* easy. Choset's figure 3.5 puts three circular robots of increasing radius
in one room with a doorway and asks each to cross it. The widget below is that figure with numbers:
a $4 \times 5$ m room, a $0.6$ m doorway in the dividing wall, one convex crate, and three discs —
a point, Rusty at $r = 0.11$ m, and a third whose radius you set.

<Deferred>
  <DiscInflation />
</Deferred>

For a disc translating in the plane the configuration is the center $(x, y)$, so $\Q = \mathbb{R}^2$
and it is tempting to think of configuration space as the workspace. It is not — they are different
spaces that happen to have the same coordinates — but the C-obstacle of a disc does have a closed
form. The disc of radius $r$ at $q$ meets the crate iff some crate point lies within $r$ of $q$, so
$\QO$ is the crate with every point pushed outward by $r$: edges slide out by $r$, corners become
quarter-circles, and walls — zero-width obstacles — become capsules of width $2r$. Choset's phrase is
that "we 'grow' the polygon outward and the walls inward." The point robot then plans in the grown
map.

Three numbers to check against the readouts. The point robot's free area is the room less the crate,
$20 - 0.4 = 19.60\ \mathrm{m}^2$. For Rusty the crate's C-obstacle has area $|\WO| + r\,p + \pi r^2
= 0.4 + 0.11 \cdot 2.6 + \pi \cdot 0.0121 = 0.7240\ \mathrm{m}^2$ — the original, a strip of width
$r$ along the perimeter $p$, and corner arcs that together make exactly one disc. And the doorway,
$0.6$ m wide, narrows to $0.6 - 2r$: Rusty passes with $0.19$ m to spare on each side, the free space
is one component, and the purple path goes through. Raise the third radius past $0.30$ m and the two
capsules meet in the doorway; $\Qfree$ falls into **two** components and the query has no answer —
which is exactly what Choset's figure 3.5(c) shows and what no amount of clever search can fix. The
check `doorway` pins the component counts $1/1/2$ for $r = 0, 0.11, 0.35$ and the free area
$19.60\ \mathrm{m}^2$.

Now the honesty item this widget is really about. The disc inflation is *correct* — "inflate by the
radius and plan for a point" is exactly right — and it is right **only for discs**. A disc has no
orientation, so its footprint is the same set translated; for any body that rotates, $R(q)$ changes
shape with $\theta$, and the "grow the obstacle" picture fails at the first corner. The Minkowski
Studio in the mathematics section shows what replaces it.

### An arm's C-obstacle is a shape in a different space

Here is the micro-example that runs through the rest of the chapter, chosen so that every number can
be checked with a protractor. Reach has $L_1 = L_2 = 1$, zero-thickness links, base at the origin;
the only obstacle is a small square $\WO = [1.4, 1.6] \times [-0.1, 0.1]$ — side $0.2$, centered at
$(1.5, 0)$. Link 1 has length $1$ and the square begins at $x = 1.4$, so link 1 can never touch it:
the C-obstacle comes from **link 2 alone**.

Five configurations by hand.

- **(a)** $q = (0°, 0°)$. Link 2 runs from the elbow $(1, 0)$ to the tip $(2, 0)$, straight through
  the square's center. **Collision.**
- **(b)** $q = (0°, 13°)$. Link 2 leaves $(1, 0)$ at $13°$; at $x = 1.4$ its height is
  $0.4 \tan 13° = 0.092 < 0.1$, inside the square's left edge. **Collision.**
- **(c)** $q = (0°, 15°)$. Height at $x = 1.4$ is $0.4 \tan 15° = 0.107 > 0.1$: the link clears the
  corner $(1.4, 0.1)$. **Free.**
- **(d)** $q = (45°, -90°)$. Elbow at $(0.7071, 0.7071)$, link 2 at absolute heading $-45°$, tip at
  $(\sqrt2, 0) = (1.4142, 0)$ — inside the square. **Collision.**
- **(e)** $q = (90°, \theta_2)$ for any $\theta_2$. Elbow at $(0, 1)$; the nearest point of the
  square is the corner $(1.4, 0.1)$ at distance $\sqrt{1.96 + 0.81} = 1.664 > L_2$. **Free for every**
  $\theta_2$.

So the slice of $\QO$ at $\theta_1 = 0$ is exactly $|\theta_2| \le \arctan(0.1/0.4) = \arctan\tfrac14
= 0.2450$ rad $= 14.04°$, the angle the near corner subtends from the elbow. On a $1°$ raster whose
cells are centered at integer degrees, that is the cells $\theta_2 = -14°, \dots, 14°$: $2 \cdot 14 +
1 = 29$ cells out of $360$ — the number the widget's readout shows for the preset. Generalizing (e),
the $\theta_1$-extent of the blob is the set of elbow positions within $L_2 = 1$ of the square:
$(1.4 - \cos\theta_1)^2 + (\sin\theta_1 - 0.1)^2 \le 1$, i.e. $2.8\cos\theta_1 + 0.2\sin\theta_1 \ge
1.97$, i.e. $|\theta_1| \lesssim 0.864$ rad $= 49.5°$. On the raster, the rows $\theta_1 = \pm 49°$
hold one cell each and the rows $\pm 50°$ are empty; the blob is one connected region of $2{,}015$
cells ($1.55\%$ of $T^2$) and touches no seam. The check `cspace` asserts all of it, and bisects the
exact segment test along the $\theta_1 = 0$ row to recover $\arctan\tfrac14$ to $10^{-9}$.

Compare this with the square itself: a $0.2 \times 0.2$ box. Its shadow on the torus is a curved
lens about $99°$ wide and $29°$ tall at its waist, with no straight edges and no right angles. That
is the picture to carry into the mathematics.

### Why "grow the obstacle" fails for a polygon

One more experiment before the definitions. Suppose the robot is a convex polygon that translates at
a fixed heading $\theta$ among a convex obstacle. Its C-obstacle is again convex, and it is again
built from the obstacle and the robot — but *not* by growing the obstacle: it is the obstacle with
the *reflected* robot swept along its boundary, the **Minkowski difference** $\WO \ominus R$. Turn the
robot and the sweep changes; the C-obstacle's vertex count can change with it. The Minkowski Studio,
placed beside the star algorithm below, computes it one vertex at a time. The misconception it kills
is that Minkowski sums are expensive. For convex polygons they are a *merge of two sorted lists*.

### Configuration, configuration space, degrees of freedom

<KeyIdea title="Definitions (Choset §3.1) — configuration, C-space, degrees of freedom">
The **configuration** of a robot system is a complete specification of the position of every point
of that system. The **configuration space** $\Q$ is the space of all configurations; a
configuration $q$ is a point of it. The number of **degrees of freedom** is $\dim \Q$ — equivalently,
the minimum number of parameters needed to specify a configuration. $\Rq \subset \W$ denotes the set
of workspace points the robot occupies at $q$.
</KeyIdea>

For the disc, $q = (x, y)$ and $R(x, y) = \{(x', y') : (x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 \le r^2\}$, so
$\Q = \mathbb{R}^2$. For the two-joint arm, $q = (\theta_1, \theta_2)$ with each $\theta_i \in S^1$,
so $\Q = S^1 \times S^1 = T^2$, the torus. Choset's figure 3.2 cuts the torus along $\theta_1 = 0$ and
$\theta_2 = 0$ and flattens it to a square whose opposite edges are identified — the square the
widgets draw, with its seams marked. The *workspace* of the arm, in the sense of the set of points
the end effector reaches, is an annulus; it is **not** a configuration space, because every interior
point is reached by two configurations (elbow-up and elbow-down), so a tip position does not specify
where every point of the robot is. Chapter 2's `Reach::ik` returns both.

### C-obstacles, free space, and the two kinds of path

<KeyIdea title="Definitions (Choset §3.2) — C-obstacle, free space, free and semifree paths">
The **configuration space obstacle** of workspace obstacle $\WO_i$ is
$\QO_i = \{q \in \Q : \Rq \cap \WO_i \ne \emptyset\}$. The **free space** is
$\Qfree = \Q \setminus \bigcup_i \QO_i$, assumed open unless noted. A **free path** is a continuous
$c : [0, 1] \to \Qfree$; a **semifree path** is a continuous $c : [0, 1] \to \operatorname{cl}(\Qfree)$,
which may touch obstacle boundaries.
</KeyIdea>

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-cobstacle}{\QO_i} = \{\, q \in \Q : \htmlClass{term-robot}{\Rq} \cap
\htmlClass{term-obstacle}{\WO_i} \neq \emptyset \,\},
\qquad
\Qfree = \Q \setminus \bigcup_i \htmlClass{term-cobstacle}{\QO_i}.
$$
</LinkedMath>

Hover a tinted term and every figure on the page mutes the other roles: the amber of the C-obstacle
is the amber of the torus, the slate of the workspace obstacle is the slate of the block. The
definition is a *preimage*: $\QO_i$ is the set of $q$ that the map $q \mapsto \Rq$ sends into the
sets that meet $\WO_i$. Two consequences follow at once and both matter for code. First, obstacles
union: $\QO(\WO_i \cup \WO_j) = \QO_i \cup \QO_j$, because $\Rq$ meets a union iff it meets a member
(Choset problem 3.5) — so a rasterizer may ask one checker about a whole `Scene` rather than one
obstacle at a time. Second, for a *convex* robot translating against a *convex* obstacle, $\QO$ is
convex (problem 3.4; the star algorithm below is its constructive proof).

<Derivation title="Derivation 1 — The disc's C-obstacle is a Minkowski inflation (Choset §3.2.1)" result="\QO = \WO \oplus B_r(0),\qquad |\WO \oplus B_r| = |\WO| + r\,p + \pi r^2">
**Statement.** For a disc of radius $r$ translating in the plane, with configuration its center $q$,
$\QO_i = \WO_i \oplus B_r(0) = \{o + b : o \in \WO_i,\ \|b\| \le r\}$. For a convex polygon $\WO$ with
perimeter $p$ the inflated area is $|\WO| + r\,p + \pi r^2$.

**Step 1 — the preimage, unpacked.** $R(q) = q + B_r(0)$. Then $R(q) \cap \WO \ne \emptyset$ iff
there is an $o \in \WO$ with $\|q - o\| \le r$ iff $q = o + b$ for some $o \in \WO$, $\|b\| \le r$,
iff $q \in \WO \oplus B_r$. Nothing about the disc's shape entered except that it is a ball about
its reference point: the sum is exactly the obstacle "grown" by $r$.

**Step 2 — the boundary.** The boundary of $\WO \oplus B_r$ for a convex polygon consists of each edge
offset outward by $r$ along its normal, joined at each vertex by a circular arc of radius $r$
sweeping from the incoming edge's normal to the outgoing edge's normal. `inflateDisc` builds exactly
this: `arcSegments` chords per full turn, so the chord polygon approaches the true inflation from
the inside.

**Step 3 — the area.** The offset strips contribute $r$ times the perimeter $p$. The arcs at the
vertices have angles that sum to the total turning of a convex polygon, $2\pi$, so together they make
one full disc, $\pi r^2$. For $\WO = [1, 2]^2$ and $r = 0.5$: $1 + 0.5 \cdot 4 + \pi \cdot 0.25 =
\mathbf{3.7854}$. The check `inflate` asserts the closed form and that the chord polygon's area
climbs toward it from below — $3.7804$ with $32$ chords, $3.7854$ with $720$. $\blacksquare$

*Choset's warning* (his page 44): "although both the workspace and the configuration space for this
system can be represented by $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the obstacles appear to simply 'grow' in this
example, the configuration space and workspace are different spaces, and the transformation from
workspace obstacles to configuration space obstacles is not always so simple." A rotating body's
footprint is not a ball about any point; Derivations 3 and 4 are what "not so simple" costs.
</Derivation>

### Dimension: counting by constraints

<KeyIdea title="Definitions (Choset §3.3) — holonomic and nonholonomic constraints; mobility; redundancy">
A **holonomic constraint** is an equation on the configuration variables, $g(q, t) = 0$. Each linearly
independent holonomic constraint reduces $\dim \Q$ by one: $n$ coordinates with $m$ independent
holonomic constraints give an $(n - m)$-dimensional configuration space. A **nonholonomic constraint**
is a velocity constraint $g(q, \dot q, t) = 0$ and removes no dimension — Chapter 20's subject. For a
**closed chain** of $k$ links and $n$ joints with $f_i$ degrees of freedom at joint $i$, in an ambient
space with $N$ rigid-body DOF ($3$ planar, $6$ spatial), the **mobility** is Grübler's
$M = N(k - n - 1) + \sum_i f_i$, valid when the joint constraints are independent. A robot with more
DOF than its task needs is **redundant**.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Derivation 2 — Dimension by point counting (Choset §3.3)" result="\dim \Q = 3 \text{ (planar body)},\; 6 \text{ (spatial body)},\; 12 - 10 = 2 \text{ (Reach)}">
**Statement.** A planar rigid body has three degrees of freedom and a spatial one six; a system of
$n$ coordinates with $m$ independent holonomic constraints has $n - m$.

**Step 1 — place $A$ freely.** Fix three non-collinear points $A, B, C$ on the body. $A$'s position
is free: $2$ coordinates in the plane, $3$ in space.

**Step 2 — $B$ is constrained to a circle (sphere).** Rigidity fixes $d(A, B)$, so $B$ lies on a
circle about $A$ in the plane — one angle $\theta$ remains — or on a sphere in space, where two
angles remain.

**Step 3 — $C$ follows, up to a discrete choice.** Two fixed distances $d(A, C)$, $d(B, C)$ place
$C$ in the plane up to a reflection: zero continuous freedoms remain. In space, three distances leave
$C$ on a circle about the axis $AB$: one angle remains. Every other point of the body is then fixed by
its three distances. Totals: $2 + 1 = 3$ and $3 + 2 + 1 = 6$, with configuration spaces
$\mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1$ and $\mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$. $\blacksquare$

*Reach, counted the indirect way* (Choset's own example). Treat each link as a spatial rigid body:
$12$ coordinates. Six constraints confine the two links to the table plane, two pin the first joint
to a point, and — once link 1's angle is chosen — two pin the second joint to the end of link 1:
$12 - 10 = 2$. The direct count is shorter: one revolute joint, one degree of freedom, twice. *A
closed chain*: Choset's figure 3.9 has six links and seven revolute joints in the plane, so
$M = 3(6 - 7 - 1) + 7 = 1$ — a one-parameter mechanism, like the four-bar linkage of problem 3.7.
</Derivation>

Choset's table 3.1 collects the configuration spaces this book plans in, and the inequalities beside
it are the ones a planner written against coordinates cannot see:

| Robot | $\Q$ |
|---|---|
| Mobile robot translating in the plane (Rusty as a disc) | $\mathbb{R}^2$ |
| Mobile robot translating and rotating in the plane (Rusty, Hitch's chassis) | $SE(2)$ or $\mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1$ |
| Rigid body translating in space | $\mathbb{R}^3$ |
| A spacecraft | $SE(3)$ or $\mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$ |
| An $n$-joint revolute arm (Reach) | $T^n$ |
| A planar mobile robot with an attached $n$-joint arm | $SE(2) \times T^n$ |

$S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1 = T^n \ne S^n$; $S^1 \times S^1 \times S^1 \ne SO(3)$; $SE(2) \ne
\mathbb{R}^3$; $SE(3) \ne \mathbb{R}^6$. The torus is compact and the plane is not; this chapter
*uses* the difference (the blob wraps, the seam is a lie of the chart) and Chapter 5 proves it.

### Polygons as intersections of half-planes

<KeyIdea title="Definitions (Choset App. F.1) — half-planes, convex polygonal region, polygon">
A line $h(x, y) = ax + by - c = 0$ with unit normal $(a, b)$ splits the plane into the **positive
half-plane** $h^+ = \{h \ge 0\}$ and the **negative half-plane** $h^- = \{h \le 0\}$. A **convex
polygonal region** is an intersection of finitely many negative half-planes (by convention; multiply
$h$ by $-1$ to flip a side). A **polygonal region** is a finite union of convex ones, not necessarily
bounded or connected. A **polygon** is a closed, simply connected polygonal region — a polygonal
region homeomorphic to the closed unit disc.
</KeyIdea>

The convention is what makes the rest of the appendix mechanical. List a convex polygon's vertices
counterclockwise; edge $i$ runs from $r_i$ to $r_{i+1}$; its outward unit normal $n_i$ is the edge
direction rotated clockwise by $90°$; its half-plane is $h_i(x, y) = n_i \cdot ((x, y) - r_i) \le 0$.
A point is inside iff every $h_i \le 0$. Choset's figure F.2 is the triangle $h_1 = -x + y - 3$,
$h_2 = -y$, $h_3 = x$, whose negative half-planes intersect in the region $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$,
$y \le x + 3$ — wait: $x \le 0$. Read the signs: $h_3 = x \le 0$ is the *left* half-plane. The
convention is easy to say and easy to get backwards, which is why `halfPlaneOfEdge` fixes it once and
every polygon in the module reads the same way.

### Contacts, and the half-planes they define

<KeyIdea title="Definition (Choset App. F.2) — Type A and Type B contacts">
Fix the robot's heading $\theta$. Two convex polygons touch without overlapping — eq. F.2,
$R(q) \cap W \ne \emptyset$ and $\operatorname{int} R(q) \cap \operatorname{int} W = \emptyset$ — in
exactly two ways. A **Type A contact**: an edge $E^R_i$ of the robot contains a vertex $o_j$ of the
obstacle. A **Type B contact**: an edge $E^W_j$ of the obstacle contains a vertex $r_i$ of the robot.
(Both at once, figure F.4(c), is an edge lying along an edge.)
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Derivation 3 — Contact half-planes characterize collision for convex polygons (App. F.2)" result="q \in \QO(\theta) \iff f^R_{ij}(q) \le 0 \;\forall\text{ applicable A},\quad f^W_{ij}(q) \le 0 \;\forall\text{ applicable B}">
**Statement.** For fixed $\theta$, $\QO(\theta) = \{(x, y) : (x, y, \theta) \in \QO\}$ is a convex
polygon, and $q \in \QO(\theta)$ iff every *applicable* contact's half-plane inequality holds. A
Type A pair $(E^R_i, o_j)$ is applicable (eq. F.3) when

$$
(o_{j-1} - o_j) \cdot n^R_i(\theta) \ge 0 \quad\text{and}\quad (o_{j+1} - o_j) \cdot n^R_i(\theta) \ge 0,
$$

equivalently when the *negated* robot normal $-n^R_i$ lies between the normals of the two obstacle
edges meeting at $o_j$; its half-plane is (eq. F.4) $f^R_{ij}(q) = n^R_i(\theta) \cdot (o_j -
r_i(x, y, \theta)) \le 0$. Symmetrically a Type B pair $(E^W_j, r_i)$ is applicable (F.5) when
$(r_{i-1} - r_i) \cdot n^W_j \ge 0$ and $(r_{i+1} - r_i) \cdot n^W_j \ge 0$, with half-plane (F.6)
$f^W_{ij}(q) = n^W_j \cdot (r_i(x, y, \theta) - o_j) \le 0$.

**Step 1 — the boundary is where the bodies touch.** If the interiors overlap at $q$, every nearby
$q'$ is also in $\QO(\theta)$, so $q$ is interior to $\QO(\theta)$. Boundary configurations are
therefore exactly those satisfying F.2: contact without overlap.

**Step 2 — only Type A and Type B realize contact.** Two convex polygons whose interiors are disjoint
meet in a point or a segment of their boundaries; the meeting set contains a vertex of one body lying
on an edge of the other (or an edge along an edge, which is both). That is the dichotomy.

**Step 3 — each applicable pair gives a supporting half-plane.** Take an applicable Type A pair. The
condition F.3 says both obstacle edges leaving $o_j$ point into or along the robot normal's
half-space, so the whole obstacle lies on the side $n^R_i \cdot (o - r_i) \ge n^R_i \cdot (o_j -
r_i)$; the robot edge $E^R_i$ can touch the obstacle only at $o_j$. Translating the robot, the
contact persists while $n^R_i \cdot (o_j - r_i(q)) = 0$ — a line in the $(x, y)$-plane — and the robot
overlaps the obstacle exactly on the side $f^R_{ij} \le 0$. So $\QO(\theta) \subseteq \{f^R_{ij} \le
0\}$ and the line is a *supporting line* of $\QO(\theta)$. Type B is the same with roles swapped.
Non-applicable pairs give nothing: a robot edge whose normal does not point at $o_j$ cannot touch it
from outside.

**Step 4 — a convex set is the intersection of its supporting half-planes.** $\QO(\theta)$ is convex
(it is a Minkowski difference of convex sets, Derivation 4), and every edge of its boundary is a
contact line from Step 3, so $\QO(\theta)$ equals the intersection of the applicable half-planes.
Hence $q \in \QO(\theta)$ iff all applicable inequalities hold — and *one* failing inequality is a
certificate of separation. $\blacksquare$

*Why $n^R_i$ depends on $\theta$ only.* The robot's normals rotate with it and do not move with it;
applicability is a function of $\theta$ alone, and the star algorithm exploits this by sorting
normals once per heading.

*Nonconvex bodies.* Decompose both into convex pieces $\{R_l\}$, $\{W_k\}$ and test every pair; the
union of the pairwise $\QO$'s is the C-obstacle (the union property above).
</Derivation>

### The star algorithm

<Deferred>
  <MinkowskiStudio />
</Deferred>

<Derivation title="Derivation 4 — The star algorithm computes QO(θ) = WO ⊖ R(0,0,θ) in linear time after sorting (App. F.3)" result="\QO(\theta) = \WO \ominus R(0, 0, \theta) = \operatorname{hull}\{\, o_j - r_i(\theta) \,\}">
**Statement.** Negate the robot's edge normals; merge-sort them with the obstacle's by angle; scan
the merged list once. Each time a negated robot normal $-n^R_i$ falls between adjacent obstacle
normals, a Type A contact of $E^R_i$ with the vertex $o_j$ between those normals is applicable, and it
contributes the vertices $o_j - r_i$ and $o_j - r_{i+1}$; each time an obstacle normal $n^W_j$ falls
between adjacent negated robot normals, a Type B contact of $E^W_j$ with $r_i$ is applicable and
contributes $o_j - r_i$ and $o_{j+1} - r_i$. The emitted points, in scan order, are the vertices of
$\QO(\theta)$ counterclockwise, and $\QO(\theta)$ equals the Minkowski difference (eq. F.7)
$\WO \ominus R = \{o - r : o \in \WO,\ r \in R\}$.

**Step 1 — contacts persist along edges, and their extremes are vertices.** Fix an applicable Type A
pair. Slide the robot so that $o_j$ stays on edge $E^R_i$: the configuration traces a segment of the
boundary of $\QO(\theta)$. At one extreme $o_j$ coincides with $r_i$, at the other with $r_{i+1}$;
the reference point is then at $o_j - r_i(0, 0, \theta)$ and $o_j - r_{i+1}(0, 0, \theta)$. These are
two vertices of $\QO(\theta)$, and the edge between them is a translate of $E^R_i$ — with outward
normal $-n^R_i$, since the robot is reflected through its reference point. Symmetrically a Type B
pair gives the vertices $o_j - r_i$ and $o_{j+1} - r_i$, joined by a translate of $E^W_j$ with normal
$n^W_j$.

**Step 2 — angular order of normals is boundary order.** The boundary of a convex polygon, walked
counterclockwise, has outward normals that rotate monotonically through $2\pi$. The boundary of
$\QO(\theta)$ is made of translates of robot edges (normals $-n^R_i$) and obstacle edges (normals
$n^W_j$), so walking it counterclockwise visits those edges in the angular order of
$\{-n^R_i\} \cup \{n^W_j\}$. Merging the two already-sorted lists *is* that walk.

**Step 3 — the merged scan visits every applicable pair exactly once.** Keep a current robot vertex
$i$ and obstacle vertex $j$, starting from the pair extreme in the $+x$ direction. Consuming a robot
entry means walking the (reflected) robot edge $E^R_i$ while $o_j$ stays put — a Type A contact — and
advancing $i$; consuming an obstacle entry means walking $E^W_j$ while $r_i$ stays put — Type B — and
advancing $j$. Each entry is consumed once, so each applicable contact is emitted once and the scan
closes on its start after $n_R + n_W$ steps. Cost: $O((n_R + n_W)\log(n_R + n_W))$ for a general
sort, $O(n_R + n_W)$ here because each list is already in order.

**Step 4 — equality with the hull of all pairwise differences.** $\WO \ominus R = \WO \oplus (-R)$
is a Minkowski sum of convex polygons, hence convex, and its extreme points are differences of
extreme points, so it is the convex hull of the $n_R n_W$ points $o_j - r_i$. The star algorithm's
vertices are among these points and its edges have the correct normals, so the two polygons coincide.
That hull is `minkowskiBruteForce`, the oracle the check `star` holds the scan to on $500$ seeded
convex pairs at random headings: identical polygons, worst area gap $2 \times 10^{-15}$.
$\blacksquare$

*The preset, worked.* Robot $(0, 0), (0.2, 0), (0, 0.2)$ at $\theta = 0$; obstacle $[1, 2]^2$. The
merged list is $-n^R_3$ at $0°$, $n^W_1$ at $0°$, $-n^R_1$ at $90°$, $n^W_2$ at $90°$, $n^W_3$ at
$180°$, $-n^R_2$ at $225°$, $n^W_4$ at $270°$ — seven entries, seven steps. The emitted points are
$(2, 0.8), (2, 1), (2, 2), (1.8, 2), (0.8, 2), (0.8, 1), (1, 0.8)$; two of them, $(2, 1)$ and $(1.8,
2)$, lie on edges because a robot edge is parallel to an obstacle edge, and dropping collinear
vertices leaves the five the design doc names — $(1, 0.8), (2, 0.8), (2, 2), (0.8, 2), (0.8, 1)$ —
with area $1 + 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.02 = \mathbf{1.42}$: the square, two strips, and the reflected triangle
in the corner. Turn the robot to $\theta = 30°$ and no edges are parallel: **seven** vertices. The
check `star` pins all of it.
</Derivation>

### GJK: distance as the distance from a Minkowski difference to the origin

<Derivation title="Derivation 5 — GJK computes the distance between convex polytopes from support functions alone (App. F.5, Algorithm 23)" result="d(A, B) = \min_{z \in A \ominus B} \|z\|,\qquad h_{A \ominus B}(x) = h_A(x) + h_B(-x),\quad s_{A \ominus B}(x) = s_A(x) - s_B(-x)">
**Statement.** $d(A, B) = \min_{a \in A, b \in B} \|a - b\| = \min_{z \in Z} \|z\|$ with
$Z = A \ominus B$ (eqs. F.8–F.10); the minimizer $z^*$ is unique; and Gilbert, Johnson and Keerthi's
iteration finds it using only the support value $h_Z(x) = \max_{z \in Z} z \cdot x$ and support point
$s_Z(x)$ (F.11–F.12), which never require $Z$ to be built.

**Step 1 — rewrite over the Minkowski difference.** Every $a - b$ is a point of $Z$ and every point
of $Z$ is some $a - b$, so the two minima are the same number (F.10). The distance between two bodies
is the distance from one convex set to the origin.

**Step 2 — uniqueness.** $Z$ is convex (Derivation 4, Step 4) and $\|\cdot\|$ is a strictly convex
function away from the origin, so $\arg\min_{z \in Z} \|z\|$ is a single point $z^*$. The witness pair
$(a^*, b^*)$ with $a^* - b^* = z^*$ need *not* be unique — two parallel edges facing each other have a
whole segment of nearest pairs — and Choset says so after F.10. Uniqueness is of the difference.

**Step 3 — the simplex iteration.** Keep a working set $V_k$ of at most three points of $Z$ (a point,
a segment or a triangle in the plane) and let $x_k$ be the point of $\operatorname{hull}(V_k)$
nearest the origin. Ask $Z$ for its support point in direction $-x_k$: $z_k = s_Z(-x_k)$. If
$h_Z(-x_k) = -\|x_k\|^2$ — no point of $Z$ projects farther toward the origin than $x_k$ itself —
then $x_k = z^*$ and the iteration stops (Algorithm 23, step 4). Otherwise $z_k$ is strictly closer to
the origin in the direction $-x_k$, so $\operatorname{hull}(V_k \cup \{z_k\})$ contains points nearer
than $x_k$; keep the edge of the old simplex that contained $x_k$ plus $z_k$ (step 6) and repeat. The
distance to the origin strictly decreases, the working set never revisits a vertex of $Z$ in exact
arithmetic, and $Z$ has at most $n_A + n_B$ vertices, so the loop terminates in at most that many
steps. If the origin lands inside the triangle, $d = 0$ and the bodies intersect.

**Step 4 — support functions compose, so $Z$ is never built.** $h_{A \ominus B}(x) = \max\{(a - b)
\cdot x\} = \max_a a \cdot x + \max_b b \cdot (-x) = h_A(x) + h_B(-x)$ (F.13), and if $a^*$, $b^*$
attain those maxima then $s_{A \ominus B}(x) = a^* - b^*$ (F.14). For a polygon the support point is
the vertex with the largest dot product — a scan of $n$ vertices — so one GJK step costs
$O(n_A + n_B)$ and the whole query is linear with a tiny constant. $\blacksquare$

*What the check pins.* `gjk` runs the two-dimensional iteration against Chapter 2's brute-force
polygon distance on $500$ seeded convex pairs ($22$ of them intersecting): the distances agree to
$4 \times 10^{-16}$, the witnesses realize the distance, the intersection verdict matches the
separating-axis test, and no query needs more than $5$ iterations — well under the $n_A + n_B$ bound.

*What `parry2d` adds.* The engine the Rust crate delegates to runs this same loop with a
bounding-volume hierarchy to skip far pairs, EPA (expanding polytope algorithm) to recover the
*penetration* depth when the origin is inside $Z$, and shape-casting — GJK along a motion — for the
swept query of Chapter 2's `Collision::swept`. Choset's one-sentence extension to polyhedra is to
start from four points and keep a face instead of an edge.
</Derivation>

### Reach's Jacobian and its singularities

<Deferred>
  <JacobianLens />
</Deferred>

Chapter 2 gave Reach's forward kinematics $\varphi : T^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$,
$\varphi(q) = (L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2),\ L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1 +
\theta_2))$. Choset's §3.8 asks how velocities transform: as the arm moves, $\dot x = \tfrac{\partial
\varphi}{\partial q}\dot q = J(q)\dot q$, where $J$ is the Jacobian of $\varphi$, also called its
differential $D\varphi$ — the linear map that Chapter 5 will show is independent of the chart.

<KeyIdea title="Definition (Choset §3.8) — singular configuration">
A configuration $q$ is **singular** when $J(q)$ loses rank. At such a $q$ the $n$-dimensional set of
joint velocities $\dot q$ maps to a lower-dimensional set of end-point velocities $\dot x$:
instantaneous end-point motion is impossible in some direction.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Derivation 6 — Reach's Jacobian, det J = L₁L₂ sin θ₂, and Example 3.8.1 (Choset §3.8)" result="J(q) = \begin{bmatrix} -L_1\sin\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)\end{bmatrix},\quad \det J = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2">
**Statement.** The matrix above is $\partial\varphi/\partial q$; its determinant is
$L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2$; the arm is singular exactly when $\sin\theta_2 = 0$ — straight or folded.
Choset's numbers: $L_1 = L_2 = 1$, $q = (\pi/4, \pi/2)$, $\dot q = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ give
$J = \begin{bmatrix} -\sqrt2 & -\sqrt2/2 \\ 0 & -\sqrt2/2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\det J = 1$, and
$\dot x = (-\sqrt2, 0)^{\mathsf T}$.

**Step 1 — differentiate entrywise.** $\partial\varphi_1/\partial\theta_1 = -L_1\sin\theta_1 -
L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$; $\partial\varphi_1/\partial\theta_2 = -L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$;
$\partial\varphi_2/\partial\theta_1 = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$;
$\partial\varphi_2/\partial\theta_2 = L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. Read the columns: column $i$ is
the tip velocity when joint $i$ alone turns at unit rate — every link beyond joint $i$ swings about
it, so the column is the sum over those links of $L_k$ times the link's heading rotated by $90°$. That
sentence is the implementation of `jacobian`, and it serves any number of links.

**Step 2 — the determinant.** Write $s_1 = \sin\theta_1$, $c_1 = \cos\theta_1$, $s_{12} =
\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$, $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. Then
$\det J = (-L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12})(L_2 c_{12}) - (-L_2 s_{12})(L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12}) = -L_1 L_2 s_1
c_{12} + L_1 L_2 s_{12} c_1 = L_1 L_2 \sin((\theta_1 + \theta_2) - \theta_1) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2$
by the sine difference identity.

**Step 3 — what rank loss means.** At $\sin\theta_2 = 0$ the two columns are parallel (both are
multiples of $(-\sin\theta_1, \cos\theta_1)$ when the arm is straight), so the image of
$\dot q \mapsto J\dot q$ is a line perpendicular to the arm. Motion *along* the arm is instantaneously
impossible — the tip is at the edge of the annulus and can only move tangentially. The arm has not
broken; one direction is unavailable, and a planner that asks for it asks for $\dot q = J^{-1}\dot x$
with $\det J \to 0$: infinite joint speed. Chapters 17–18 meet this as a velocity limit that goes to
zero; Chapter 21 as a rank condition.

**Step 4 — Choset's numbers.** At $q = (\pi/4, \pi/2)$: $\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 3\pi/4$, $s_1 = c_1 =
\sqrt2/2$, $s_{12} = \sqrt2/2$, $c_{12} = -\sqrt2/2$. So $J_{11} = -\sqrt2/2 - \sqrt2/2 = -\sqrt2$,
$J_{12} = -\sqrt2/2$, $J_{21} = \sqrt2/2 - \sqrt2/2 = 0$, $J_{22} = -\sqrt2/2$; $\det J = \sin(\pi/2)
= 1$; and $J (1, 0)^{\mathsf T} = (-\sqrt2, 0)^{\mathsf T} = (-1.4142, 0)$ — the tip, at
$(0, \sqrt2)$, moves straight left when the shoulder alone turns, as his figure 3.21 draws. The check
`jacobian` asserts the four entries, the determinant and $\dot x$ to $10^{-12}$, and holds `jacobian`
to central differences of `Reach::fk` on $300$ seeded arms to $10^{-7}$. $\blacksquare$

*The velocity ellipse.* The Jacobian Lens maps the unit circle of joint velocities through $J$: an
ellipse with semi-axes the singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2$ of $J$ and area $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2
= \pi|\det J|$ — Yoshikawa's manipulability. At $\theta_2 = 10°$ (any $\theta_1$, any $L_1 = L_2 =
1$): $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \sin 10° = 0.1736$, $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 = \|J\|_F^2 = 3 + 2\cos 10° =
4.9696$, so $\sigma_1 = 2.228$, $\sigma_2 = 0.078$ and the ellipse is **28.6** times longer than it is
wide; at $\theta_2 = 0$ it is a segment. Exercise 4 asks for this number before the widget shows it.

*Example 3.8.2, the polygon.* A point fixed at $r = (r_1, r_2)$ on a body with configuration
$q = (q_1, q_2, q_3) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1$ sits at $x = (q_1, q_2) + R(q_3)\, r$, with Jacobian
$$
J(q) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -r_1 \sin q_3 - r_2 \cos q_3 \\ 0 & 1 & \;\;\, r_1 \cos q_3 - r_2 \sin q_3 \end{bmatrix},
$$
a $2 \times 3$ matrix of rank $2$ *always*: a rigid body in the plane has no singular configurations,
and $\varphi^{-1}$ is one-to-many because $\dim \Q > \dim M$. The third column is the lever arm
rotated by $90°$, the velocity of the point under pure rotation. `bodyPointJacobian` implements it and
the same check differences `bodyPointFk` against it.

*$J^{\mathsf T}$ is the force map.* Power is coordinate-free: $\tau \cdot \dot q = f \cdot \dot x =
f \cdot J\dot q$ for every $\dot q$, so $\tau = J^{\mathsf T} f$. The widget's $J^{\mathsf T}$ toggle
shows a unit tip force becoming joint torques; Chapter 7 lifts workspace potentials through this map
and Chapter 17 makes it dynamics.
</Derivation>

<Algorithm
  name="POLYGON-INTERSECTS (Choset App. F.2)"
  inputs="convex robot R (vertices r_i CCW, normals n^R_i), configuration q = (x, y, θ), convex obstacle W (vertices o_j, normals n^W_j)"
  outputs="true iff R(q) ∩ W ≠ ∅"
  complexity="O(n_R n_W) as written; O(n_R + n_W) with the normals merged as in STAR"
>
1. for each robot edge $E^R_i$ and obstacle vertex $o_j$: if $(o_{j-1} - o_j) \cdot n^R_i(\theta) \ge 0$ and $(o_{j+1} - o_j) \cdot n^R_i(\theta) \ge 0$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— applicable Type A (F.3)
2. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;if $n^R_i(\theta) \cdot (o_j - r_i(q)) > 0$ return **false** &nbsp;&nbsp;— one violated half-plane (F.4) separates the bodies
3. for each obstacle edge $E^W_j$ and robot vertex $r_i$: if $(r_{i-1} - r_i) \cdot n^W_j \ge 0$ and $(r_{i+1} - r_i) \cdot n^W_j \ge 0$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— applicable Type B (F.5)
4. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;if $n^W_j \cdot (r_i(q) - o_j) > 0$ return **false** &nbsp;&nbsp;— (F.6)
5. return **true** &nbsp;&nbsp;— every applicable inequality holds: $q \in \QO(\theta)$ (Derivation 3)
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="STAR (Choset App. F.3): QO(θ) = WO ⊖ R(0, 0, θ)"
  inputs="convex robot R with reference point at the origin, heading θ, convex obstacle W, both CCW"
  outputs="the vertices of QO(θ) counterclockwise, each tagged with the contact that produced it"
  complexity="O((n_R + n_W) log(n_R + n_W)); O(n_R + n_W) since each list is already sorted"
>
1. rotate $R$ by $\theta$; compute normals $n^R_i(\theta)$, $n^W_j$
2. $L_R \leftarrow$ the angles of $-n^R_i$; $L_W \leftarrow$ the angles of $n^W_j$; each cyclically sorted — rotate so each starts at its smallest angle
3. merge $L_R$ and $L_W$ by angle into one list of $n_R + n_W$ entries
4. $i \leftarrow$ the robot edge first in $L_R$, $j \leftarrow$ the obstacle edge first in $L_W$; emit $o_j - r_i$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— the pair extreme in $+x$
5. for each entry of the merged list, in order:
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;if it is robot edge $i$: $i \leftarrow i + 1$; emit $o_j - r_i$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— Type A: $E^R_i$ slid along $o_j$ (Derivation 4, Step 1)
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;else it is obstacle edge $j$: $j \leftarrow j + 1$; emit $o_j - r_i$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— Type B: $E^W_j$ slid along $r_i$
8. drop the closing duplicate and any collinear vertex; return the list &nbsp;&nbsp;— equals $\operatorname{hull}\{o_j - r_i\}$, the brute-force oracle
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="GJK (Choset Algorithm 23) on Z = A ⊖ B"
  inputs="convex polygons A, B through their support functions h, s; a tolerance"
  outputs="d(A, B) = min_{z ∈ Z} ‖z‖, the unique minimizer z*, and a witness pair (a*, b*)"
  complexity="each step O(n_A + n_B); at most n_A + n_B steps in exact arithmetic — 5 on the chapter's 500 random pairs"
>
1. $V_0 \leftarrow \{s_Z(d_0)\}$ for any direction $d_0$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— Choset seeds with three vertices of $Z$; one support point works equally and never needs $Z$ listed
2. $k \leftarrow 0$
3. $x_k \leftarrow \arg\min_{x \in \operatorname{hull}(V_k)} \|x\|$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— nearest point of a point, segment or triangle; if the origin is inside the triangle return $d = 0$
4. if $\|x_k\|^2 = h_Z(-x_k) \cdot (-1)$, i.e. $h_Z(-x_k) = -\|x_k\|^2$ (within tolerance): return $\|x_k\|$ with the witnesses from $x_k$'s barycentric weights
5. $z_k \leftarrow s_Z(-x_k) = s_A(-x_k) - s_B(x_k)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— (F.14); the point of $Z$ farthest toward the origin
6. $V_{k+1} \leftarrow$ the edge (or point) of $\operatorname{hull}(V_k)$ containing $x_k$, plus $z_k$
7. $k \leftarrow k + 1$; go to 3
</Algorithm>

Line 4 of GJK is the step worth a sentence. $h_Z(-x_k) = \max_z z \cdot (-x_k)$; if that maximum is
attained at $x_k$ itself the value is $-\|x_k\|^2$, and no point of $Z$ lies on the origin's side of
the supporting line through $x_k$ perpendicular to $x_k$. That line is a certificate — the same kind
of certificate as the single violated half-plane in POLYGON-INTERSECTS, and the same geometry: a
supporting half-plane of a convex set.

### Polygons and contacts

The polygon type enforces the one convention everything depends on — counterclockwise, convex — in
its constructor, and derives normals on demand so they can never go stale after a transform.

```rust title="crates/cspace/src/polygon.rs" {9-17,27-33}
use nalgebra::{Point2, Vector2};
use geom::Pose2;

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub struct NotConvexOrNotCcw;

/// A convex polygon as Choset's App. F.1 writes it: an intersection of negative
/// half-planes, stored as its counterclockwise vertex list. Edge i runs r_i → r_{i+1}.
#[derive(Debug, Clone, PartialEq)]
pub struct Polygon { verts: Vec<Point2<f64>> }

impl Polygon {
    /// The only constructor. Rejecting clockwise or nonconvex input here means no
    /// function below ever has to re-check the winding it relies on.
    pub fn new_ccw(verts: Vec<Point2<f64>>) -> Result<Self, NotConvexOrNotCcw> {
        if verts.len() < 3 || signed_area(&verts) <= 0.0 || !is_convex(&verts) {
            return Err(NotConvexOrNotCcw);
        }
        Ok(Polygon { verts })
    }
    pub fn len(&self) -> usize { self.verts.len() }
    pub fn vertex(&self, i: usize) -> Point2<f64> { self.verts[i % self.verts.len()] }

    /// Outward unit normal of edge i: the edge direction rotated clockwise by 90°.
    /// Derived, never stored — a stored normal is stale after the next `transformed`.
    pub fn edge_normal(&self, i: usize) -> Vector2<f64> {
        let e = self.vertex(i + 1) - self.vertex(i);
        Vector2::new(e.y, -e.x).normalize()
    }

    /// h_i(x, y) = n_i · ((x, y) − r_i): negative inside, by the F.1 convention.
    pub fn half_plane(&self, i: usize, p: &Point2<f64>) -> f64 {
        self.edge_normal(i).dot(&(p - self.vertex(i)))
    }

    /// r_i(x, y, θ) = R(θ) r_i + (x, y): the robot's vertices at configuration q.
    pub fn transformed(&self, pose: &Pose2) -> Polygon {
        Polygon { verts: self.verts.iter().map(|v| pose.act(v)).collect() }
    }
}
```

The contact test is written out so the reader sees the box's insides: applicability first (F.3, F.5),
then one half-plane value per applicable pair (F.4, F.6). It is $O(n_R n_W)$ and makes no attempt to
be fast; its job is to make `parry2d`'s answer a *verified* answer.

```rust title="crates/cspace/src/contact.rs" {12-24,38-46}
use super::polygon::Polygon;
use geom::Pose2;

/// The two ways convex bodies touch without overlapping (Choset F.2, figure F.4).
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum ContactKind {
    /// Robot edge E^R_i contains obstacle vertex o_j.
    TypeA { robot_edge: usize, obs_vertex: usize },
    /// Obstacle edge E^W_j contains robot vertex r_i.
    TypeB { obs_edge: usize, robot_vertex: usize },
}

/// Eqs. F.3 and F.5. Only θ enters: the robot's normals rotate with it, its position does not.
pub fn applicable_contacts(robot: &Polygon, theta: f64, obs: &Polygon) -> Vec<ContactKind> {
    let r = robot.transformed(&Pose2::new(0.0, 0.0, theta));
    let mut out = Vec::new();
    for i in 0..r.len() {
        let n = r.edge_normal(i);
        for j in 0..obs.len() {
            let (o, prev, next) = (obs.vertex(j), obs.vertex(j + obs.len() - 1), obs.vertex(j + 1));
            // Both obstacle edges leaving o_j point along or away from the robot normal:
            // −n^R_i lies between the normals of the edges adjacent to o_j.
            if (prev - o).dot(&n) >= 0.0 && (next - o).dot(&n) >= 0.0 {
                out.push(ContactKind::TypeA { robot_edge: i, obs_vertex: j });
            }
        }
    }
    for j in 0..obs.len() {
        let n = obs.edge_normal(j);
        for i in 0..r.len() {
            let (v, prev, next) = (r.vertex(i), r.vertex(i + r.len() - 1), r.vertex(i + 1));
            if (prev - v).dot(&n) >= 0.0 && (next - v).dot(&n) >= 0.0 {
                out.push(ContactKind::TypeB { obs_edge: j, robot_vertex: i });
            }
        }
    }
    out
}

/// f^R_ij(q) = n^R_i(θ) · (o_j − r_i(q))  or  f^W_ij(q) = n^W_j · (r_i(q) − o_j)   (F.4, F.6).
pub fn contact_value(c: ContactKind, robot: &Polygon, q: &Pose2, obs: &Polygon) -> f64 {
    let r = robot.transformed(q);
    match c {
        ContactKind::TypeA { robot_edge: i, obs_vertex: j } => r.edge_normal(i).dot(&(obs.vertex(j) - r.vertex(i))),
        ContactKind::TypeB { obs_edge: j, robot_vertex: i } => obs.edge_normal(j).dot(&(r.vertex(i) - obs.vertex(j))),
    }
}

/// App. F.2: q ∈ QO(θ) iff every applicable half-plane constraint holds. One violation
/// is a certificate of separation — the supporting line that keeps the bodies apart.
pub fn polygon_intersects(robot: &Polygon, q: &Pose2, obs: &Polygon) -> bool {
    applicable_contacts(robot, q.theta, obs)
        .into_iter()
        .all(|c| contact_value(c, robot, q, obs) <= 0.0)
}
```

### The star algorithm, and its oracle

This is the chapter's owned artifact — Chapters 8 and 10 import it and never reimplement it — so it
is written to be checked, not merely used. Every emitted vertex carries the contact that produced
it, and the brute-force hull sits beside it in the same file.

```rust title="crates/cspace/src/star.rs" {24-33,44-61}
use nalgebra::{Point2, Vector2};
use super::polygon::{convex_hull, Polygon};
use geom::Pose2;

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum Source { Robot, Obstacle }

/// One entry of the merged normal list: whose edge, which edge, at what angle in [0, 2π).
#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy)]
struct Normal { source: Source, edge: usize, angle: f64 }

fn angle_of(v: Vector2<f64>) -> f64 {
    let a = v.y.atan2(v.x);
    if a < 0.0 { a + std::f64::consts::TAU } else { a }
}

/// Cyclic rotation so a monotone-but-wrapped list starts at its smallest angle.
fn start_at_min(mut list: Vec<Normal>) -> Vec<Normal> {
    let k = (0..list.len()).min_by(|&a, &b| list[a].angle.total_cmp(&list[b].angle)).unwrap_or(0);
    list.rotate_left(k);
    list
}

/// QO(θ) = WO ⊖ R(0, 0, θ) by Choset's star algorithm (App. F.3).
///
/// WO ⊖ R = WO ⊕ (−R), and the boundary of a Minkowski sum of convex polygons is
/// made of both bodies' edges in order of outward normal. −R's normals are R's
/// negated, so: negate, merge by angle, walk the merged list. Consuming a robot
/// entry slides E^R_i along the current obstacle vertex (Type A) and advances i;
/// consuming an obstacle entry slides E^W_j along the current robot vertex (Type B)
/// and advances j. Both lists are already sorted, so this is one merge: O(n_R + n_W).
pub fn star_algorithm(robot: &Polygon, theta: f64, obs: &Polygon) -> Polygon {
    let r = robot.transformed(&Pose2::new(0.0, 0.0, theta));
    let rs = start_at_min((0..r.len()).map(|i| Normal { source: Source::Robot, edge: i, angle: angle_of(-r.edge_normal(i)) }).collect());
    let os = start_at_min((0..obs.len()).map(|j| Normal { source: Source::Obstacle, edge: j, angle: angle_of(obs.edge_normal(j)) }).collect());

    // The merge.
    let mut merged = Vec::with_capacity(rs.len() + os.len());
    let (mut a, mut b) = (0, 0);
    while a < rs.len() || b < os.len() {
        if b >= os.len() || (a < rs.len() && rs[a].angle <= os[b].angle) { merged.push(rs[a]); a += 1; }
        else { merged.push(os[b]); b += 1; }
    }

    // The scan. (i, j) start at the vertex pair extreme in +x; o_j − r_i is a vertex of QO(θ).
    let (mut i, mut j) = (rs[0].edge, os[0].edge);
    let diff = |j: usize, i: usize| Point2::from(obs.vertex(j) - r.vertex(i).coords);
    let mut verts = vec![diff(j, i)];
    for e in &merged {
        match e.source {
            // Type A: E^R_i slides along o_j, from o_j − r_i to o_j − r_{i+1}.
            Source::Robot => { i = (e.edge + 1) % r.len(); verts.push(diff(j, i)); }
            // Type B: E^W_j slides along r_i, from o_j − r_i to o_{j+1} − r_i.
            Source::Obstacle => { j = (e.edge + 1) % obs.len(); verts.push(diff(j, i)); }
        }
    }
    verts.pop(); // the scan closes on its start
    Polygon::new_ccw(remove_collinear(verts)).expect("the merge-scan of convex inputs is convex and CCW")
}

/// Eq. F.7 read literally: the convex hull of all n_R · n_W pairwise differences o_j − r_i.
/// O(n_R n_W log) — wasteful in a planner, exactly right as the oracle `star_algorithm` must equal.
pub fn minkowski_bruteforce(robot: &Polygon, theta: f64, obs: &Polygon) -> Polygon {
    let r = robot.transformed(&Pose2::new(0.0, 0.0, theta));
    let pts: Vec<Point2<f64>> = (0..obs.len()).flat_map(|j| (0..r.len()).map(move |i| (j, i)))
        .map(|(j, i)| Point2::from(obs.vertex(j) - r.vertex(i).coords)).collect();
    convex_hull(&pts)
}

/// §3.2.1: WO ⊕ B_r for a convex polygon — edges offset by r, vertices replaced by arcs.
pub fn inflate_disc(obs: &Polygon, r: f64, arc_segments: usize) -> Polygon { /* as in the TypeScript port */ }

/// App. F.4: QO ⊂ SE(2) as n_θ exact slices QO(θ_k). The stack is a picture; each slice is a theorem.
pub fn se2_slices(robot: &Polygon, obs: &Polygon, n_theta: usize) -> Vec<(f64, Polygon)> {
    (0..n_theta).map(|k| {
        let theta = -std::f64::consts::PI + std::f64::consts::TAU * k as f64 / n_theta as f64;
        (theta, star_algorithm(robot, theta, obs))
    }).collect()
}
```

### The rasterizer: any footprint, any chart

Choset's pixel method, generic over the configuration type. The rasterizer never learns what an arm
is; it asks a `Collision` checker one question per cell. `thicken` is his §3.2.2 footnote made a
parameter — zero for the micro-example's exact segments, the capsule radius for the Workbench.

```rust title="crates/cspace/src/raster.rs" {4-7,30-42}
use collide::{Collision, Footprint};

/// Any robot that can produce a footprint from a chart point. Reach<2> implements it
/// over T2; a disc over R2; nothing in this file knows which.
pub trait FootprintAt<Q> {
    fn footprint_at(&self, q: &Q) -> Footprint;
}

/// Cells centered on a uniform grid of [−π, π)²: with n = 360 the centers sit at integer
/// degrees, which is what makes "29 cells in the θ₁ = 0° row" a statement about this grid.
pub struct TorusGrid { pub n1: usize, pub n2: usize }

impl TorusGrid {
    pub fn point(&self, i: usize, j: usize) -> T2 {
        T2::new(-PI + i as f64 * TAU / self.n1 as f64, -PI + j as f64 * TAU / self.n2 as f64)
    }
}

/// One bit per cell: set iff the cell's configuration is in QO.
pub struct BitGrid { pub n1: usize, pub n2: usize, bits: Vec<u64> }

impl BitGrid {
    pub fn get(&self, i: usize, j: usize) -> bool { let k = i * self.n2 + j; self.bits[k / 64] >> (k % 64) & 1 == 1 }
    pub fn row_count(&self, i: usize) -> usize { (0..self.n2).filter(|&j| self.get(i, j)).count() }
    /// 4-connected components of the occupied (or free) cells with both seams identified:
    /// connectivity *measured* before Chapter 5 states what the torus is.
    pub fn components_torus(&self, occupied: bool) -> usize { /* flood fill, wrapping both axes */ }
}

/// Choset's pixel method. A free cell is a free *point*, not a free square: `thicken` > 0
/// inflates the footprint so a free cell certifies a free neighborhood. Conservative —
/// the planner is then resolution complete, never complete (his footnote 3, p. 47).
pub fn rasterize_cobstacle<Q>(
    robot: &dyn FootprintAt<Q>, scene: &dyn Collision, grid: &TorusGrid,
    chart: impl Fn(usize, usize) -> Q, thicken: f64,
) -> BitGrid {
    let mut out = BitGrid::new(grid.n1, grid.n2);
    for i in 0..grid.n1 {
        for j in 0..grid.n2 {
            let fp = robot.footprint_at(&chart(i, j)).thickened(thicken);
            if scene.intersects(&fp) { out.set(i, j); }
        }
    }
    out
}
```

### The Jacobian

```rust title="crates/cspace/src/jacobian.rs" {7-18}
use nalgebra::{Matrix2, Matrix2x3, Vector2};
use robots::{Reach, T2};

/// J(q) = ∂φ/∂q for the 2R arm (Choset Example 3.8.1). Column i is the tip velocity when
/// joint i alone turns at unit rate: every link beyond joint i swings about it, so the
/// column is Σ_{k ≥ i} L_k · (heading of link k rotated by 90°).
pub fn jacobian(reach: &Reach<2>, q: &T2) -> Matrix2<f64> {
    let [l1, l2] = reach.lengths;
    let (s1, c1) = q.th1().sin_cos();
    let (s12, c12) = (q.th1() + q.th2()).sin_cos();
    Matrix2::new(
        -l1 * s1 - l2 * s12, -l2 * s12,
         l1 * c1 + l2 * c12,  l2 * c12,
    )
}

/// det J = L₁ L₂ sin θ₂ — computed from the matrix; the identity is a test, not an assumption.
pub fn det_jacobian(reach: &Reach<2>, q: &T2) -> f64 { jacobian(reach, q).determinant() }

/// Singular iff J loses rank: for 2R, sin θ₂ = 0 — arm straight or folded.
pub fn is_singular(reach: &Reach<2>, q: &T2, tol: f64) -> bool { det_jacobian(reach, q).abs() <= tol }

/// Example 3.8.2: a point r on a planar rigid body. Rank 2 always — never singular.
pub fn body_point_jacobian(r: Vector2<f64>, q3: f64) -> Matrix2x3<f64> {
    let (s, c) = q3.sin_cos();
    Matrix2x3::new(1.0, 0.0, -r.x * s - r.y * c,
                   0.0, 1.0,  r.x * c - r.y * s)
}
```

## Putting it together: a Workbench whose free space is in two pieces

The integration lab asks one question of the full Workbench: is Reach's free space connected? Load the
*lab* preset of the C-Space Morph. It takes the Workbench of Chapter 2 — block, post, shelf — with the
block slid toward the base to $(0.75, 0.35)$ so that link 1 can reach it, and adds a **clamp**, a

Two things appear on the torus that the micro-example never showed. First, each near obstacle cuts a
**vertical band**: link 1 collides with the block for $\theta_1 \in [8°, 47°]$ and with the clamp for
$\theta_1 \in [118°, 152°]$, and a link-1 collision does not care about $\theta_2$, so those columns
are forbidden in full. Second, the two bands together do what one band cannot: a single band removes
an annulus from the torus and leaves a connected annulus behind, but two bands cut that annulus into
two strips. The readout confirms it — $\QO$ has $3$ components ($33.5\%$ of the torus), and
$\Qfree$ has **2**. Switch the lab obstacles off and $\Qfree$ is **1** again; the block's band alone,
the post's and the shelf's blobs, all merge into one free component.

The lab's query is $\qstart = (90°, 57°)$ and $\qgoal = (-90°, 57°)$, both free, both drawn, and
in different components. No planner in this book will connect them. Not Chapter 6's A\*, which is
complete and will say so after expanding every free cell; not Chapter 7's potential field, which
will sit in a local minimum against a band; not Chapter 11's PRM, whose roadmap will have two
components forever. The first thing a planner must respect is the connectivity of $\Qfree$, and this
page has now *measured* it, on a raster, before Chapter 5 says what connectivity means on a torus. The
check `lab` pins the band edges, the component counts with and without the clamp, the start and goal
labels, and the occupancy.

A last honesty item, because the lab makes it concrete. The Workbench as Chapter 2 ships it — block
at $[0.5, 1.0] \times [1.0, 1.5]$, post, shelf — keeps every obstacle beyond link 1's reach: its
nearest corner is $1.12$ m from the base and $L_1 + 0.03 = 1.03$. Its $\Qfree$ is **connected** (the
default *Workbench* preset shows one free component), and a design that promised a disconnected
Workbench would have been wrong by exactly the amount this chapter teaches: whether a C-space is in
one piece is not visible from the workspace picture. You have to compute it.

  <Exercise level="C" difficulty={2} title="How flat is the ellipse?">
    In the Jacobian Lens set $\theta_2 = 10°$ with $L_1 = L_2 = 1$. The velocity ellipse has
    semi-axes $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2$, the singular values of $J$. Predict their ratio from two facts
    you can compute by hand — $\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = |\det J| = \sin\theta_2$ and
    $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 = \|J\|_F^2 = L_1^2 + 2L_2^2 + 2L_1 L_2 \cos\theta_2$ — then read it off
    the widget. Then switch on $J^{\mathsf T}$ and find a configuration where a unit tip force needs
    the *largest* joint torque; explain why it is the same $q$ at which the ellipse is longest.
    <CheckAnswer
      id="ch04.e4"
      prompt="σ₁ / σ₂ at θ₂ = 10°, L₁ = L₂ = 1 (any θ₁), to one decimal"
      answer={28.6}
      tolerance={0.6}
    >
    With $p = \sigma_1\sigma_2 = \sin 10° = 0.1736$ and $s = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 = 3 + 2\cos 10°
    = 4.9696$, the two values solve $\sigma^4 - s\sigma^2 + p^2 = 0$: $\sigma_1 = 2.228$,
    $\sigma_2 = 0.078$, ratio $28.6$. The ratio does not depend on $\theta_1$ — rotating the whole arm
    rotates the ellipse without reshaping it — and the check `jacobian` asserts both facts. For the
    torque question: $\tau = J^{\mathsf T} f$ has the same singular values as $J$, so the largest
    torque for a unit force is $\sigma_1$, attained when $f$ points along the ellipse's long axis;
    $\sigma_1$ is largest when the arm is straight ($\theta_2 = 0$), where $\|J\|_F^2 = 5$ and
