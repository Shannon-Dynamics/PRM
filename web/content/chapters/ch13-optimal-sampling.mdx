Chapters [11](/chapters/ch11-prm) and [12](/chapters/ch12-tree-planners) bought feasibility with
randomness and proved the price: the failure probability decays exponentially in the number of
samples. They said nothing about the *quality* of the path, and anyone who has watched Tree Grower
knows why — the first path is a jagged accident of sample order.

This chapter asks the question Choset's 2005 text could not. Does more sampling make the path
better? For RRT the answer, proved by Karaman and Frazzoli in 2011, is *no, almost surely*: the tree
is anchored to its root through edges it never reconsiders. The repair is two local operations,
choose-parent and rewire, inside a neighborhood of radius $r_n = \gamma (\log n / n)^{1/d}$, and the
"aha" is *why that exact rate*. The ball must shrink so that the per-iteration cost stays
$O(\log n)$, but no faster than the scale at which a random geometric graph stays connected —
Chapter 11's tiling balls, now required to keep receiving samples forever.

From RRT\* and PRM\* the chapter builds the modern batch family — Informed RRT\* samples only where
improvement is possible, FMT\* and BIT\* search an implicit random geometric graph with a heuristic —
and ends with what "anytime" means for a robot that must move before the planner is done. Nothing in
this chapter is in Choset; its scaffolding, the ball tiling and the expansiveness constants, is. The
sister book's [Chapter 20](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch20-motion-planning)
states the central theorem in a paragraph and defers here.
</Overview>

## Building intuition: a tree allowed to change its mind

The ordinary RRT adds a node by attaching it to the nearest tree node, and that attachment is final.
RRT\* adds the same node — the Voronoi bias of Chapter 12 is untouched — and then does two things in
a ball around it. **Choose-parent:** among the tree nodes in the ball, attach to the one through which
the new node's cost-to-come is smallest, not the nearest. **Rewire:** for every other node in the
ball, if going through the new node would be cheaper than its current cost, make the new node its
parent and propagate the saving down its subtree.

Left and right consume the same seeded stream of random targets, so they have the same nodes; only
the edges differ. Watch the two cost curves. The left one is flat — it changes only on the rare
occasion that a *new* node happens to enter the goal region more cheaply than the old one, and it
never approaches the dashed lattice floor. The right one descends, and every drop is a flash of
rewired edges somewhere in the tree. The misconception this widget exists to kill is *"more samples
make RRT's path better."* They do not. They make RRT\*'s path better, and the whole chapter is about
what the asterisk costs.

Two more things to try. Slide $\gamma / \gamma^*$ below one. The right curve stalls — not
immediately, but it stops approaching the floor — because the ball now shrinks faster than samples
arrive to fill it, and the theorem's hypothesis has failed. Slide it to three and the descent is
faster per iteration and slower per second: every insertion now considers dozens of neighbors, and
the histogram of $\lvert Q_{near} \rvert$ tells you how many. The constant $\gamma^*$ is where
those two pressures balance, and it is a *computed* number, not a tuning knob.

<KeyIdea title="The one sentence version">
RRT's path is frozen by its parent pointers; RRT\* re-chooses the parent of every new node and of
every node near it inside a ball of radius $\gamma(\log n / n)^{1/d}$, which is the smallest ball
that keeps the random geometric graph connected along the optimal path and the largest that keeps
the per-iteration work logarithmic — and with it the best path's cost converges to the optimum
almost surely.
</KeyIdea>

### Definitions

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — asymptotic optimality, almost-sure suboptimality">
A planner is **asymptotically optimal** if, for every robustly feasible problem with a robustly
optimal solution,
$$
\Pr\Big[\lim_{n \to \infty} c_n^{\mathrm{ALG}} = c^*\Big] = 1 .
$$
It is **almost surely suboptimal** if $\Pr[\lim_n c_n^{\mathrm{ALG}} = c^*] = 0$: the limit exists
— $c_n$ is monotone — and is wrong with probability one. Robustness is the regularity the theorems
need: a path that can be perturbed by $\delta$ without leaving $\Qfree$, and an optimum that is the
limit of such paths' costs, so that a shrinking tube around it stays free.
</KeyIdea>

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — choose-parent, rewire, the informed set, anytime">
**Choose-parent.** Attach $q_{new}$ to the $v \in Q_{near}$ minimizing $\mathrm{Cost}(v) + c(v,
q_{new})$ subject to $\Dist(v, q_{new}) \ne$ NIL.

**Rewire.** For each $v \in Q_{near}$ with $\mathrm{Cost}(q_{new}) + c(q_{new}, v) <
\mathrm{Cost}(v)$ and $\Dist(q_{new}, v) \ne$ NIL, set $\mathrm{par}(v) := q_{new}$ and propagate
the decrease to $v$'s subtree.

**Informed set.** $X_{\hat f} = \{x : \hat g(x) + \hat h(x) \le c_{best}\}$. For `dist` cost in
$\mathbb{R}^d$ it is the prolate hyperspheroid with foci $\qstart, \qgoal$, transverse diameter
$c_{best}$ and conjugate diameter $\sqrt{c_{best}^2 - c_{min}^2}$.

**Anytime planner.** Returns a feasible solution early and improves it monotonically until
interrupted — "deadline in, best-so-far out".
</KeyIdea>

### RRT is almost surely suboptimal

<KeyIdea title="Theorem (Karaman and Frazzoli 2011)">
For $d \ge 2$, `dist` cost, and the paper's regularity assumptions — robust feasibility, a robustly
optimal path, finite $\mu(\Qfree)$ — the RRT's best cost converges to a suboptimal value:
$\Pr[\lim_n c_n^{\mathrm{RRT}} = c^*] = 0$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Why the tree's first children decide its fate" result="\Pr[\lim_n c_n^{\mathrm{RRT}} = c^*] = 0">
**Step 1 — every path passes through a child of the root.** Any tree path from $\qstart$ to the goal
leaves the root along one of the root's edges. The best path's cost is at least the best over the
root's children $u$ of $c(\qstart, u)$ plus the optimal cost from $u$ to the goal.

**Step 2 — the first children leave in the wrong directions.** With positive probability, the root's
first few children all leave at angles bounded away from the optimal path's initial direction — the
first few random targets simply landed elsewhere. Call the cone of good initial directions $C$.

**Step 3 — later good children are summably unlikely.** For the root to acquire a new child in $C$
at iteration $n$, a random target must fall in the root's Voronoi cell intersected with $C$. That
cell shrinks as the tree densifies around the root, so the probability of acquiring a good child at
iteration $n$ decays with $n$ fast enough that its sum over $n$ is finite.

**Step 4 — Borel–Cantelli.** A sequence of events whose probabilities sum to a finite value happens
only finitely often, almost surely. So the root almost surely acquires only finitely many good
children — and with positive probability, by Step 2, none at all.

**Step 5 — a fixed loss.** A path that leaves the root outside $C$ costs at least $c^* + \epsilon$
for an $\epsilon > 0$ depending on the cone, by the regularity of the optimum. With positive
probability the limit of $c_n$ is therefore at least $c^* + \epsilon$; the paper sharpens this to
probability one. $\blacksquare$

*Why shortcutting does not rescue it.* Chapter 11's greedy shortcutting replaces pieces of the path
by geodesics between its own points. It cannot change which side of an obstacle the path passes —
its homotopy class — and RRT's commitment through a bad first child is often exactly that. Exercise 6
asks for the two-class map where shortcutting can never win.
</Derivation>

### PRM\*, RRG and RRT\* are asymptotically optimal

<KeyIdea title="Theorem (Karaman and Frazzoli 2011)">
With the connection radius
$$
r_n = \gamma \Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{1/d},
\qquad
\gamma > \gamma^*_{2011} = 2 \Big(1 + \frac1d\Big)^{1/d} \Big(\frac{\mu(\Qfree)}{\zeta_d}\Big)^{1/d},
$$
or the $k$-nearest rule $k_n = k^* \log n$ with $k^* > e(1 + 1/d)$, PRM\* and RRT\* satisfy
$\Pr[\lim_n c_n = c^*] = 1$, and the expected per-iteration cost stays $O(\log n)$ because
$\mathbb{E} \lvert Q_{near} \rvert = \Theta(\log n)$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The shrinking tiling, and where the constant comes from" result="\sum_n \Pr[\text{some ball empty at } n] < \infty \iff c\gamma^d > 1 + 1/d">
**Step 1 — fix a robustly optimal path.** Let $\sigma^*$ have clearance $\delta$, so a tube of
radius $\delta$ around it is free.

**Step 2 — tile it with shrinking balls.** As in Theorem 7.4.1, cover $\sigma^*$ with balls — but of
radius proportional to $r_n$, say $r_n / 4$, with centers $r_n / 4$ apart, so that any two
consecutive balls lie inside a common ball of radius $r_n / 2 \le \delta$ once $n$ is large. The
number of balls is $M_n \approx 4L / r_n$, growing like $(n / \log n)^{1/d}$.

**Step 3 — one empty ball.** A ball of radius $r_n/4$ has measure $\zeta_d (r_n/4)^d = c\,\gamma^d
\log n / n$ for the constant $c = \zeta_d / (4^d \mu(\Qfree))$, measuring relative to $\Qfree$.
Being missed by all $n$ samples has probability
$$
\Big(1 - \frac{c\,\gamma^d \log n}{n}\Big)^n \;\le\; e^{-c\gamma^d \log n} \;=\; n^{-c\gamma^d} .
$$

**Step 4 — the union bound, and the constant.** The probability that *some* ball is empty at stage
$n$ is at most $M_n n^{-c\gamma^d} \approx 4L\, (n / \log n)^{1/d}\, n^{-c\gamma^d}$. Summing over
$n$ converges iff the exponent satisfies $c\gamma^d - 1/d > 1$, that is $c\gamma^d > 1 + 1/d$.
Solving for $\gamma$ gives a threshold of the form $(1 + 1/d)^{1/d} (\mu(\Qfree)/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$
times a geometric factor from the ball radii — the $2$ in $\gamma^*_{2011}$. *This step is where
$(1 + 1/d)$ enters*, and Exercise 1 asks for it with the constants carried.

**Step 5 — Borel–Cantelli.** Summable, so almost surely every ball is hit for all large $n$.

**Step 6 — the graph shadows the path.** Samples in consecutive balls are within $r_n$ of each
other and connected by a free segment (Step 2), so $G_n$ contains a path shadowing $\sigma^*$
whose cost tends to $c^*$ as the balls shrink. PRM\*'s Dijkstra finds something at least that good.

**Step 7 — RRT\* follows.** Choose-parent and rewire make the tree's cost-to-come at each sample
match the shortest path in $G_n$ along the shadow, in the limit — this is the step the 2020
correction is about. $\blacksquare$

*The other direction.* Why not take $\gamma$ huge and be done? Because $\mathbb{E}\lvert Q_{near}
\rvert = n \cdot \mu(B_{r_n}) / \mu(\Qfree) \propto \gamma^d \log n$: the per-iteration work is
logarithmic for *any* fixed $\gamma$, but its constant is $\gamma^d$, and the $\eta$ cap on edge
length means a large ball also wastes steer calls on neighbors the planner cannot reach in one step.
The rate $(\log n / n)^{1/d}$ is forced by Step 4; the constant is a budget.
</Derivation>

### The 2020 correction

<KeyIdea title="Theorem (Solovey, Janson, Schmerling, Frazzoli and Pavone 2020)">
The 2011 proof of RRT\*'s optimality has a gap at Step 7: rewiring reconsiders only the nodes inside
the *current* ball, so the tree need not reproduce the RRG's shortest path along the shadow. A
repaired argument establishes asymptotic optimality of RRT\* — with a *smaller* constant,
$$
\gamma > \gamma^*_{2020} = \big(2(1 + 1/d)\big)^{1/d} \Big(\frac{\mu(\Qfree)}{\zeta_d}\Big)^{1/d},
$$
and correspondingly for the $k$-nearest variant.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Repairing Step 7" result="\text{asymptotic optimality of RRT}^* \text{ with } \gamma^*_{2020} < \gamma^*_{2011}">
**Step 1 — locate the gap.** In Step 7 above, the shadow path exists in $G_n$, but RRT\* is a tree:
a node's parent is chosen among the nodes present *when it was inserted*, and rewiring later touches
only nodes within $r_n$ of each new node. Nothing guarantees that the tree's chain of parents ever
aligns with the graph's shortest path.

**Step 2 — track a chain in the tree directly.** Instead of comparing with the graph, follow a
sequence of tree nodes, one per tiling ball along $\sigma^*$, and bound the tree path's cost through
them.

**Step 3 — samples must arrive in a usable order.** For the chain to form, each ball must contain a
sample *and* the samples must arrive so that when the sample in ball $i + 1$ is inserted, a node in
ball $i$ is already present and within $r_n$ — then choose-parent can pick it, or a later rewire can
fix it. That is a per-ball probability, smaller than mere occupancy.

**Step 4 — close the union bound.** The modified events are still summable for $\gamma$ above a
threshold, and the threshold that comes out is $\gamma^*_{2020}$ — smaller than the 2011 constant,
because the repaired argument needs one sample per ball at the right time rather than a geometric
factor of slack. $\blacksquare$

The implementation names both constants, defaults to the corrected one, and the widget lets you pick
either. The chapter says plainly: the 2011 proof had a gap that took nine years to notice, and the
result survived.
</Derivation>

### The $(\epsilon, \alpha, \beta)$ lens, and the $k$-nearest variant

Two remarks tie the theorem back to Chapter 11. First, Step 6 needs consecutive balls to *see*
each other through the local planner, which is $\epsilon$-goodness in miniature: the shadowing
balls are mutually visible because the tube around $\sigma^*$ is free. A space that is not
$\epsilon$-good — a corridor thinner than every $r_n$ for the $n$ you can afford — defeats the
asymptotic argument in practice exactly as it defeated Theorem 7.4.2's constants. Second, the
$k$-nearest rule is the same theorem in a different coordinate. In a region of density $\rho$ the
$k_n$ nearest neighbors occupy a ball of radius $(k_n / \rho \zeta_d)^{1/d} \propto (\log n /
n)^{1/d}$ — the radius rule again, with the density made implicit — and the constant $k^* = e(1 +
1/d)$ comes out of the same union bound with a Chernoff bound on a Poisson count in place of the
emptiness probability. The $k$-nearest rule adapts to local density, which is why some
implementations prefer it; the radius rule is the one whose constant is tied to $\mu(\Qfree)$ and
therefore to the world. The check `k-nearest RRT*` runs both on seed 13 and finds their costs
within one percent of each other.

### The constants, for $n = 1000$ in the unit square

$(\log n / n)^{1/2} = (6.9078 / 1000)^{1/2} = 0.08311$. With $d = 2$, $\mu(\Qfree) = 1$,
$\zeta_2 = \pi$:

| constant | value | $r_{1000}$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\gamma^*_{2011} = 2 \cdot 1.5^{1/2} \pi^{-1/2}$ | $1.3820$ | $0.1149$ |
| $\gamma^*_{2020} = 3^{1/2} \pi^{-1/2}$ | $0.9772$ | $0.0812$ |
| $k^* = e(1 + 1/2)$ | $4.077$ | $k_{1000} = \lceil 4.077 \cdot 6.9078 \rceil = 29$ |

A ball of radius $0.08$ in a unit square after a thousand samples: about twenty neighbors to
consider per insertion, each a steer call. That is the price of the asterisk, and the check
`r_1000 in the unit square` pins every number in the table.

### Informed sampling is exactly where improvement is possible

<KeyIdea title="Theorem (Gammell, Srinivasa and Barfoot 2014)">
With `dist` cost and admissible $\hat g, \hat h$, no configuration outside $X_{\hat f}$ lies on a
path cheaper than $c_{best}$. Sampling uniformly in $X_{\hat f}$ preserves RRT\*'s guarantees and
multiplies the probability of a useful sample by $\mu(\Qfree) / \mu(X_{\hat f})$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The ellipse, and how to sample it" result="X_{\hat f} = \text{ellipse with foci } \qstart, \qgoal,\ \text{string length } c_{best}">
**Step 1 — triangle inequality.** Any path through $x$ costs at least $\mathrm{dist}(\qstart, x) +
\mathrm{dist}(x, \qgoal) = \hat g(x) + \hat h(x)$.

**Step 2 — outside is useless.** If $\hat g(x) + \hat h(x) > c_{best}$, no path through $x$ can beat
the incumbent. The set of useful points is $\{x : \hat g(x) + \hat h(x) \le c_{best}\}$ — the set of
points whose string from one focus to the other has length at most $c_{best}$: an ellipse in the
plane, a prolate hyperspheroid in $\mathbb{R}^d$, with transverse semi-axis $c_{best}/2$ and
conjugate semi-axes $\sqrt{c_{best}^2 - c_{min}^2} / 2$.

**Step 3 — sample it directly.** Draw a uniform point of the unit ball, scale by
$\mathrm{diag}(c_{best}/2, \sqrt{c_{best}^2 - c_{min}^2}/2, \dots)$, rotate so the first axis
points along $(\qgoal - \qstart) / c_{min}$ — a Householder reflection does it in one line — and
translate to the midpoint. Uniform in the ball maps to uniform in the ellipsoid because the map is
affine.

**Step 4 — reject what leaves $\Q$.** Early on the ellipse spills far outside the room, and the draws
that leave the chart's box are rejected. On $T^2$ the formula is wrong once $c_{best}$ exceeds the
torus circumference (Exercise 2); the implementation falls back to rejection against the manifold
metric there, exact and slower. $\blacksquare$

*Necessary, not sufficient.* Every improving path lies in the ellipse; most of the ellipse lies in no
improving path, because obstacles cut it. The readout in the widget is $\mu(X_{\hat f} \cap \Q) /
\mu(\Q)$ — the part of the room worth sampling at all — and the check `informed set measure` pins
it against a Monte Carlo count and the unclipped $\pi a b$.
</Derivation>

### FMT\* and BIT\*: search the graph you never build

<KeyIdea title="Theorem (Janson, Schmerling, Clark and Pavone 2015)">
On one batch of $n$ samples with $r_n = \gamma(\log n/n)^{1/d}$ and $\gamma > 2 (1/d)^{1/d}
(\mu(\Qfree)/\zeta_d)^{1/d}$, growing the tree outward in cost-to-come order and connecting each
frontier node to the locally optimal parent *chosen ignoring obstacles, then checking only that
edge* is asymptotically optimal with $O(n \log n)$ expected collision checks.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Lazy dynamic programming is wrong only where it does not matter" result="\Pr[\lim c_n^{\mathrm{FMT}^*} = c^*] = 1 \text{ with } O(n\log n) \text{ checks}">
**Step 1 — the lazy choice.** FMT\* expands the open node $z$ of least cost-to-come and, for each
unvisited $x$ within $r_n$, picks the parent $y$ minimizing $\mathrm{Cost}(y) + c(y, x)$ over the
open nodes in $x$'s ball *without* collision checking, then checks the one edge $(y, x)$. If it is
blocked, $x$ is left for later — it is not given a second-best parent.

**Step 2 — when the lazy choice is wrong.** The choice is wrong only when the optimal in-ball parent
is blocked: $x$ gets no parent now and may get a worse one later. Inside a $\delta$-clear corridor
the ball of radius $r_n$ is eventually inside the corridor, and that event stops happening.

**Step 3 — bounded error.** Each lazily chosen edge is within $O(r_n)$ of the local optimum, there
are $O(1 / r_n)$ edges along the shadow path, and the total excess stays bounded and vanishes along a
subsequence.

**Step 4 — the bill.** One nearest-neighbor structure, $O(\log n)$ expected neighbors per node, one
collision check per *accepted* edge: $O(n \log n)$ in all, and no rewiring. $\blacksquare$

*BIT\*.* Gammell, Srinivasa and Barfoot's Batch Informed Trees runs the same idea in batches. Each
batch adds informed samples to an implicit random geometric graph of radius $r_n$ and searches it
exactly like [Chapter 6](/chapters/ch06-graph-search)'s A\*: an edge queue keyed $\hat g(v) + \hat
c(v, x) + \hat h(x)$, a vertex queue keyed $\hat g(v) + \hat h(v)$, collision checks only when an
edge is popped. After a solution, samples and vertices that cannot improve it are pruned, and the
tree is kept across batches so the next batch repairs rather than rebuilds — an LPA\*-style reuse.
</Derivation>

## The algorithm

<Algorithm
  name="RRT* — one iteration: sample, extend, choose-parent, rewire"
  inputs="the cost tree T, the sampler, η, the neighborhood rule (r_n or k_n), the local planner Δ"
  outputs="T with q_new inserted at its cheapest reachable parent and its neighbors rewired; a new incumbent if a goal-connected node got cheaper"
  complexity="|Q_near| = O(log n) steer calls per iteration, O(n log n) total; cost propagation O(|subtree|) per rewire"
>
1. $q_{rand} \leftarrow$ SAMPLE; $q_{near} \leftarrow$ NEAREST($T$, $q_{rand}$); $q_{new} \leftarrow$ EXTEND($q_{near}$, $q_{rand}$, $\eta$) — `Trapped` ends the iteration
2. $Q_{near} \leftarrow \{\, v \in T : \mathrm{dist}(v, q_{new}) \le \min(r_n, \eta) \,\}$, or the $k_n$ nearest; always including $q_{near}$
3. **choose-parent:** $v^* \leftarrow \arg\min_{v \in Q_{near}} \mathrm{Cost}(v) + c(v, q_{new})$ subject to $\Dist(v, q_{new}) \ne$ NIL — try candidates in order of that sum and stop at the first free one
4. add $q_{new}$ with parent $v^*$ and $\mathrm{Cost}(q_{new}) = \mathrm{Cost}(v^*) + c(v^*, q_{new})$
5. **rewire: for all** $v \in Q_{near} \setminus \{v^*\}$ **do**
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $\mathrm{Cost}(q_{new}) + c(q_{new}, v) < \mathrm{Cost}(v)$ **and** $\Dist(q_{new}, v) \ne$ NIL **then** $\mathrm{par}(v) \leftarrow q_{new}$; propagate the decrease through $v$'s subtree
7. **goal test:** if $\mathrm{dist}(q_{new}, \qgoal) \le \eta$ and $\Dist(q_{new}, \qgoal) \ne$ NIL, record $q_{new}$ as goal-connected
8. incumbent $\leftarrow \min$ over goal-connected $v$ of $\mathrm{Cost}(v) + c(v, \qgoal)$ — a rewire deep in the tree lowers it without touching the goal
</Algorithm>

Line 3 is sorted so that the first free candidate is the answer; line 2's $\eta$ cap is what keeps
the steer calls short. With `rewire = false` and $Q_{near} = \{q_{near}\}$ the same code is Chapter
12's RRT, which is how the widgets feed one seed to both.

<Algorithm
  name="PRM* — the radius rule for a roadmap"
  inputs="n, γ (or k*), the sampler, Δ"
  outputs="a roadmap with O(n log n) expected edges whose shortest paths converge to the optimum"
  complexity="O(n log n) expected edges, each one steer call"
>
1. **for** $i = 1 \dots n$ **do** $q_i \leftarrow$ SAMPLE; $r \leftarrow \gamma (\log i / i)^{1/d}$ (or $k \leftarrow \lceil k^* \log i \rceil$)
2. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**for all** $q_j$ within $r$ of $q_i$ (or its $k$ nearest) **do if** $\Dist(q_i, q_j) \ne$ NIL **then** add the edge with cost $c(q_i, q_j)$
3. query by Chapter 11's Algorithm 7
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="FMT* — lazy dynamic programming on one batch"
  inputs="n samples including q_start, q_goal; r_n; Δ"
  outputs="a tree over the samples and the path to q_goal, or failure if the open set empties first"
  complexity="O(n log n) expected: one kd-tree, O(log n) neighbors per node, one check per accepted edge"
>
1. $\mathrm{Cost}(\qstart) \leftarrow 0$; OPEN $\leftarrow \{\qstart\}$; all other samples UNVISITED
2. **while** OPEN is not empty **do**
3. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;$z \leftarrow$ the OPEN node of least $\mathrm{Cost}$; **if** $z = \qgoal$ **then return** the path
4. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**for all** UNVISITED $x$ within $r_n$ of $z$ **do**
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;$y \leftarrow \arg\min$ over OPEN nodes within $r_n$ of $x$ of $\mathrm{Cost}(y) + c(y, x)$ — obstacles ignored
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $\Dist(y, x) \ne$ NIL **then** $\mathrm{par}(x) \leftarrow y$; $\mathrm{Cost}(x) \leftarrow \mathrm{Cost}(y) + c(y, x)$; move $x$ to OPEN
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;move $z$ to CLOSED
8. **return** failure
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="BIT* — one batch, searched like A*"
  inputs="the current tree, the unconnected samples, a batch size, γ, the incumbent c_best"
  outputs="an improved incumbent, or an exhausted batch"
  complexity="per batch: an implicit RGG of radius r_n over tree vertices and samples, searched by an edge queue keyed ĝ(v) + ĉ(v, x) + ĥ(x) with collision checks only on pop"
>
1. **prune:** drop every sample and vertex $x$ with $\hat g(x) + \hat h(x) \ge c_{best}$; detach children of dropped vertices and return them to the sample set if they could still help
2. **sample:** draw `batch` new configurations from the informed set; $r_n \leftarrow \gamma (\log n / n)^{1/d}$ over vertices plus samples
3. **re-key:** every tree vertex $v$ enters the vertex queue keyed $g(v) + \hat h(v)$
4. **repeat** while a queue is non-empty:
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;while the best vertex key $\le$ the best edge key, pop a vertex and push its edges $(v, x)$ to samples (and to vertices it could rewire) within $r_n$, keyed $\hat g(v) + \hat c(v, x) + \hat h(x)$
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;pop the best edge $(v, x)$; **if** $g(v) + \hat c(v, x) + \hat h(x) \ge c_{best}$ **then** the batch is exhausted — clear both queues
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** $g(v) + c(v, x) < g(x)$ **and** $\Dist(v, x) \ne$ NIL **then** attach or rewire $x$ under $v$, propagate, and push $x$ to the vertex queue; update $c_{best}$ if $\qgoal$ got cheaper
</Algorithm>

### The five-node rewire, by hand

Euclidean plane, no obstacles, a tree of four nodes before one RRT\* iteration: the root $A = (0, 0)$
with $\mathrm{Cost} = 0$; $B = (3, 0)$ under $A$, $\mathrm{Cost} = 3$; $C = (3, 3)$ under $B$,
$\mathrm{Cost} = 6$; $D = (0, 3)$ under $A$, $\mathrm{Cost} = 3$. The new sample is $E = (1.5, 1.5)$
and the neighborhood radius for this step is $r = 2.5$.

**Near set.** $\mathrm{dist}(E, \cdot) = 1.5\sqrt2 = 2.1213$ to each of $A, B, C, D$: all four are
in $Q_{near}$.

**Choose-parent.** Via $A$: $0 + 2.1213 = 2.1213$. Via $B$ or $D$: $3 + 2.1213 = 5.1213$. Via $C$:
$6 + 2.1213 = 8.1213$. Parent $A$; $\mathrm{Cost}(E) = 2.1213$.

**Rewire.** The cost through $E$ to any neighbor is $2.1213 + 2.1213 = 4.2426 = 3\sqrt2$. $B$:
$4.2426 > 3$, keep. $D$: keep. $C$: $4.2426 < 6$ — **rewire** $\mathrm{par}(C) := E$ and
$\mathrm{Cost}(C)$ drops from $6$ to $4.2426$, a $29.3$ percent saving; $C$ has no children, so the
propagation is a no-op. Plain RRT would have attached $E$ under its nearest node — a tie here,
broken toward $A$ — and left $C$ at $6$ forever.

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/radius.rs"
/// r_n = γ (log n / n)^{1/d}. γ is a parameter, not hidden: the widget must be able to break it.
pub fn r_n(n: usize, d: usize, gamma: f64) -> f64 {
    gamma * ((n as f64).ln() / n as f64).powf(1.0 / d as f64)
}
/// γ* = 2 (1 + 1/d)^{1/d} (μ_free / ζ_d)^{1/d} — Karaman & Frazzoli 2011, Theorem 38.
pub fn gamma_star_kf2011(d: usize, mu_free: f64) -> f64 {
    2.0 * (1.0 + 1.0 / d as f64).powf(1.0 / d as f64) * (mu_free / unit_ball_volume(d)).powf(1.0 / d as f64)
}
/// γ* = (2 (1 + 1/d))^{1/d} (μ_free / ζ_d)^{1/d} — Solovey, Janson, Schmerling, Frazzoli & Pavone 2020.
pub fn gamma_star_solovey2020(d: usize, mu_free: f64) -> f64 {
    (2.0 * (1.0 + 1.0 / d as f64)).powf(1.0 / d as f64) * (mu_free / unit_ball_volume(d)).powf(1.0 / d as f64)
}
/// k_n = ⌈e (1 + 1/d) log n⌉.
pub fn k_n(n: usize, d: usize) -> usize { (std::f64::consts::E * (1.0 + 1.0 / d as f64) * (n as f64).ln()).ceil() as usize }

pub enum Neighborhood { Radius { gamma: f64, eta: f64 }, KNearest { k_star: f64 } }
```

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/rrt_star.rs" {24-40}
/// Ch. 12's tree plus cost-to-come and child lists. Wrapping, not forking: `inner` is the
/// same arena the Ch. 12 widgets draw, so RRT and RRT* can be fed identical samples.
pub struct CostTree<M: Chart> { pub inner: Tree<M>, pub cost: Vec<f64>, children: Vec<SmallVec<[NodeId; 4]>> }

impl<M: Chart> CostTree<M> {
    pub fn near(&self, q: &M::Point, r: f64) -> Vec<(NodeId, f64)> { self.inner.nn.within(q, r) }   // Ch. 11 KdTree::within
    pub fn add(&mut self, q: M::Point, parent: NodeId, edge: f64) -> NodeId {
        let id = self.inner.add(q, parent);
        self.cost.push(self.cost[parent] + edge);
        self.children.push(SmallVec::new());
        self.children[parent].push(id);
        id
    }
    /// Change v's parent and push the cost change through its subtree (iterative DFS).
    pub fn reparent(&mut self, v: NodeId, new_parent: NodeId, edge: f64) -> usize {
        let old = self.inner.nodes[v].parent.unwrap();
        self.children[old].retain(|&c| c != v);
        self.inner.nodes[v].parent = Some(new_parent);
        self.children[new_parent].push(v);
        let delta = self.cost[new_parent] + edge - self.cost[v];
        let (mut stack, mut touched) = (vec![v], 0);
        while let Some(u) = stack.pop() { self.cost[u] += delta; touched += 1; stack.extend(self.children[u].iter().copied()); }
        touched - 1
    }
}

impl<M: Chart, S: Extend<M>, Smp: Sampler<M>> RrtStar<M, S, Smp> {
    /// (parent, edge cost) minimizing Cost(v) + c(v, q_new) over `near` with Δ succeeding.
    /// Sorted by that sum, so the first candidate whose segment is free is the answer.
    fn choose_parent(&self, free: &dyn FreeSpace<M>, q_new: &M::Point, near: &[(NodeId, f64)]) -> Option<(NodeId, f64)> {
        let mut cands: Vec<_> = near.iter().map(|&(v, d)| (self.tree.cost[v] + d, v, d)).collect();
        cands.sort_by(|a, b| a.0.partial_cmp(&b.0).unwrap());
        cands.into_iter().find(|&(_, v, _)| self.steer.steer(&self.space, free, &self.tree.q(v), q_new).is_some())
             .map(|(_, v, d)| (v, d))
    }

    /// For each v in `near`: if Cost(new) + c(new, v) < Cost(v) and Δ(new, v), reparent.
    fn rewire(&mut self, free: &dyn FreeSpace<M>, new: NodeId, near: &[(NodeId, f64)]) -> usize {
        let c_new = self.tree.cost[new];
        let mut count = 0;
        for &(v, d) in near {
            if v == self.tree.parent(new) { continue; }
            if c_new + d < self.tree.cost[v] && self.steer.steer(&self.space, free, &self.tree.q(new), &self.tree.q(v)).is_some() {
                self.propagated += self.tree.reparent(v, new, d);
                count += 1;
            }
        }
        count
    }

    /// One iteration: sample → nearest → extend → near set → choose_parent → rewire → goal test.
    pub fn iterate(&mut self, free: &dyn FreeSpace<M>) -> Option<f64> {
        let q_rand = self.sample(free)?;
        let (near_id, _) = self.tree.inner.nearest(&q_rand);
        let q_new = match self.steer.extend(&self.space, free, &self.tree.q(near_id), &q_rand, self.eta) {
            ExtendStatus::Trapped => return None, ExtendStatus::Reached(q) | ExtendStatus::Advanced(q) => q };
        let n = self.tree.size() + 1;
        let mut near = match self.nbhd {
            Neighborhood::Radius { gamma, eta } => self.tree.near(&q_new, r_n(n, M::D, gamma).min(eta)),
            Neighborhood::KNearest { k_star } => self.tree.inner.nn.k_nearest(&q_new, k_n(n, M::D)),
        };
        if !near.iter().any(|&(v, _)| v == near_id) { near.push((near_id, self.space.dist(&self.tree.q(near_id), &q_new))); }
        // The nearest node's segment is already known free: it is always a valid parent.
        let (parent, edge) = self.choose_parent(free, &q_new, &near).unwrap_or((near_id, self.space.dist(&self.tree.q(near_id), &q_new)));
        let id = self.tree.add(q_new.clone(), parent, edge);
        self.rewires += self.rewire(free, id, &near);
        if self.space.dist(&q_new, &self.goal) <= self.goal_radius && self.steer.steer(&self.space, free, &q_new, &self.goal).is_some() { self.goal_nodes.push(id); }
        self.refresh_best()                                 // Some(cost) only if the incumbent dropped
    }
}
```

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/anytime.rs"
pub struct Solution<P> { pub path: Vec<P>, pub cost: f64 }

/// "Deadline in, best-so-far out." One unit of work per `step`, `Some(cost)` when the incumbent
/// improved; `best()` is always available. Chapter 23's planning stack consumes exactly this.
pub trait Anytime<P> {
    fn step(&mut self) -> Option<f64>;
    fn best(&self) -> Option<Solution<P>>;
    fn run_until(&mut self, deadline: std::time::Instant) -> Option<Solution<P>> {
        while std::time::Instant::now() < deadline { self.step(); }
        self.best()
    }
```

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/fmt.rs"
impl<M: Chart, S: Steer<M>> Anytime<M::Point> for Fmt<M, S> {
    /// Expand one frontier node — lazy dynamic programming, one collision check per accepted edge.
    fn step(&mut self) -> Option<f64> {
        let z = self.open.pop()?;                                    // least cost-to-come
        for (x, _) in self.nn.within(&self.samples[z], self.r) {
            if self.state[x] != Unvisited { continue; }
            // Locally optimal parent among OPEN nodes in x's ball, obstacles ignored …
            let Some((y, c)) = self.nn.within(&self.samples[x], self.r).into_iter()
                .filter(|&(y, _)| self.state[y] == Open)
                .map(|(y, d)| (y, self.cost[y] + d))
                .min_by(|a, b| a.1.partial_cmp(&b.1).unwrap()) else { continue };
            // … then check only that edge. Wrong exactly when the best parent is blocked.
            if self.steer.steer(&self.space, &*self.free, &self.samples[y], &self.samples[x]).is_some() {
                self.cost[x] = c; self.parent[x] = Some(y); self.state[x] = Open; self.open.push(x, c);
            }
        }
        self.state[z] = Closed;
        (z == self.goal_idx).then(|| { self.done = true; self.cost[z] })
    }
    fn best(&self) -> Option<Solution<M::Point>> { self.done.then(|| self.path_to(self.goal_idx)) }
}
```

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/bit.rs"
impl<M: Chart, S: Steer<M>> Anytime<M::Point> for Bit<M, S> {
    /// One unit of work: pop the best edge (expanding vertices while a vertex could beat it), or
    /// open a new batch when both queues are empty. Keys are Ch. 6's f = g + h on the implicit RGG.
    fn step(&mut self) -> Option<f64> {
        if self.q_v.is_empty() && self.q_e.is_empty() { self.new_batch(); return None; }   // prune, sample, re-key
        while self.q_v.peek_key() <= self.q_e.peek_key() { let v = self.q_v.pop().unwrap(); self.expand(v); }
        let (v, x) = self.q_e.pop()?;
        let c = self.space.dist(&self.pts[v], &self.pts[x]);
        if self.g[v] + c + self.h_hat(x) >= self.c_best { self.q_v.clear(); self.q_e.clear(); return None; }
        if self.g[v] + c >= self.g[x] { return None; }
        self.edge_checks += 1;
        self.steer.steer(&self.space, &*self.free, &self.pts[v], &self.pts[x])?;    // lazy: checked on pop
        self.attach(x, v, c);                                                       // or rewire + propagate
        (self.in_tree[GOAL] && self.g[GOAL] < self.c_best).then(|| { self.c_best = self.g[GOAL]; self.c_best })
    }
    fn best(&self) -> Option<Solution<M::Point>> { self.c_best.is_finite().then(|| self.path_to(GOAL)) }
}
```

```rust title="crates/sampling/src/optimal/informed.rs"
pub struct InformedSampler<M: Chart> {
    start: M::Point, goal: M::Point, c_min: f64, pub c_best: f64,
    center: SVector<f64, { M::D }>, axis: SVector<f64, { M::D }>, fallback: Uniform,
}

impl<M: Chart> Sampler<M> for InformedSampler<M> {
    fn sample(&mut self, space: &M, free: &dyn FreeSpace<M>, rng: &mut SmallRng) -> Option<M::Point> {
        if !self.c_best.is_finite() { return self.fallback.sample(space, free, rng); }   // no incumbent yet
        let a = self.c_best / 2.0;
        let b = (self.c_best.powi(2) - self.c_min.powi(2)).max(0.0).sqrt() / 2.0;
        // Unit ball → scale → rotate (Householder: e₁ ↦ axis) → translate → reject outside 𝒬.
        let u = unit_ball(rng);
        let s = SVector::from_fn(|i, _| if i == 0 { a * u[i] } else { b * u[i] });
        let v = (SVector::ith(0, 1.0) - self.axis).normalize();
        let r = s - 2.0 * v.dot(&s) * v;
        let c = r + self.center;
        let q = space.from_coords(&c)?;                       // None if outside the chart's box
        free.is_free(&q).then_some(q)
    }
}

impl<M: Chart> InformedSampler<M> {
    pub fn update(&mut self, c_best: f64) { self.c_best = c_best; }
    /// μ(X_f̂ ∩ 𝒬)/μ(𝒬) — w13.2's readout; in the plane the ellipse is clipped against the box by quadrature.
    pub fn measure_ratio(&self, space: &M) -> f64 { ellipse_box_area(self, space.bounds()) / space.measure() }
}
```

## Putting it together: the anytime contract

The lab is the Batch Bench. Four asymptotically optimal planners, one seed, one world, and one
deadline each: stop at the deadline, execute what you have, keep planning, and switch to the better
path when it arrives. That loop is the one [Chapter 23](/chapters/ch23-capstone)'s planning stack
runs for Reach, and it is what `trait Anytime` is for — one unit of work per `step`, `Some(cost)`
when the incumbent improved, `best()` always available.

<Deferred minHeight={820}>
  <BatchBench />
</Deferred>

Read the curves at three deadlines. At a hundred milliseconds, RRT\* and Informed RRT\* have usually
found a first path and begun to improve it; BIT\* has processed its first batch and often holds the
best path of the four; FMT\* has nothing — it is still drawing its batch or marching through it.
At half a second the curves have crossed at least once. At two seconds FMT\* has returned its single
answer, often the best, and will never improve it; the incremental planners are still descending.
The misconception the lab kills is *"asymptotically optimal means better at every budget."* The
theorems are all about $n \to \infty$; at a deadline the winner is decided by constants, and the
constants depend on the world.

Three honesty items the chapter keeps. Every theorem here is asymptotic — at finite $n$ the cost is
a random variable, and the widgets show its spread across seeds. The hypotheses are necessary:
robust optimality, `dist` as the cost, and the $\gamma$ bound, which Rewire Watch lets you violate.
And nothing in this chapter makes Hitch drivable: the straight-line steer is still wrong for a car,
and the cost it optimizes is the length of a path the car cannot follow. Kinodynamic RRT\* replaces
the steer with a two-point boundary-value solver in [Chapter 21](/chapters/ch21-nonholonomic-ii).

One last contrast before Part III closes. Asymptotic optimality is *global* and *slow*: the planner
converges to the best path in any homotopy class, eventually. [Chapter 19](/chapters/ch19-trajectory-optimization)'s
trajectory optimization is the complementary route — *local* and *fast*, descending a cost from an
initial guess and never leaving its homotopy class. The two meet in practice as a pipeline: an RRT\*
path to pick the class, an optimizer to make it smooth.
