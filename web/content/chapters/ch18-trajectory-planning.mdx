Every planner in Parts II and III handed Reach a curve and walked away. The curve had no clock. This
chapter attaches one, and the first thing it discovers is that the best clock is almost never
uniform. Run a path at constant speed and some motor pegs where the configuration is heavy and
idles where it is light; run it on the clock this chapter computes and exactly one motor is at its
limit at every instant, in turn.

The idea that makes this tractable fits in one sentence. **Once the path is fixed, an $n$-joint
arm with $n$ torque limits is a one-dimensional system in the path parameter $s$**, and its
time-optimal execution is a curve in the $(s, \dot s)$ phase plane that hugs a velocity-limit
curve and switches between maximum and minimum acceleration a finite number of times. A reader who
can read that phase plane can read every time-scaling result since 1985, including the 2018
reformulation that replaced switch-point hunting with interval propagation.

The second half of the chapter asks what happens when the path is *not* fixed, and meets the honest
ancestor of every state-lattice planner: Choset's grid search over $(q, \dot q)$, run here on
Chapter 6's A\*. It is time-optimal for its own discretization, complete in a precise
$\epsilon$-sense, and exponential in the number of joints — which is exactly why
[Chapter 19](/chapters/ch19-trajectory-optimization) lets the path move by optimization instead.
</Overview>

## The problem: one path, two clocks

Take the spline below — three waypoints on Reach's torus, the arm hanging below its shoulder on a
vertical Workbench — and execute it twice in the same $0.62$ s. The left arm runs a *uniform*
clock: accelerate briefly, cruise at constant $\dot s$, brake briefly. The right arm runs the clock
this chapter computes.

The uniform clock spends its acceleration where the clock says and pegs a motor; the time-scaled clock spends it where the configuration can afford it, and at every instant exactly one gauge is full and neither exceeds its rail.">
  <TwoClocksHook />
</Figure>

Nothing about the curve differs between the two lanes. What differs is $s(t)$: the map from time to
position along the path. Choset's §11.1 makes the distinction a definition. A **path** is a
twice-differentiable curve $q : [0, 1] \to \Q$. A **time scaling** is a twice-differentiable,
monotone map $s : [0, t_f] \to [0, 1]$ with $\dot s > 0$ on $(0, t_f)$. A **trajectory** is their
composition $q(s(t))$. Uniform time scalings $s(t) = kt$ are a thin subset of the ones allowed, and
the question of this chapter is: of all admissible clocks, which is fastest?

## Building intuition

### The phase plane is a speed-limit sign that changes every meter

The whole problem lives in one picture. Put $s$ on the horizontal axis and $\dot s$ on the
vertical. A time scaling is a curve from $(0, \dot s_0)$ to $(1, \dot s_f)$ that never dips below
$\dot s = 0$. At every state $(s, \dot s)$ the torque limits allow a range of path accelerations
$L \le \ddot s \le U$ — a *cone* of directions the curve may take. Above a certain speed the cone
closes: there is no torque vector that keeps the arm on the path. That locus is the
**velocity-limit curve**, and the region above it is **inadmissible**, hatched amber in the widget
exactly as a configuration-space obstacle would be — it is an obstacle in $(s, \dot s)$.

Three things to notice, each of which becomes a theorem in the next section.

**The limit is coupled, not per-joint.** Drag a waypoint so the elbow folds sharply mid-path and the
amber region dips to a waist. No single joint is near its own speed limit there; what has happened
is that the centrifugal torque one joint needs, $h\dot q_1^2$, is a torque the other joint's motor
must supply, and above some $\dot s$ it cannot while the first joint is also braking at full torque.
The dashed grey ghost is a constant-speed clock; wherever it turns amber, that clock is infeasible,
and no controller that only looks at the current joint speeds will know why.

**The optimal curve is made of two kinds of arc.** Solid blue curves are integrated forward at the
maximum acceleration $U$; the dashed blue curve $F$ is integrated backward from the goal at the
minimum acceleration $L$. The orange profile is a concatenation of pieces of them, switching at the
ticked points. The fastest trajectory is the one that is *highest* in the plane while staying out
of the amber: $t_f = \int_0^1 ds/\dot s$, so area under the curve is time saved.

**Zero-inertia points are where the picture changes shape.** The vertical dashed amber lines mark
$s$ values where some $a_i(s) = (M q')_i$ changes sign: that actuator momentarily cannot change
$\ddot s$ at all, and instead bounds $\dot s$ directly. The limit curve is continuous through such
a point but its slope is not, and the optimal profile may have to *slide* along it rather than
bounce off. Raise both torque limits by a factor of $1.5$ on the default spline and a slide appears
near $s = 0.57$.

The design question most readers bring to this chapter is: "time-optimal means every motor at full
power, right?" The Saturation Scope answers it with the torque histories of the solution above.

At every instant one strip rides its rail and the other does whatever the coupling demands; the
switch is the instant the saturated joint changes. Scale the limits to $1.5\times$ and the amber
stretch is a **singular arc**: the profile slides along the velocity-limit curve with an

derives exactly when that happens and what acceleration to use.

<KeyIdea title="The one-sentence version">
Fix the path and the $n$ torque limits become $2n$ bounds on a single scalar acceleration
$\ddot s$. The time-optimal clock maximizes $\dot s$ pointwise subject to those bounds, which makes
it bang-bang in $\ddot s$ — and finding it is a boundary-value problem in a two-dimensional plane
that can be drawn, read, and solved by integration.
</KeyIdea>

## The mathematics

### Definitions

**Path, time scaling, trajectory** are as in the hook: $q : [0,1] \to \Q$ twice differentiable;
$s : [0, t_f] \to [0,1]$ twice differentiable and monotone with $\dot s > 0$ on the open interval;
the trajectory is $q(s(t))$. Twice differentiability of $s$ is what makes $\ddot q$ exist and be
bounded, which is what makes torque finite.

**The motion cone** at $(s, \dot s)$ is the set of tangent directions of curves through that state
whose path acceleration satisfies $L(s, \dot s) \le \ddot s \le U(s, \dot s)$. The state is
**inadmissible** when $L > U$: the cone is empty and the robot is doomed to leave the path
immediately. At an admissible state the robot may still be doomed *eventually*, if every curve
inside the cones from there reaches the inadmissible region (Choset's figure 11.2).

**The velocity-limit curve** $v(s)$ is the locus where $L = U$ — the cone collapses to one vector.
It is computed by equating $L_i = U_j$ for every pair of actuators, solving each for $\dot s$, and
keeping the minimum.

**A bang-bang time scaling** has $\ddot s \in \{L, U\}$ at every instant except possibly on
singular arcs. At least one actuator is saturated at all times.

**Zero-inertia, critical, singular.** A point where $a_i(s) = 0$ for some $i$ is a *zero-inertia
point*: actuator $i$ cannot affect $\ddot s$ and instead bounds $\dot s$ directly. A point of the
limit curve $\dot s^{\max}$ set by such a bound is *critical* ($L < U$ there, unlike on $v$). A
critical point from which integrating $U$ forward or $L$ backward penetrates the limit curve
immediately is *singular*.

**A $\delta_v(c_0, c_1)$-safe trajectory** keeps clearance at least $c_0 + c_1\|\dot q\|$ from every
obstacle at every instant — the faster, the wider the berth.

### Path-constrained dynamics

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset eqs. 11.2–11.6)">
Along $q(s)$, the standard form $M\ddot q + \dot q\T\Gamma\dot q + g = u$ becomes
<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-robot}{a(s)}\,\ddot s + \htmlClass{term-cobstacle}{b(s)}\,\dot s^2 + c(s) = u,
\qquad a = M(q)\,q',\quad b = M(q)\,q'' + q'\T\,\Gamma(q)\,q',\quad c = g(q).
$$
</LinkedMath>
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Substituting the path into the Chapter 17 equations" result="a(s)\ddot s + b(s)\dot s^2 + c(s) = u">
**Step 1 — the chain rule.** With $q = q(s(t))$,
$\dot q = q'\dot s$ and $\ddot q = q''\dot s^2 + q'\ddot s$ (eqs. 11.2–11.3). The first is linear
in $\dot s$; the second has a term in $\dot s^2$ and a term in $\ddot s$, and nothing else.

**Step 2 — substitute.** Into $M(q)\ddot q + \dot q\T\Gamma(q)\dot q + g(q) = u$:
$$
M\,(q''\dot s^2 + q'\ddot s) + (q'\dot s)\T\,\Gamma\,(q'\dot s) + g = u .
$$

**Step 3 — group by powers of the clock.** The velocity-product term is bilinear in $\dot q$, so
the two factors of $\dot s$ come out as $\dot s^2$:
$$
\underbrace{M q'}_{a(s)}\,\ddot s + \underbrace{\big(M q'' + q'\T\Gamma q'\big)}_{b(s)}\,\dot s^2 + \underbrace{g}_{c(s)} = u .
$$
Every coefficient depends on $s$ alone. The $n$ equations now constrain one scalar unknown
$\ddot s$ at each state $(s, \dot s)$ — that is the whole content of path-velocity decomposition.

**The velocity-product piece of $b$ is exactly Chapter 17's `velocityProduct(q, q′)`**, the vector
$C(q, \dot q)\dot q$ evaluated at $\dot q = q'$. No new physics is computed here; the library
function `PathDynamics::abc` calls the Chapter 17 model and does the two matrix–vector products.

**Why $b_i$ can vanish when $a_i$ does not, and vice versa (Choset Problem 11.5).** $a_i = (Mq')_i$
is zero when the path's tangent is orthogonal, in the $M$-metric's $i$-th row, to joint $i$ — the
RP arm's prismatic joint at the midpoint of a straight Cartesian line, where the radial velocity is
momentarily zero. $b_i$ collects $M q''$ and the centrifugal/Coriolis terms, which can vanish on a
straight segment ($q'' = 0$) with no Coriolis coupling even where $a_i \ne 0$. The micro-example
below has $b_1 = 0$ and $a_1 = \pi$ at every $s$; Choset's RP arm has $a_2(\tfrac12) = 0$ and
$b_2 \equiv 0$.
</Derivation>

**The micro-example.** Reach from the Chapter 17 micro-example ($L_i = 1$, $r_i = \tfrac12$,
$m_1 = 2$, $m_2 = 1$, $I_1 = \tfrac16$, $I_2 = \tfrac1{12}$) on a *horizontal* Workbench
($a_g = 0$), joint 2 held at $\pi/2$ by its own motor while joint 1 sweeps a quarter turn:
$q(s) = (\pi s/2,\ \pi/2)$, so $q' = (\pi/2, 0)$, $q'' = 0$, and $M$ is constant along the path with
$M = \big[\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 1/3 \end{smallmatrix}\big]$ from Chapter 17. Then

$$
a(s) = M q' = \big(\pi,\ \tfrac{\pi}{6}\big) = (3.1416,\ 0.5236),\qquad
b(s) = \big(\Gamma^1_{11},\ \Gamma^2_{11}\big)\,\tfrac{\pi^2}{4} = (0,\ 0.5 \cdot 2.4674) = (0,\ 1.2337),\qquad
c(s) = 0 .
$$

Joint 2's $b_2 = h\,(q_1')^2$ is the centrifugal torque the elbow motor must supply to keep the elbow
at $\pi/2$ while the shoulder swings — the coupling that will set the speed limit.

### Acceleration bounds and the velocity-limit curve

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset eqs. 11.8–11.10)">
With $\alpha_i = (u_i^{\max} - b_i\dot s^2 - c_i)/a_i$ and $\beta_i$ the same with $u_i^{\min}$:
$U_i = \alpha_i, L_i = \beta_i$ if $a_i > 0$ and swapped if $a_i < 0$;
$L = \max_i L_i$, $U = \min_i U_i$; the feasible set is $L \le \ddot s \le U$, and it is empty above
$v(s) = \min_{i \ne j} \dot s_{ij}(s)$ where $\dot s_{ij}$ solves $L_i = U_j$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="From torque limits to a cone, and from the cone to the curve" result="L(s,\dot s) \le \ddot s \le U(s,\dot s);\quad v(s) = \min_{i,j}\dot s_{ij}(s)">
**Step 1 — isolate $\ddot s$ in each row.** Row $i$ of $u^{\min} \le a\ddot s + b\dot s^2 + c \le u^{\max}$
reads $u_i^{\min} - b_i\dot s^2 - c_i \le a_i\ddot s \le u_i^{\max} - b_i\dot s^2 - c_i$.

**Step 2 — divide, and flip if negative.** Dividing by $a_i > 0$ gives $\beta_i \le \ddot s \le \alpha_i$.
Dividing by $a_i < 0$ reverses the inequality: $\alpha_i \le \ddot s \le \beta_i$. That swap is the
whole content of eq. 11.8, and it is why the sign of $a_i$ matters: a joint whose coordinate
decreases along the path has its "maximum torque" bound on the *low* side of $\ddot s$.

**Step 3 — intersect the intervals.** $\ddot s$ must satisfy all $n$ rows, so it lies in
$[\max_i L_i, \min_i U_i] = [L, U]$. Each $L_i$ and $U_i$ is an affine function of $x = \dot s^2$ at
fixed $s$: $L_i = p_i + m_i x$ with $m_i = -b_i/a_i$. The max of affine functions is convex and
piecewise affine; the min is concave. Neither is smooth, so neither is $v$.

**Step 4 — the boundary of admissibility.** The cone is empty when $L > U$, i.e. when some
$L_i > U_j$. At fixed $s$, as $\dot s$ grows from zero, the first value at which some pair crosses,
$L_i(\dot s) = U_j(\dot s)$, is where admissibility is lost. Because each side is affine in $x$,
the crossing is in closed form, $x_{ij} = (p^U_j - p^L_i)/(m^L_i - m^U_j)$, and $v(s) = \min$ over
pairs of $\sqrt{x_{ij}}$ taken over positive roots. The minimum rule is exact rather than heuristic:
if any $L_i = U_j$ then $L \ge L_i = U_j \ge U$, so the state is already on or past the boundary.

**What the closed form assumes (Choset footnote 2).** That $\dot s = 0$ is admissible for every $s$
— the robot can hold any configuration statically — and that admissibility is lost at most once as
$\dot s$ grows. Friction, weak actuators, or velocity-dependent limits can violate this and create
inadmissible *islands* in the plane. The algorithm below ignores islands; the library reports the
first assumption's failure as an error rather than guessing.

**A check that is not a frozen number.** `velocityLimit` is compared against a brute-force scan of
$L \le U$ with bisection on $150$ seeded random $(a, b, c)$ rows in two and three dimensions; the
worst disagreement is $4 \times 10^{-14}$.
</Derivation>

Back to the micro-example, limits $|\tau_1| \le 20$, $|\tau_2| \le 12$ N·m. Joint 1:
$U_1 = 20/\pi = 6.366$, $L_1 = -6.366$, independent of $\dot s$ because $b_1 = c_1 = 0$. Joint 2:
$U_2 = (12 - 1.2337\dot s^2)/0.5236 = 22.918 - 2.3562\dot s^2$ and $L_2 = -22.918 - 2.3562\dot s^2$.
The only pair that can collapse is $L_1 = U_2$:
$-6.366 = 22.918 - 2.3562\dot s^2$, so $\dot s^2 = 12.429$ and

$$
v(s) = 3.5254 \quad\text{for every } s
$$

— a joint-1 speed of $5.54$ rad/s. Faster than this, the elbow motor cannot supply the centrifugal
torque $h\dot q_1^2$ while the shoulder brakes at full torque. Lower $\tau_2^{\max}$ to $10$ N·m and
$v$ drops to $3.2875$; the library pins both.

### Time optimality is bang-bang

<KeyIdea title="Statement">
$t_f = \int_0^1 ds/\dot s$, so the time-optimal scaling maximizes $\dot s$ pointwise subject to the
cones; its phase-plane curve follows a cone boundary ($\ddot s = U$ or $\ddot s = L$) except possibly
at zero-inertia points, and at least one actuator is saturated at all times.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Why the optimum rides the cone boundary" result="\ddot s \in \{L, U\}\ \text{except on singular arcs}">
**Step 1 — higher is faster.** For two admissible curves $\dot s_a(s) \ge \dot s_b(s)$ on $[0, 1]$,
$\int ds/\dot s_a \le \int ds/\dot s_b$. Time is monotone in the curve's height, so the optimum is
the pointwise-highest admissible curve.

**Step 2 — a tangent strictly inside the cone is wasteful.** If at some state the curve's slope
$d\dot s/ds = \ddot s/\dot s$ lies strictly between $L/\dot s$ and $U/\dot s$, one can raise the
curve locally — accelerate harder before, brake harder after — without leaving admissibility, and
the new curve is higher. So on an optimal curve the tangent is on the boundary almost everywhere.

**Step 3 — the reachable set is bounded by two integral curves.** From any state, the set of all
curves with tangents inside the cones is bounded above by the $U$-integral curve and below by the
$L$-integral curve (Choset's figure 11.2). The optimum is therefore a concatenation of pieces of
$U$- and $L$-curves.

**Step 4 — where the switches are.** The pieces meet where a $U$-curve crosses an $L$-curve, or
where the limit curve forces a change: a $U$-curve that would penetrate the amber must have been
abandoned earlier for an $L$-curve that grazes the limit tangentially. Finding those points is the
algorithm.

**The Pontryagin reading.** [Chapter 19](/chapters/ch19-trajectory-optimization) derives the same
result from the minimum principle: for a minimum-time problem the Hamiltonian is affine in the
control, $\partial H/\partial u$ contains no $u$, and $H$ is minimized at a bound of the control set.
Choset's §11.3.1 makes the connection explicit for the double integrator, where the switch is at
$t_s = \sqrt{d/u_{\max}}$ — the same number the lattice reproduces below.
</Derivation>

The micro-example never touches its limit curve. With constant $U_1 = 6.366$ and $L_1 = -6.366$ the
$A_0$ curve is $x = \dot s^2 = 2 \cdot 6.366\,s$ and $F$ is $x = 2 \cdot 6.366\,(1 - s)$; they cross at
$s = \tfrac12$ with $\dot s = \sqrt{6.366} = 2.5231 < 3.5254$. One switch, at

$$
t_s = \sqrt{2 \cdot 0.5 / 6.366} = 0.3963\ \text{s},\qquad t_f = 2 t_s = 0.7927\ \text{s}.
$$

Joint 2 is honest at the peak: during acceleration
$\tau_2 = a_2 U_1 + b_2\dot s^2 = 0.5236 \cdot 6.366 + 1.2337 \cdot 6.366 = 11.19 < 12$ N·m. That is
Choset's Example 11.2.1 situation — the limit curve never reached, one switch — on this book's arm.
Turn gravity on ($a_g = 9.81$) and at $s = \tfrac12$ the gravity vector is $c = (10.405, -3.468)$,
giving $U_1 = 3.054$, $L_1 = -9.678$ and $v(\tfrac12) = 4.080$; at $s = 0$ the shoulder's gravity
torque is $c_1 = 19.62$ N·m against a $20$ N·m limit, so $U_1(0) = 0.1210$ — the arm can barely lift,
and the whole motion takes $2.3276$ s with its single switch pushed out to $s = 0.7342$.

### Zero-inertia points and singular arcs

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset §11.2.1)">
Where $a_i(s) = 0$, row $i$ becomes the direct velocity bound
$u_i^{\min} \le b_i\dot s^2 + c_i \le u_i^{\max}$, giving $\dot s^{\max}_{zip}(s)$; the true limit
is $\dot s^{\max} = \min(v, \dot s^{\max}_{zip})$. At a singular point the feasible accelerations are
$\ddot s_{\max} = \min(\ddot s^{+}_{tangent}, U)$ and $\ddot s_{\min} = \max(\ddot s^{-}_{tangent}, L)$
with $\ddot s_{tangent} = \dot s^{\max}\,d\dot s^{\max}/ds$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Sliding along the limit curve" result="\ddot s_{\max} = \min(\ddot s^{+}_{tangent}, U),\quad \ddot s_{\min} = \max(\ddot s^{-}_{tangent}, L)">
**Step 1 — the row loses its $\ddot s$.** With $a_i = 0$, row $i$ of eq. 11.7 reads
$u_i^{\min} \le b_i\dot s^2 + c_i \le u_i^{\max}$. Nothing the actuator does changes $\ddot s$; the
constraint is on $\dot s$ alone. Solving for $\dot s$ gives $\dot s^{\max}_{zip}$ (eq. 11.11). If
$b_i = 0$ too, the row constrains nothing — provided the robot can hold the configuration, which is
Choset's Problem 11.6 and the standing assumption.

**Step 2 — a critical point is not a collapsed cone.** On $v(s)$ the cone is a single vector,
$L = U$. At a point of $\dot s^{\max}$ set by a zero-inertia bound, $L < U$: the cone is open, but it
may point out of the admissible region.

**Step 3 — clip to the tangent.** Following $U$ forward from a singular point penetrates the limit
curve; the fastest admissible acceleration is the one that keeps the state *on* the curve, which is
the curve's tangent in the $(s, \dot s)$ plane: $d\dot s/dt = \ddot s = \dot s\,d\dot s^{\max}/ds$.
Hence $\ddot s_{\max} = \min(\ddot s^{+}_{tangent}, U)$, with the right-hand derivative where the
curve has a corner, and symmetrically $\ddot s_{\min} = \max(\ddot s^{-}_{tangent}, L)$ for the
backward integration.

**Step 4 — singular arcs.** Critical points lie where $M(q)$ loses rank along the path's tangent, a
lower-dimensional set; a path crossing it transversally has isolated critical points, a path moving
along it has a *critical arc*. Integrating $\ddot s_{\max}$ and $\ddot s_{\min}$ instead of $U$ and
$L$ lets the algorithm slide along such an arc with an acceleration strictly between $L$ and $U$,
rather than chattering between them — and on a slide neither actuator is saturated.

**In $x = \dot s^2$ the rule is a one-liner.** The library integrates in $x$, where a stage from
$s_k$ to $s_{k+1}$ with constant $\ddot s$ moves $x$ by $2\ddot s\,\Delta$. When the free step would
land above $x_{\max}(s_{k+1})$, the stage acceleration that lands exactly on the curve is
$u^* = (x_{\max} - x_k)/(2\Delta)$; if $u^*$ is inside the cone the stage slides, otherwise it
penetrates. At a critical point that is Choset's rule; at an ordinary point of $v$ the collapsed
cone makes the test pass only when the integral curve is tangent to the limit — which is the
tangent-point condition of step 4 of the algorithm, found for free.
</Derivation>

**Choset's RP arm, honestly.** Example 11.2.1 runs the RP arm of Chapter 17's Example 10.1.2
($m_1 = 5$, $I_1 = 0.1$, $r_1 = 0.2$, $m_2 = 3$, $I_2 = 0.05$, $a_g = 9.8$) along the straight

### GRID SEARCH is optimal for its discretization

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset Algorithm 21, Theorem 11.3.2)">
For $\ddot q_i = a_i$ with $a_i \in \{-a_{\max}, 0, a_{\max}\}$ held for time $h$, every reachable
state lies on a lattice with spacing $a_{\max}h$ in $\dot q$ and $\tfrac12 a_{\max}h^2$ in $q$;
breadth-first expansion by level returns the fewest-steps safe trajectory. For a point robot in
$n \in \{2, 3\}$ dimensions among polyhedral obstacles, if a $\delta_v(c_0, c_1)$-safe trajectory of
duration $T_{opt}$ exists, then with $h \le v_{\max}/a_{\max}$ and
$h \le c_0\epsilon/(2a_{\max}c_1(1 - \epsilon) + 5v_{\max})$ the search returns a
$(1 - \epsilon)\delta_v$-safe trajectory of duration at most $T_{opt}$ from an $O(\epsilon)$-perturbed
start to an $O(\epsilon)$ goal neighborhood, in time $O((1/\epsilon)^{3n})$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Why the tree is a grid, and what the theorem buys" result="O((1/\epsilon)^{3n}),\ \text{time} \le T_{opt},\ (1-\epsilon)\delta_v\text{-safe}">
**Step 1 — closure under the control set.** Integrating $\ddot q_i = c\,a_{\max}$, $c \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$,
for time $h$ from $(q_i, \dot q_i)$ gives $\dot q_i + c\,a_{\max}h$ and
$q_i + \dot q_i h + \tfrac12 c\,a_{\max}h^2$. Write $\dot q_i = m\,a_{\max}h$ and
$q_i = p\cdot\tfrac12 a_{\max}h^2$ with integers $m, p$; then the new indices are $m + c$ and
$p + 2m + c$ — integers again. Every node of Choset's figure 11.8 lies on the grid of figure 11.9,
and the tree is really a graph on that grid.

**Step 2 — level is time.** Each edge takes exactly $h$, so breadth-first expansion is uniform-cost
search with unit edges, and the first level at which a node enters the goal region is the minimum
number of steps: time-optimal for the chosen discretization of time and controls. A* with an
admissible heuristic pops the same level with fewer expansions. The per-axis continuous bang-bang
time $\max_i T_i$ is admissible because it ignores the other axes, the obstacles, the velocity
limit and the discretization — every one of which can only make the lattice slower.

**Step 3 — safety absorbs the discretization.** Any continuous $\delta_v$-safe trajectory can be
tracked by lattice moves within an error that shrinks with $h$; the margin $c_0 + c_1\|\dot q\|$ is
spent on that error, leaving $(1 - \epsilon)\delta_v$. The two bounds on $h$ are what make the
bookkeeping close — the first keeps $v_{\max}$ an integer multiple of $a_{\max}h$, the second ties
the tracking error to the margin.

**Step 4 — the grid is finite, so the time is bounded.** The configuration-space diameter, the
velocity bound and $h$ fix the number of lattice points, which is $O((1/\epsilon)^{3n})$ once $h$
is expressed through $\epsilon$. Polynomial in $\epsilon$, exponential in $n$: nobody has run this
beyond a few degrees of freedom, and Choset says so.

**What the theorem does not say.** The $(1 - \epsilon)\delta_v$-safe trajectory the algorithm finds
may look nothing like the true time-optimal $\delta_v$-safe one; the only promise is about its
duration. And the proof — Donald and Xavier's — is beyond this chapter as it is beyond Choset's.

**The manipulator generalization (Choset pp. 396–399).** For $M(q)\ddot q + C\dot q + g = u$ with box
torque limits, the feasible accelerations at a state form a parallelepiped $\mathcal{A}(q, \dot q)$
that changes during the step and collapses where $M^{-1}$ loses rank. A bound on the largest
eigenvalue of $M$ keeps it from collapsing; a bound on $\dot M$ gives a conservative subset
$\hat{\mathcal{A}} \subset \mathcal{A}$ feasible over the whole step; the controls are the
$\ddot q$ grid points inside $\hat{\mathcal{A}}$. The completeness result survives with
$(1 + \epsilon)T_{opt}$ in place of $T_{opt}$; the running time stays exponential in $n$.
</Derivation>

### TOPP-RA: reachability on squared speed

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Pham & Pham 2018)">
With $x = \dot s^2$ and $u = \ddot s$, $dx/ds = 2u$, and each actuator limit
$u_i^{\min} \le a_i u + b_i x + c_i \le u_i^{\max}$ is *linear* in $(x, u)$ at fixed $s$. On a grid
$s_0 < \dots < s_N$ with $x_{k+1} = x_k + 2u_k\Delta_k$, the set of $x_k$ from which some feasible
$u_k$ lands $x_{k+1}$ in a target interval is itself an interval, computable by two two-variable
linear programs. A backward pass yields the controllable intervals $K_k$; a greedy forward pass
picking the largest feasible $x_{k+1} \in K_{k+1}$ is time-optimal for the discretization.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Interval propagation instead of switch hunting" result="K_k = \{x \ge 0 : \exists u\ \text{feasible at } k,\ x + 2u\Delta_k \in K_{k+1}\};\quad x_{k+1} = \max"
>
**Step 1 — the substitution.** $\tfrac{d}{ds}\dot s^2 = 2\dot s\,\tfrac{d\dot s}{ds} = 2\ddot s$.
In $x$ the kinematics are linear and a constant-$\ddot s$ stage is a straight line; the micro-example's
switch at $s = \tfrac12$ lands on the grid exactly, and $\dot s = 0$ is no longer a singularity of
the integration.

**Step 2 — convexity of the stage constraint set.** At fixed $s_k$ the $2n$ inequalities in
$(x_k, u_k)$ are half-planes, and $x_{k+1} = x_k + 2u_k\Delta$ is affine. The feasible set is a
convex polygon; its projection onto the $x_k$ axis is an interval.

**Step 3 — propagate backward.** $K_N = [x_f, x_f]$. Given $K_{k+1} = [\ell, h]$, add the two
half-planes $\ell \le x_k + 2u_k\Delta \le h$ and take the minimum and maximum of $x_k$ over the
polygon — two two-variable LPs, solved exactly by vertex enumeration since there are at most
$2n + 6$ half-planes. The result is $K_k$, the set of states at $s_k$ from which the goal is still
reachable: the complement of Choset's "doomed" states, computed without ever drawing a curve.

**Step 4 — greedy forward is optimal.** A larger $x_k$ never shrinks the reachable set downstream
(controllability is monotone in $x$ under these constraints), so choosing the largest admissible
$x_{k+1} \in K_{k+1}$ at each stage dominates every other choice pointwise, and pointwise-highest
is fastest by the first derivation.

**What it sidesteps.** No tangent points, no root finding, no footnote-3 bisection, no special
case for zero-inertia rows — a row with $a_i = 0$ is just a half-plane with no $u$ in it. The price
is a grid: the answer is optimal for the discretization, and the stage constraints are enforced at
$s_k$ only. On the chapter's seeded gravity-loaded splines at $N = 800$ the two constructions agree
on $t_f$ to $0.38\%$ at worst, and they agree on which problems have no solution at all.
</Derivation>

## The algorithm

Choset's five steps, with the §11.2.1 modification and the footnote-3 bisection that this chapter's
implementation uses in step 4. The construction is drawn live in w18.1: $F$ dashed, each $A_i$
solid, the hit points in amber.

<Algorithm
  name="time_scale(a, b, c, u_min, u_max, ṡ₀, ṡ_f)"
  inputs="path-constrained dynamics on a grid s_k = k/N, actuator limits, boundary speeds"
  outputs="switch list 𝒮, the profile (s_k, ṡ_k), t_f — or StallsAtZero / NoSolution / LimitIsland"
  complexity="O(N) stages per curve; O(N log(1/tol)) per switch for the bisection"
>
1. $\mathcal{S} \leftarrow \{\}$, $i \leftarrow 0$, $(s_i, \dot s_i) \leftarrow (0, \dot s_0)$. Tabulate $\dot s^{\max}(s_k) = \min(v, \dot s^{\max}_{zip}, \dot s^{\max}_{rate})$ and fail with NoSolution if any $\dot s^{\max}(s_k) \le 0$ (the robot cannot hold that configuration).
2. **Backward curve $F$.** Integrate $\ddot s = L$ backward from $(1, \dot s_f)$ until the limit curve is penetrated (record the hit), $s = 0$, or $\dot s = 0$ at $s < 1$ — in which case return StallsAtZero (Problem 11.3). A stage that would penetrate but can land on the curve with $\ddot s_{\min} = \max(\ddot s^{-}_{tangent}, L)$ slides instead.
3. **Forward curve $A_i$.** Integrate $\ddot s = U$ forward from $(s_i, \dot s_i)$, sliding with $\ddot s_{\max} = \min(\ddot s^{+}_{tangent}, U)$ where allowed, until it crosses $F$ — append the crossing $s$ to $\mathcal{S}$ and **return** — or penetrates the limit curve at $(s_{lim}, \dot s_{lim})$. If it reaches $s = 1$ below $\dot s_f$ or reaches $\dot s = 0$, return NoSolution (Problem 11.4).
4. **Tangent point (footnote 3).** On the line $s = s_{lim}$ bisect on $\dot s' \in [0, \dot s_{lim}]$ for the highest $L$-curve from $(s_{lim}, \dot s')$ that does not penetrate the limit curve; its point of closest approach is $(s_{tan}, \dot s_{tan})$ — a tangent point, or a critical point. Integrate $\ddot s = L$ backward from $(s_{lim}, \dot s')$ until it rises above $A_i$; the crossing $s_{i+1}$ is a max→min switch — append it. If it reaches $\dot s = 0$ or passes below the start, return NoSolution: the start state is doomed (figure 11.2).
5. $(s_{i+2}, \dot s_{i+2}) \leftarrow (s_{tan}, \dot s_{tan})$ is a min→max switch — append it, set $i \leftarrow i + 2$, go to 3. A min→max switch immediately followed by a max→min at the same node is a slide, not a switch, and is removed from $\mathcal{S}$.
</Algorithm>

Two implementation choices matter. The curves are integrated in $x = \dot s^2$ by RK4 on the uniform
grid, with the stage states clamped to the admissible region at the half-grid nodes — next to a
zero-inertia point a stage that lands a hair above the limit would otherwise read a bound
$n_i/a_i$ with $a_i \approx 0$ and drive $x$ negative. And the bisection in step 4 replaces the
slope test $dv/ds = U/v$ of Choset's main text: it never selects a tangent point whose own
deceleration curve is doomed, which the slope test can, and it costs about $48$ extra $L$-curve
integrations per switch.

<Algorithm
  name="toppra(a, b, c, u_min, u_max, grid)"
  inputs="path-constrained dynamics tabulated at s_0 < … < s_N, limits, ṡ₀, ṡ_f"
  outputs="controllable sets K_k, the greedy profile x_k, t_f — or the first k at which K_k is empty"
  complexity="O(N) two-variable LPs backward, O(N) forward"
>
1. $K_N \leftarrow [\dot s_f^2, \dot s_f^2]$.
2. for $k = N-1$ down to $0$: half-planes $u_i^{\min} \le a_i u + b_i x + c_i \le u_i^{\max}$ at $s_k$, plus $x \ge 0$ and $K_{k+1}^{lo} \le x + 2u\Delta_k \le K_{k+1}^{hi}$; $K_k \leftarrow [\min x, \max x]$ over the polygon's vertices. If empty, report no solution from $s_k$.
3. if $\dot s_0^2 \notin K_0$: no solution (the start is doomed).
4. for $k = 0$ to $N-1$: $u_k \leftarrow$ the largest $u$ feasible at $(s_k, x_k)$ with $x_k + 2u\Delta_k \in K_{k+1}$; $x_{k+1} \leftarrow x_k + 2u_k\Delta_k$.
5. $t_f \leftarrow \sum_k 2\Delta_k/(\sqrt{x_k} + \sqrt{x_{k+1}})$.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="GRID SEARCH((q*_start, q̇*_start), G, 𝒜, h)  —  Choset Algorithm 21"
  inputs="lattice start state, goal region, control set {−a_max, 0, a_max}ⁿ, timestep"
  outputs="a piecewise-constant-acceleration trajectory to G, or FAILURE"
  complexity="O((1/ε)^{3n}) — Theorem 11.3.2"
>
1. place the start at the root of $T$ (level 0); $level \leftarrow 0$, $solved \leftarrow$ false
2. while not $solved$: if level $level$ is empty return FAILURE
3. for each node in level $level$, for each control in $\mathcal{A}$: integrate the control for time $h$, getting $newnode$
4. if $newnode$ is new, and the arc neither passes within $c_0 + c_1\|\dot q\|$ of an obstacle nor exceeds $v_{\max}$: add it as a child
5. if the arc enters $G$: $solved \leftarrow$ true, store $newnode$
6. $level \leftarrow level + 1$
7. return the stored trajectory that reaches $G$ first
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm

1. graph: node $(p, m)$; neighbors $(p + 2m + c,\ m + c)$ for $c \in \{-1, 0, 1\}^n$, kept if the quadratic arc is safe and $|(m + c)\,a_{\max}h| \le v_{\max}$; every node inside $G$ shares one key

use nalgebra::SVector;

/// A twice-differentiable curve q : [0, 1] → Q. `ddq` must be *continuous*: the
/// velocity-product term b(s) contains M q'', and a C¹ spline would make it jump
/// at every knot — the phase plane would grow a fence of fake zero-inertia points.
pub trait Path<const N: usize> {
    fn q(&self, s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N>;
    fn dq(&self, s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N>;
    fn ddq(&self, s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N>;
}

/// A straight line in the chart; ddq ≡ 0. The micro-example's path.
pub struct Segment<const N: usize> { pub a: SVector<f64, N>, pub b: SVector<f64, N> }

impl<const N: usize> Path<N> for Segment<N> {
    fn q(&self, s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N> { self.a + s * (self.b - self.a) }
    fn dq(&self, _s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N> { self.b - self.a }
    fn ddq(&self, _s: f64) -> SVector<f64, N> { SVector::zeros() }
}

/// Natural cubic spline through m ≥ 2 waypoints at uniform knots s_k = k/(m − 1).
/// Natural ends (q'' = 0) are the right choice for a rest-to-rest motion: b(s)
/// then vanishes at both ends and the plane's edges are governed by a and c alone.
pub struct CubicSpline<const N: usize> {
    knots: Vec<f64>,
    pts: Vec<SVector<f64, N>>,
    /// Second derivatives at the knots, from one tridiagonal solve per coordinate.
    m2: Vec<SVector<f64, N>>,
}

impl<const N: usize> CubicSpline<N> {
    /// Waypoints on Tⁿ are unwrapped first: consecutive angles differ by the
    /// shortest arc, so a path through the seam is fitted in the chart lift
    /// rather than interpolated across a 2π jump (Chapter 5).
    pub fn on_torus(waypoints: &[SVector<f64, N>]) -> Self {
        let mut pts = vec![waypoints[0]];
        for w in &waypoints[1..] {
            let prev = *pts.last().unwrap();
            pts.push(prev + (w - prev).map(|d| (d + PI).rem_euclid(2.0 * PI) - PI));
        }
        Self::fit(pts)
    }

    fn fit(pts: Vec<SVector<f64, N>>) -> Self {
        let m = pts.len();
        let knots: Vec<f64> = (0..m).map(|k| k as f64 / (m - 1) as f64).collect();
        // Interior rows: h M_{k-1} + 4h M_k + h M_{k+1} = 6 (d_k − d_{k-1}); M_0 = M_{m-1} = 0.
        let m2 = natural_second_derivatives(&knots, &pts);
        Self { knots, pts, m2 }
    }
}

```rust title="crates/trajectory/src/constrained.rs + limits.rs"
use dynamics::Dynamics;

/// a(s) s̈ + b(s) ṡ² + c(s) = u — Chapter 17's model restricted to a path (C eq. 11.6).
pub struct PathDynamics<'a, D: Dynamics<N>, P: Path<N>, const N: usize> {
    pub dynamics: &'a D,
    pub path: &'a P,
}

impl<D: Dynamics<N>, P: Path<N>, const N: usize> PathDynamics<'_, D, P, N> {
    /// b uses the velocity-product *vector* at q̇ = q'(s): q'ᵀ Γ q' = C(q, q') q' exactly.
    pub fn abc(&self, s: f64) -> (SVector<f64, N>, SVector<f64, N>, SVector<f64, N>) {
        let (q, dq, ddq) = (self.path.q(s), self.path.dq(s), self.path.ddq(s));
        let m = self.dynamics.mass(&q);
        (m * dq, m * ddq + self.dynamics.velocity_product(&q, &dq), self.dynamics.gravity(&q))
    }

    /// The torque that executes the path at phase state (s, ṡ) with path acceleration s̈.
    pub fn torque(&self, s: f64, sdot: f64, sddot: f64) -> SVector<f64, N> {
        let (a, b, c) = self.abc(s);
        a * sddot + b * sdot * sdot + c
    }
}

pub struct TorqueLimits<const N: usize> { pub min: SVector<f64, N>, pub max: SVector<f64, N> }

/// Below this |a_i| a row is zero-inertia: it bounds ṡ, not s̈.
pub const ZERO_INERTIA_EPS: f64 = 1e-9;

/// (L, U) at a phase-plane state — eqs. 11.8–11.9. Dividing by a negative a_i
/// flips the inequality; that swap is the whole content of 11.8.
pub fn accel_bounds<const N: usize>(abc: &Abc<N>, lim: &TorqueLimits<N>, sdot: f64) -> (f64, f64) {
    let x = sdot * sdot;
    let (mut lo, mut hi) = (f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::INFINITY);
    for i in 0..N {
        let ai = abc.0[i];
        if ai.abs() < ZERO_INERTIA_EPS { continue; }
        let alpha = (lim.max[i] - abc.1[i] * x - abc.2[i]) / ai;
        let beta  = (lim.min[i] - abc.1[i] * x - abc.2[i]) / ai;
        let (li, ui) = if ai > 0.0 { (beta, alpha) } else { (alpha, beta) };
        lo = lo.max(li);
        hi = hi.min(ui);
    }
    (lo, hi)
}

/// v(s) from the pairwise crossings L_i = U_j (eq. 11.10). Each bound is affine
/// in x = ṡ², p + m x, so the crossing is x = (p_U − p_L)/(m_L − m_U); keep the
/// smallest positive root over all pairs. +∞ when no pair can collapse the cone.
pub fn velocity_limit<const N: usize>(abc: &Abc<N>, lim: &TorqueLimits<N>) -> f64 {
    let rows: Vec<Row> = (0..N).filter(|&i| abc.0[i].abs() >= ZERO_INERTIA_EPS).map(|i| Row::new(abc, lim, i)).collect();
    let mut best = f64::INFINITY;
    for li in &rows { for uj in &rows {
        let den = li.m_l - uj.m_u;
        if den.abs() < 1e-14 { continue; }
        let x = (uj.p_u - li.p_l) / den;
        if x > 0.0 && x < best { best = x; }
    } }
    best.sqrt()
}

/// ṡ_max(s) = min(v, ṡ_zip, ṡ_rate): the true limit curve of §11.2.1, with
/// joint-rate limits |q̇_i| ≤ v_i folded in as rows ṡ ≤ v_i / |q'_i| (Exercise 5).
pub fn sdot_max<const N: usize>(abc: &Abc<N>, dq: &SVector<f64, N>, lim: &TorqueLimits<N>, rate: Option<&[f64; N]>) -> LimitPoint {
    let v = velocity_limit(abc, lim);
    let zip = zero_inertia_limit(abc, lim);        // u_min ≤ b_i ṡ² + c_i ≤ u_max on rows with a_i = 0
    let rate = joint_rate_limit(dq, rate);
    LimitPoint::min_of(v, zip, rate)               // remembers which bound is active: cone, zip or rate
}

/// One grid stage in x = ṡ². When the free step leaves the admissible region,
/// try the stage acceleration that lands exactly on the limit curve; it is
/// feasible iff it lies in the cone — Choset's s̈_max = min(s̈⁺_tangent, U) at a
/// critical point, and the tangency test for free on an ordinary point of v(s).
fn step_clipped(t: &Tables, k: usize, x: f64, mode: Mode, dir: i32) -> StepOut {
    let raw = rk4_in_x(t, k, x, mode, dir);              // stage states clamped to x ≤ x_max at the half grid
    let kn = (k as i32 + dir) as usize;
    if !above(raw, t.xmax[kn]) { return StepOut::free(raw); }
    let u_star = dir as f64 * (t.xmax[kn] - x) / (2.0 * t.ds);
    let (l0, u0) = accel_bounds(&t.abc[k], &t.lim, x.sqrt());
    let (l1, u1) = accel_bounds(&t.abc[kn], &t.lim, t.xmax[kn].sqrt());
    if u_star >= l0.min(l1) && u_star <= u0.max(u1) { StepOut::slide(t.xmax[kn]) } else { StepOut::penetrated(raw) }
}

pub fn time_scale<const N: usize>(pd: &PathDynamics<'_, impl Dynamics<N>, impl Path<N>, N>, lim: &TorqueLimits<N>,
                                  sdot0: f64, sdot_f: f64, grid: usize) -> Result<TimeScaling, TimeScaleError> {
    let t = Tables::tabulate(pd, lim, grid);
    // Step 2: F backward from (1, ṡ_f).
    let f = integrate_backward(&t, grid, sdot_f * sdot_f, Mode::Min)?;    // StallsAtZero if x ≤ 0 before s = 0
    let (mut ki, mut xi, mut switches, mut profile) = (0, sdot0 * sdot0, vec![], Profile::new(grid));
    loop {
        // Step 3: A_i forward with U; crossing F is tested on the free curve before penetration.
        match integrate_forward(&t, ki, xi, Mode::Max, &f)? {
            Forward::CrossedF { node, frac, a } => { profile.write(ki, node, &a, Mode::Max); profile.write_tail(&f);
                                                   switches.push_crossing(node, frac, grid); break; }
            Forward::Penetrated { k_lim, a, .. } => {
                // Step 4, footnote 3: bisect on ṡ′ at s_lim for the highest admissible L-curve.
                let (x_prime, lc) = bisect_highest_admissible_l_curve(&t, k_lim, a[k_lim], &f);
                let k_tan = lc.closest_approach_to_limit();                        // tangent or critical point
                let meet = meet_backward(&t, &a, ki, k_lim, x_prime)?;            // where L rises above A_i
                profile.write(ki, meet.node, &a, Mode::Max);
                profile.write(meet.node + 1, k_tan, &lc, Mode::Min);
                switches.push_max_to_min(meet); switches.push_min_to_max(k_tan);  // Step 5
                (ki, xi) = (k_tan, lc[k_tan]);
            }
        }
    }
    Ok(profile.finish(switches))     // t_f = Σ 2Δ/(ṡ_k + ṡ_{k+1}); slides reported as Mode::Slide
}
```

```rust title="crates/trajectory/src/toppra.rs"
/// TOPP-RA (Pham & Pham 2018): controllable intervals in x = ṡ² backward, greedy forward.
pub fn toppra<const N: usize>(pd: &PathDynamics<'_, impl Dynamics<N>, impl Path<N>, N>, lim: &TorqueLimits<N>,
                              sdot0: f64, sdot_f: f64, grid: usize) -> Result<TimeScaling, TimeScaleError> {
    let ds = 1.0 / grid as f64;
    let abc: Vec<Abc<N>> = (0..=grid).map(|k| pd.abc(k as f64 * ds)).collect();
    let mut k_set = vec![(sdot_f * sdot_f, sdot_f * sdot_f); grid + 1];
    for k in (0..grid).rev() {
        // Half-planes in (x, u): the 2N actuator rows at s_k, x ≥ 0, and ℓ ≤ x + 2uΔ ≤ h for K_{k+1}.
        let mut hp = actuator_half_planes(&abc[k], lim);     // a row with a_i = 0 is a half-plane with no u in it
        hp.push(HalfPlane { p: 1.0, r: 2.0 * ds, q: k_set[k + 1].1 });
        hp.push(HalfPlane { p: -1.0, r: -2.0 * ds, q: -k_set[k + 1].0 });
        k_set[k] = extreme_x(&hp).ok_or(TimeScaleError::NoSolution { s: k as f64 * ds, detail: "controllable set empty".into() })?;
    }
    if sdot0 * sdot0 < k_set[0].0 || sdot0 * sdot0 > k_set[0].1 { return Err(TimeScaleError::NoSolution { s: 0.0, detail: "ṡ₀ not controllable".into() }); }
    let mut x = vec![sdot0 * sdot0; grid + 1];
    for k in 0..grid {
        let (lo, hi) = feasible_u(&abc[k], lim, x[k]);                        // the cone at (s_k, x_k)
        let u = hi.min((k_set[k + 1].1 - x[k]) / (2.0 * ds)).max(lo.max((k_set[k + 1].0 - x[k]) / (2.0 * ds)));
        x[k + 1] = (x[k] + 2.0 * u * ds).max(0.0);                            // greedy: largest x_{k+1} ∈ K_{k+1}
    }
    Ok(TimeScaling::from_x(x, ds))
}
```

use search::{astar, Graph};

/// Integer lattice coordinates: q_i = p_i · ½ a_max h², q̇_i = m_i · a_max h.
#[derive(Clone, PartialEq, Eq, Hash)]
pub struct LatticeState<const N: usize> { pub p: [i64; N], pub m: [i64; N] }

pub struct LatticeGraph<'a, const N: usize> {
    pub a_max: f64, pub h: f64, pub v_max: f64,
    pub c0: f64, pub c1: f64,
    /// Clearance at a configuration; the lattice samples it along every arc.
    pub clearance: &'a dyn Fn(&SVector<f64, N>) -> f64,
    pub goal: LatticeState<N>,
}

impl<const N: usize> Graph<LatticeState<N>> for LatticeGraph<'_, N> {
    /// Neighbors are (p + 2m + c, m + c) for c ∈ {−1, 0, 1}ᴺ, kept when the
    /// quadratic arc is δ_v(c₀, c₁)-safe and the end velocity is within v_max.
    fn neighbors(&self, s: &LatticeState<N>) -> Vec<(LatticeState<N>, f64)> {
        controls::<N>().filter_map(|c| {
            let next = LatticeState { p: array_fn(|i| s.p[i] + 2 * s.m[i] + c[i]), m: array_fn(|i| s.m[i] + c[i]) };
            self.arc_safe(s, &c).then_some((next, self.h))
        }).collect()
    }
}

/// also Chapter 19's min-time double integrator read off the minimum principle.
pub fn bang_bang_time_1d(q0: f64, v0: f64, q1: f64, v1: f64, a: f64) -> f64 { /* two candidate families */ }

/// Algorithm 21 with A* (h = max_i per-axis bang-bang time, admissible) or Choset's literal breadth-first.
pub fn lattice_search<const N: usize>(start: LatticeState<N>, goal: LatticeState<N>, g: &LatticeGraph<'_, N>) -> Option<Vec<[i64; N]>> {
    let h = |n: &LatticeState<N>| (0..N).map(|i| bang_bang_time_1d(q_of(n.p[i], g), v_of(n.m[i], g), q_of(goal.p[i], g), v_of(goal.m[i], g), g.a_max))
                                        .fold(0.0, f64::max);
    let path = astar(g, start, goal, h)?;
    Some(path.windows(2).map(|w| array_fn(|i| w[1].m[i] - w[0].m[i])).collect())   // controls are Δm
}

## Putting it together

The integration lab is the pipeline [Chapter 23](/chapters/ch23-capstone) ships: a Chapter 12 RRT
path for Reach, shortcut by Chapter 13 into a polyline, smoothed into a `CubicSpline` on $T^2$,
time-scaled here, and replayed by the Chapter 17 integrator with the computed torques. On the
Workbench with gravity the gauge grazes its rail at every instant and never crosses it — that is
what bang-bang looks like from the motor's side.

Two honesty items the lab makes visible. First, the shortcutting step matters more than it looks:
a polyline has corners, a spline through a polyline's vertices has large $q''$ near sharp ones, and
large $q''$ is large $b(s)$, which lowers the velocity-limit curve exactly where the path turns. The
time scaler is only as fast as the path is smooth. Second, decoupled planning is honest about time
only *locally*: it finds the fastest clock for the path it was handed, and a slightly different path
through the same homotopy class may be much faster. Choset's §11.2.2 sketches Shiller and Dubowsky's
answer — enumerate grid paths, bound their times from below with a speed cap, time-scale the
best, prune the rest, and polish locally with the time scaler as the objective. Modern practice
replaces that search with the optimizers of the next chapter, which move the path and the clock
together. The lattice of §11.3.3 is the other direct route, and its exponential grid is the reason
Chapter 21's state lattices and hybrid A\* prune it with motion primitives and a heuristic rather
than enumerate it.

What survives into [Chapter 19](/chapters/ch19-trajectory-optimization) unchanged: the time-scaled
trajectory is the warm start CHOMP and iLQR polish; the bang-bang structure reappears from
Pontryagin's minimum principle; and the `Trajectory` type is what every controller there accepts.
