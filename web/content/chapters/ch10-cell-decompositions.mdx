Every planner so far answered "how do I get *there*?" This chapter asks "how do I visit
*everywhere*?" — the question of a vacuum cleaner, a demining robot, a lawnmower, a wiping pad on
a bench. One object answers both. Carve $\Qfree$ into **cells** simple enough that a back-and-forth
motion sweeps each one, and record which cells touch in an **adjacency graph**: planning is then a
search on that graph (Chapter 6), and coverage is a walk that visits every node.

The interesting question is *where the cell walls go*. The trapezoidal decomposition puts one at
every vertex — correct, simple, and over-chopped. The boustrophedon decomposition puts one only
where a sweeping slice changes connectivity: at the **critical points of the slice function on the
obstacle boundary**, which is the Morse vocabulary of Chapter 8 read by the rank condition of
Chapter 9. Those critical points can be *sensed* — the range ring's shortest ray lines up with the
sweep — so coverage becomes an online algorithm that explores an unknown room while it mows it,
with a completeness guarantee and a path length linear in the area, the number of critical points,
and the perimeter. A short coda carves the plane by *visibility* instead, and uses the cells to
find an evader.

Everything on the page runs. The sweep on the chapter's micro-world produces the 12 trapezoids
and 13 adjacencies the design promises and its list of crossed edges matches a brute-force re-sort
at every event; Euler's count holds on 300 seeded worlds, convex and star-shaped; the online
coverer re-discovers the six critical points of the offline sweep from range data alone, with its
shortest ray horizontal to within a hundredth of a degree; and the information-graph search
proves — by exhausting it — that one pursuer cannot clear three pockets.
</Overview>

The raster scan *looked* done. Every lap it drove was a full lap, every turn was neat, and the

## Building intuition

### Cells, walls, and a graph

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — exact cell decomposition, adjacency graph, coverage (Choset Ch. 6 introduction)">
An **exact cell decomposition** of $\Qfree$ is a partition into simple regions — **cells** $c_i$ —
whose union is exactly $\Qfree$. Two cells are **adjacent** if they share a boundary; the
**adjacency graph** has a node per cell and an edge per shared boundary. **Planning** with a
decomposition is two steps: locate the cells containing $\qstart$ and $\qgoal$, then search the
adjacency graph. A **coverage path** passes an effector of width $2r$ over every point of
$\Qfree$; with cells it is an **exhaustive walk** through the adjacency graph (every node visited)
plus a pattern inside each cell.
</KeyIdea>

The cells have to be "simple" in a specific sense: something a robot can cover without thinking.
A cell bounded left and right by vertical segments and above and below by obstacle boundary — a
*floor* and a *ceiling* that never double back in $x$ — is covered by vertical laps one effector
width apart, stepping over along the floor or ceiling between laps. That is the boustrophedon
("ox-turning") pattern of a plough, and it is why every decomposition in this chapter is built by
sweeping a vertical line.

The chapter's micro-world — the **bench** — is a convex quadrilateral plot
$B = (0, 0.2), (10, 0), (10.4, 6), (0.3, 6.2)$ with a triangle $T = (2,2), (4,1.5), (3,4)$ and a
quadrilateral $K = (6,1), (8,0.8), (8.6,4), (6.5,4.4)$; its eleven vertex abscissae are distinct.
A vertical line sweeps it from left to right and stops only at vertices. Between stops nothing can
happen: the edges the line crosses stay in the same vertical order, and the free intervals between
them only stretch or shrink. The widget's strip is that ordered list, $L$. At a stop, the two edges
meeting at the vertex are inserted into $L$, deleted from it, or one replaces the other — Choset's
four event types — and the vertex shoots a **vertical extension** up to the next edge of $L$ above
it and down to the next below, *if free space lies there*.

Watch the totals. The eleven vertices do not produce twenty-two extensions; they produce thirteen,
because most vertices have obstacle on one side. The pass-through vertices — $(0.3, 6.2)$ on the
plot's upper chain, $T$'s top $(3,4)$, $K$'s $(6.5, 4.4)$, $(8, 0.8)$, and the plot's corner
$(10, 0)$ — each get one. The leftmost and rightmost vertices of $T$ and $K$ get two. The plot's
own leftmost and rightmost vertices get none. Cells: twelve trapezoids (some degenerate into
triangles), and the adjacency graph has thirteen edges, one per extension.

### Fewer walls: the boustrophedon decomposition

Now keep only the extensions that go *both* up and down. There are four — at the leftmost and
rightmost points of $T$ and of $K$ — and with the slice's birth at $(0, 0.2)$ and death at
$(10.4, 6)$ they are the six places where the free part of the sweep line changes its number of

the trapezoids that the other extensions separated and you have seven cells. The Reeb graph docked
beside the canvas is the dual picture: a node per critical point, an edge per cell. Six nodes,
seven edges, two holes:

$$
N_{ce} = N_{cp} + N_{ob} - 1, \qquad 7 = 6 + 2 - 1,
$$

and the widget re-prints it live while you drag any vertex — Euler's count is a theorem, not a
coincidence of this world.

Then Rusty mows. With a $0.5$ m effector, the first cell (from $x = 0$ to the triangle's tip at
$x = 2$) takes $\lceil 2/0.5 \rceil = 4$ laps. From a start at $(1.5, 3)$ the boustrophedon mower
finishes the bench in $158.4$ m; switch to the trapezoidal decomposition and the same mower needs
$174.4$ m — one more lap and $14$ m more of transits, because twelve cells mean twelve entries and
twelve exits. In the living room the gap is wider: $45.9$ m against $79.1$ m, because the
octagonal corners of Rusty's C-obstacles shatter the room into 29 trapezoids. **More cells is not
finer coverage; it is more turnarounds.**

### Six slices, one definition

Nothing in the boustrophedon decomposition depends on the slices being vertical lines. Sweep
circles out from a centre, or diamonds, or rays, or the fronts of a brushfire, and the same rule —
a cell wall wherever the swept slice splits or merges — gives a different decomposition and a
different pattern of laps (Choset Figs. 6.12–6.18). The gallery computes all six on the bench by
one raster algorithm.

Two things in the gallery are worth a second look. The spiral's circles touch the outer wall many

### Cell decompositions by sweeping

<KeyIdea title="Definition — the trapezoidal decomposition (Choset §6.1)">
Let $\Qfree$ be bounded by a polygon, with polygonal holes, in **general position**: no two
vertices share an $x$-coordinate. At every vertex $v$ draw the **upper vertical extension** from
$v$ up to the first edge above it and the **lower vertical extension** down to the first edge
below — each only if it lies in $\Qfree$. The extensions cut $\Qfree$ into trapezoids (some with a
zero-length side: triangles). Two trapezoids are adjacent iff they share an extension.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The trapezoidal sweep: four event types, O(n log n)" result="L changes only at vertices; each event is an insertion, a deletion or a replacement next to e_LOWER, e_UPPER">
**Statement** (Choset §6.1). Sort the $n$ vertices by $x$. Sweep a vertical line through them
keeping the list $L$ of edges it crosses, ordered by height. With $L$ in a balanced tree, each
event updates $L$ and finds $e_{LOWER}, e_{UPPER}$ in $O(\log n)$, so the whole decomposition costs
$O(n \log n)$ — against the naive $O(n^2)$ of intersecting a vertical line through each vertex
with every edge.

**Step 1 — nothing happens between vertices.** Edges do not cross (the polygons are simple and
disjoint), so two edges both crossing the line keep their vertical order until one of them ends,
which happens at a vertex. Between events $L$ is frozen.

**Step 2 — at an event, only the two edges at $v$ change.** Let $e_{lower}, e_{upper}$ be the
edges containing $v$, labelled so that the other end of $e_{lower}$ is lower. Four cases (Choset
Fig. 6.4): both to the right of the line — insert both between $e_{LOWER}$ and $e_{UPPER}$; both
to the left — delete both; $e_{lower}$ left, $e_{upper}$ right — $e_{upper}$ takes $e_{lower}$'s
slot; $e_{lower}$ right, $e_{upper}$ left — $e_{lower}$ takes $e_{upper}$'s slot. Every update
happens *at the position of $v$ in $L$*, so finding $e_{LOWER}$ and $e_{UPPER}$ is one search.

**Step 3 — the extensions.** The extension from $v$ upward ends on $e_{UPPER}$ at height
$e_{UPPER}(v_x)$, the downward one on $e_{LOWER}$; each exists exactly when the direction
$\pm\hat y$ at $v$ points into free space, which the angular sector of free space at $v$ decides in
$O(1)$.

**Step 4 — the count.** $O(n \log n)$ to sort, $n$ events at $O(\log n)$ each. $\blacksquare$

*The bench's trace.* Number the edges $e_0, \dots, e_3$ around the plot, $e_4, e_5, e_6$ around
$T$ and $e_7, \dots, e_{10}$ around $K$, each counter-clockwise from the first listed vertex. The
sweep's list is

$$
\emptyset \to \{e_0, e_3\} \to \{e_0, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_4, e_6, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_4, e_5, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_7, e_{10}, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_7, e_9, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_8, e_9, e_2\} \to \{e_0, e_2\} \to \{e_1, e_2\} \to \emptyset,
$$

the same shape as Choset's $L: \emptyset \to \{e_8, e_{13}\} \to \{e_8, e_0, e_3, e_{13}\} \to \dots$
for his Fig. 6.1. The check re-sorts the crossed edges by brute force between every pair of events
and finds no disagreement. *Two honesty items.* The TypeScript port keeps $L$ as a sorted array —
$O(n)$ per update, which changes the constant and not the idea. And general position is an
assumption: the Apartment's rectilinear walls violate it on every wall, so the port shears
rectilinear worlds by $x \leftarrow x + \sigma y$ with $\sigma = \sqrt 2 \cdot 10^{-3}$, which
keeps every edge straight and every polygon simple, moves nothing by more than six millimetres in
these rooms, and turns each vertical wall's two corners into one critical point and one
pass-through. *Point location* (C §6.1, end): vertical lines through all events cut the plane into
slabs; a binary search finds a query's slab and a second search its floor and ceiling, $O(\log n)$
per query [C ref. 124].
</Derivation>

How many trapezoids? Each event opens new cells: a vertex with extensions both ways (a **split**)
closes one cell and opens two; a vertex where two edges leave and free space lies *between* them
(a **birth**) opens one; a pass-through closes one and opens one; a vertex where two edges arrive
with obstacle between (a **merge**) closes two and opens one; a vertex where two edges arrive with
free space between (a **death**) only closes. Counting cells opened per vertex gives

$$
\#\text{trapezoids} = V + \#\text{split} - \#\text{death}.
$$

For a convex $m$-gon boundary with convex $k_i$-gon holes, each hole has one split and no death
and the boundary has one death and no split, so the count is $(m - 1) + \sum_i (k_i + 1)$ — on the
bench, $3 + 4 + 5 = 12$. The surprise is that the closed form survives non-convexity:
$\#\text{split} - \#\text{death} = N_{ob} - 1$ for *every* polygonal free space in general position
(Exercise 1 proves it from Euler's count), so

$$
\#\text{trapezoids} = V + N_{ob} - 1, \qquad \#\text{adjacencies} = V + 2N_{ob} - 2,
$$

which the check confirms on 200 convex and 100 star-shaped seeded worlds — 69 of the latter have a
notch tip that opens or closes a slice — and which gives the living room's $28 + 2 - 1 = 29$ and
the Workbench top's $16 + 3 - 1 = 18$.

<Derivation title="Midpoint–centroid paths are collision-free" result="a path in the adjacency graph ⇒ a polyline in Q_free">
**Statement** (Choset §6.1). Let $c_0, c_1, \dots, c_k$ be a path in the adjacency graph from the
cell containing $\qstart$ to the cell containing $\qgoal$. The polyline $\qstart$ → midpoint of
the extension $c_0|c_1$ → centroid of $c_1$ → midpoint of $c_1|c_2$ → … → midpoint of
$c_{k-1}|c_k$ → $\qgoal$ lies in $\Qfree$.

**Step 1 — trapezoids are convex.** A trapezoid is the region between two non-crossing segments
over a common $x$-interval: an intersection of four half-planes.

**Step 2 — each piece joins two points of one cell.** $\qstart$ and the first midpoint lie in
$c_0$ (the midpoint is on its right or left wall); the midpoint of $c_{i-1}|c_i$ and the centroid
of $c_i$ lie in $c_i$; and so on. By convexity each segment stays in its cell.

**Step 3 — consecutive pieces meet on shared walls**, which are in $\Qfree$. $\blacksquare$

*In practice* the adjacency graph is a Chapter 6 `Graph`, costed by the length of each
centroid–midpoint–centroid hop, and A\* with the centroid-to-centroid straight line as heuristic
(admissible by the triangle inequality) finds the route; the check plans 120 random queries over
the bench, the living room and the Workbench top and samples every path at 1 cm — all inside
$\Qfree$. The argument fails for the non-convex cells of the next section, which is why the coverage
code routes its *transits* between boustrophedon cells through the trapezoids, which refine them.
</Derivation>

### Morse decompositions

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — slices, critical points, Morse decomposition (Choset §6.2.2, Def. 6.2.1 [C ref. 12])">
Let $h : \Q \to \mathbb{R}$ be a **slice function** and $Q_\lambda = h^{-1}(\lambda)$ a **slice**;
$Q_{free\,\lambda} = Q_\lambda \cap \Qfree$ is its free part, whose connected components
$Q^j_{free\,\lambda}$ are the **slice intervals**. A **critical point** is a point of
$\partial\Qfree$ where $Q_{free\,\lambda}$ changes connectivity as $\lambda$ passes $h$ of it — for
$h = x$, a vertex with extensions both up and down, or the slice's birth or death. Let $I^\star$
be the set of slice intervals containing a critical point. A **Morse decomposition** is the exact
cell decomposition whose cells are the connected components of $\Qfree \setminus I^\star$; the
**boustrophedon decomposition** [C ref. 110] is the case $h(x, y) = x$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Between critical slices the free slice keeps its topology" result="Q_free,λ₁ ≅ Q_free,λ₂ when no critical value lies between λ₁ and λ₂">
**Statement** (Choset §6.2.2; the Morse box of [Chapter 8](/chapters/ch08-roadmaps-i); Lemma 5.5.2
of [Chapter 9](/chapters/ch09-roadmaps-ii)). If $h$ restricted to $\partial\Qfree$ is Morse,
then for $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2$ with no critical value between them a diffeomorphism carries
$Q_{free\,\lambda_1}$ onto $Q_{free\,\lambda_2}$: slice intervals stretch or shrink, but never
split, merge, appear or vanish. Hence each cell is swept by a *single* interval that varies
continuously — exactly what a lawnmower needs.

**Step 1 — connectivity changes only at tangency.** An interval's endpoints lie on
$\partial\Qfree$. Moving $\lambda$, an endpoint slides along the boundary as long as the slice
crosses the boundary transversally; intervals can only be created, destroyed, cut or joined where
the slice is *tangent* to $\partial\Qfree$.

**Step 2 — tangency is a rank condition.** With $\partial\Qfree = m^{-1}(0)$, the slice is tangent
at $x$ iff $\nabla h(x) \parallel \nabla m(x)$, i.e. the stacked differential $D(h, m)(x)$ loses
rank — Lemma 5.5.2.

**Step 3 — those are the critical points of $h|_{\partial\Qfree}$.** On the one-dimensional
boundary, rank loss of $D(h, m)$ means the derivative of $h$ along the boundary vanishes.

**Step 4 — Morse theory between critical values.** For a Morse function the topology of the level
sets changes only across critical values [C ref. 315] — the Chapter 8 box applied to
$h|_{\partial\Qfree}$, with the interior of each slice interval carried along. $\blacksquare$

*Consequences.* A critical point lies at an *endpoint* of a slice interval, never inside one —
inside, the slice meets free space on both sides and is not tangent to anything (Exercise 2). *Not
every tangency is critical.* A circle centred in free space first touches the outer wall from
inside: a closed loop becomes an arc, one piece before and one after. That is a topology change of
the slice but not a *connectivity* change, and Definition 6.2.1 counts connectivity — which is why
the spiral panel of the gallery has fewer critical points than its circles have tangencies.
*Nonsmooth slices* — $|x| + |y|$, the distance $D$ — use Chapter 9's generalized gradient: for the
squarel a corner of the diamond is critical when the obstacle's normal lies in the convex hull of
the two flat sides meeting there (C §6.2.3). *Degenerate critical points* — a vertical wall seen by
$h = x$ — are what rectilinear rooms are made of; Butler et al. [C ref. 86] cover them directly,
and this book shears them away offline and merges them online (Derivation 4).
</Derivation>

### Sensing a critical point

Choset's observation (§6.2.4) is that a critical point can be *felt*. A robot following an
obstacle boundary at standoff $\delta$ knows the direction to the nearest obstacle point — its
range ring's shortest ray — and that direction is $-\nabla D$, the negative of the distance
gradient ([Chapter 7](/chapters/ch07-potential-functions), Derivation 2). The boundary normal is
$\nabla m \parallel \nabla D$ there. So the rank condition of Derivation 3 becomes one angle
comparison.

<Derivation title="Critical points are sensed as 'shortest ray parallel to the sweep'" result="for h = x: the range ring's minimum ray points straight ahead or straight behind">
**Statement.** While boundary-following, the robot is at a critical point of $h$ exactly when its
range ring's global minimum ray points along $\pm\nabla h$ — for $h = x$, along $\pm\hat x$.

**Step 1 — the ray is the normal.** The nearest obstacle point $c$ satisfies
$x - c = D(x)\,\nabla D(x)$, and $\nabla D(x)$ is the outward normal of the boundary at $c$ (the
offset curve's normal too).

**Step 2 — rank loss is parallelism.** $D(h, m)$ loses rank iff $\nabla h \parallel \nabla m$
(Lemma 5.5.2), i.e. iff the normal at $c$ is horizontal when $h = x$.

**Step 3 — one comparison.** The minimum ray is $-\nabla D$, so the test is whether its bearing is
$0$ or $\pi$. Equivalently: the follower's tangent is vertical, so its $x$-velocity changes sign —
the boundary *turns back* in the sweep direction. $\blacksquare$

*Classification from the same ray.* Moving $+x$, a turn-back with the shortest ray pointing *back*
(obstacle behind, $\nabla D = +\hat x$) is a convex extremity bulging into the robot's path — a
**merge**; with the ray pointing ahead it is a corner that pinches the slice off — a **death**.
Moving $-x$ the same two cases are a **split** and a **birth**. *The port's sensing* (`online.rs`)
watches the follower's $x$-progress and declares an event once it has fallen back by $0.3\delta$
after a maximum; on a convex extremity it then steps along the arc of radius $\delta$ to the point
level with the obstacle vertex, where the minimum ray is exactly horizontal, and re-reads the ring.
The check finds the minimum ray within $0.01°$ of $\pm\hat x$ at every split and merge on the bench,
and within a tenth of a degree — the general-position shear — in the sheared living room. *Degenerate critical
points* are where Choset's sensing fails: along an axis-aligned wall the shortest ray is horizontal
for the wall's whole length. The port's **merged-event rule** — beyond Choset — reports the
stretch where the maximum $x$ was attained once, at its middle, so a vertical wall's two corner
critical points become one event. *Why a raster scan misses them* (C Fig. 6.22): the test fires only
on the boundary being followed, and a raster follows the ceiling only on every other gap.
</Derivation>

### The Reeb graph and Euler's count

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — Reeb graph, closing critical point (Choset §6.2.4 [C ref. 154])">
The **Reeb graph** of a Morse decomposition is its dual: a node per critical point, an edge per
cell, joining the critical point where the cell opens to the one where it closes. It is connected
and planar. While a robot covers a cell it starts from one of the cell's defining critical points;
the other one — whose discovery completes the cell — is the cell's **closing critical point**.
</KeyIdea>

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-graph}{N_{ce}} = \htmlClass{term-graph}{N_{cp}} + \htmlClass{term-obstacle}{N_{ob}} - 1
\qquad\Longleftarrow\qquad
\htmlClass{term-graph}{v} - \htmlClass{term-graph}{e} + f = 2,\quad v = N_{cp},\; e = N_{ce},\; f = \htmlClass{term-obstacle}{N_{ob}} + 1.
$$
</LinkedMath>

<Derivation title="Euler's count: N_ce = N_cp + N_ob − 1" result="cells = critical points + obstacles − 1 (boustrophedon); a tree for brushfire">
**Statement** (Choset §6.2.5). For a boustrophedon decomposition of a bounded planar free space,
$N_{ce} = N_{cp} + N_{ob} - 1$, where the outer boundary's leftmost and rightmost points count as
critical points (the slice's birth and death).

**Step 1 — the Reeb graph is connected and planar**, with $v = N_{cp}$ and $e = N_{ce}$: draw each
node at its critical point and each edge through its cell.

**Step 2 — its faces are the obstacles plus the outside.** Each hole is encircled by the cells that
pass above and below it, so it is a face; the unbounded face is the outside. $f = N_{ob} + 1$.

**Step 3 — Euler's formula** for a connected plane graph [C ref. 57]: $v - e + f = 2$.

**Step 4 — rearrange:** $N_{cp} - N_{ce} + N_{ob} + 1 = 2$. $\blacksquare$

*Checks.* Choset Fig. 6.25: $21 + 2 - 1 = 22$ cells. The bench: $6 + 2 - 1 = 7$, with Reeb nodes at
$x = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.6, 10.4$. The check runs the sweep on 200 convex and 100 star-shaped seeded
worlds and the identity never fails; cells grow *linearly* in the critical points discovered. *The
same fact from the Morse side.* Chapter 8's box counts critical points with signs: for a manifold
with boundary and $h = x$, $\chi(\Qfree)$ is the sum of $(-1)^{\mathrm{index}}$ over the boundary
critical points where $\nabla h$ points *into* $\Qfree$ — births (index 0) and merges (index 1).
On the bench that is $1 - 2 = -1 = 1 - N_{ob}$, and the check computes it with Chapter 8's
`eulerCharacteristicFromCriticalPoints`. *Where the count changes form.* For the brushfire slice
$h = D$ every obstacle boundary is a whole critical set at $D = 0$ — the fronts start *around* each
obstacle rather than passing on both sides of it — so the obstacles are nodes, not faces, and the
Reeb graph is a tree: $N_{ce} = N_{cp} - 1$ with each obstacle counted once among the critical
points. The gallery's brushfire panel (6 critical points, 5 cells) is exactly that.
</Derivation>

### The complexity of coverage

<Derivation title="Coverage path length is linear in area, critical points and perimeter" result="length < Δ²/2r + 4ΔN_ce + 5P_total = Δ²/2r + 4Δ(N_cp + N_ob) + 5P_total − 4Δ">
**Statement** (Choset §6.2.5). The sensor-based boustrophedon coverage path is shorter than
$\Delta^2/(2r) + 4\Delta N_{ce} + 5 P_{total}$, where $\Delta$ is the diameter of the smallest disk
containing the space and $P_{total}$ the perimeter of the obstacles and the outer boundary.

**Step 1 — laps.** Each lap is at most $\Delta$ long. At spacing $2r$ the laps across the space
need about $\lceil \Delta/2r \rceil$ slice positions, plus one extra first lap per cell (C Fig.
6.26): lap length $\le \Delta\lceil \Delta/2r \rceil + \Delta N_{ce}$, which is at most
$\Delta^2/2r + \Delta N_{ce}$ up to the rounding Choset drops.

**Step 2 — boundary following.** The cycle algorithm follows the whole floor and ceiling of each
cell, and undoing a reverse follow retraces part of it: between $1.5 P_{cell}$ and $2 P_{cell}$ per
cell (C Fig. 6.27), less than $2 P_{total}$ in all.

**Step 3 — backtracking** to the closing critical point of a cell with uncovered neighbours: at
most $P_{cell} + \Delta$ each, $P_{total} + \Delta N_{ce}$ in all (C Fig. 6.28).

**Step 4 — discovery and traversal.** Discovering a cell's defining critical point costs at most
$P_{cell}$ more ($P_{total}$ in all); a depth-first search over the Reeb graph traverses each
covered cell at most once on the way to an uncovered one [C ref. 118], at most $P_{cell} + 2\Delta$
each — $P_{total} + 2 N_{ce}\Delta$ in all (C Figs. 6.29–6.30).

**Step 5 — sum:** $\Delta^2/2r + \Delta N_{ce} + 2P_{total} + P_{total} + \Delta N_{ce} + P_{total} +
P_{total} + 2N_{ce}\Delta = \Delta^2/2r + 4\Delta N_{ce} + 5P_{total}$, and Euler's count turns
$N_{ce}$ into $N_{cp} + N_{ob} - 1$. $\blacksquare$

*How loose is it?* The bench has $\Delta = 11.908$ m and $P_{total} = 49.955$ m; at $2r = 0.5$ and
$N_{ce} = 7$ the bound is $866.8$ m. The offline mower needs $158.4$ m and the online cycle
algorithm $272.4$ m. In the living room ($\Delta = 4.899$, $P_{total} = 23.434$, $2r = 0.44$) the
bound is $308.9$ m against $45.9$ m offline and $85.7$ m online. The bound is a guarantee of
*linearity*, not an estimate. *One caution the port taught us:* the lap *count*
$\lceil \Delta/2r \rceil + N_{ce}$ is not a bound when cells are stacked — the bench's mower uses 32
laps where the formula allows 31, because the cells above and below each obstacle each need their
own laps at the same abscissae. What the argument needs, and what holds, is the lap *length*:
stacked laps share one slice, whose total length is at most $\Delta$.
</Derivation>

How much can a lawnmower *miss*? Inside a cell with floor $f(x)$ and ceiling $c(x)$, the laps sit
at most $r$ apart horizontally from every point, so a point $(x, y)$ escapes its nearest lap at
$x_j$ only if $y < f(x_j)$ or $y > c(x_j)$ — a sliver under a dip of the floor or over a bump of the
ceiling, of height at most the variation of $f$ or $c$ between $x_j$ and $x$. Integrating over the
half-gaps gives a bound that is *not* in Choset, and that the check holds the mower to:

$$
\text{missed area} \;\le\; r \sum_{\text{cells}} \big( \mathrm{TV}(f) + \mathrm{TV}(c) \big) \;\le\; r\, P_{total},
$$

with $\mathrm{TV}$ the total vertical variation of a chain. On the bench it is $6.150$ m², and the
mower misses $0.67$ m² (98.7 % covered on a 3 cm raster) — all of it under the slanted edges of
the plot and of $T$ and $K$. In the rectilinear living room the bound is $2.350$ m² and the mower
misses almost nothing ($99.87$ %, the octagonal corners of the furniture). The online coverer,
which follows *both* floor and ceiling of every gap, covers $100.0$ % in all three worlds.

### Coda: decomposing by information — pursuit and evasion

The last decomposition in Choset Chapter 6 is carved by visibility, not by a sweep, and it is a
workspace problem: the pursuers and the evader are points in $\W_{free}$, not configurations of a
robot (C Problem 7).

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — visibility polygon, gap edges, information state (Choset §6.3, after Suzuki & Yamashita [C ref. 403] and LaValle, Guibas et al. [C refs. 172, 273])">
The **visibility polygon** $V(x) \subset \W_{free}$ is the star-shaped set of points in line of
sight of $x$. An evader $e(t)$ is caught at the first $t$ with $e(t) \in V(\gamma_i(t))$ for some
pursuer path $\gamma_i$. A region that may contain the evader is **contaminated**, otherwise
**clear**; cleared and then contaminated again is **recontaminated**. Edges of $V(x)$ that lie in
free space are **gap edges**, labelled 0 if they bound a clear region and 1 if contaminated; $B(x)$
is the vector of labels and $(x, B(x))$ the **information state**. A connected set on which the gap
structure — hence $B(x)$ — cannot change is **conservative**; conservative regions form an exact
cell decomposition. $H(\W_{free})$ is the least number of pursuers that guarantees capture.
</KeyIdea>

Crossing from one conservative region to another changes the gaps in one of four ways, and the
labels follow (Choset §6.3): a gap edge **disappears** — do nothing; a gap edge **appears** —
label it 0, since what it now hides was just visible; gap edges **merge** — the new label is the OR
of the old; a gap edge **splits** — the pieces inherit its label. The **information graph** $G_i$
has a node per (conservative region, label vector) and an edge per transition the rules allow; a
pursuer strategy is a path in $G_i$ to any node whose labels are all zero.

<Derivation title="How many pursuers: O(log n), and O(√h + log n) with holes" result="H(W_free) ≤ k + 1 when a chord held by one static pursuer splits W_free into parts needing k">
**Statement** (Choset §6.3). A simply connected polygonal $\W_{free}$ with $n$ edges can be cleared
by $O(\log n)$ pursuers; with $h$ holes, by $O(\sqrt h + \log n)$.

**Step 1 — a static pursuer splits the problem.** If a chord between two boundary vertices splits
$\W_{free}$ into $\W_1$ and $\W_2$ with $H(\W_1), H(\W_2) \le k$, then $H(\W_{free}) \le k + 1$:
park one "static pursuer" on the chord and clear $\W_1$, then $\W_2$, with the same $k$.

**Step 2 — balanced chords give logarithmic depth.** A simple polygon has a chord leaving at least a
third of the edges on each side, so recursive splitting reaches triangles (clearable by one
pursuer) after $O(\log n)$ levels, each holding one static pursuer.

**Step 3 — holes.** Triangulate; remove the $O(h)$ trichromatic triangles (those touching three
boundary components), which leaves simply connected pieces; the dual graph of the trichromatic
triangles is planar, so the planar separator theorem splits it with $O(\sqrt h)$ static pursuers.

**Step 4 — recurse:** $O(\sqrt h + \sqrt{2h/3} + \dots) = O(\sqrt h)$ static pursuers, plus
$O(\log n)$ for the simply connected pieces. $\blacksquare$

*The U-worlds of C Fig. 6.40* make the logarithm tight: a 12-edge U needs one pursuer, three U's
joined at a hub (36 edges) need two, and so on by threes. The coda's worlds are miniatures of that
construction — a square hub with two or three U-shaped pockets (20 and 28 edges) — and the widget
below runs the information-graph search on them.
</Derivation>

The port computes $V(x)$ by an angular sweep — one exact ray through every vertex and one a hair to

pixel connected to a contaminated one, because the evader is arbitrarily fast. The four transition
rules are not coded; they *emerge* from the flood, and the gap labels are read off it. The
conservative regions are computed by grouping pixels by which polygon vertices they cannot see (plus,
with a static pursuer, the corners of its view) — a refinement of Choset's regions, since $B(x)$
cannot change while that set is fixed — and labels are
carried across a region boundary by overlap: a shadow on the far side is contaminated iff it
overlaps a contaminated shadow on the near side. Breadth-first search on the resulting $G_i$ finds
a strategy for the two-pocket world after 286 information states, and the replay through the flood
leaves nothing contaminated. In the three-pocket world it exhausts all 693 reachable states without
an all-zero node — one pursuer *cannot* do it — while a second pursuer parked at the hub (the static
pursuer of Step 1) brings a solution after 2 480 states, which the replay confirms. The naive patrol
the widget shows instead — visit each pocket's far leg in turn — leaves 10.25 m² contaminated: every
pocket it clears is recontaminated through the hub while it clears the next.

## The algorithm

<Algorithm
  name="TRAPEZOIDAL SWEEP  —  Choset §6.1, Fig. 6.4"
  inputs="a polygonal free space in general position (boundary and holes)"
  outputs="trapezoids, the adjacency graph with one edge per vertical extension"
  complexity="O(n log n) with L a balanced tree (the port: a sorted array, O(n) per update)"
>
1. sort the vertices by $x$; $L \leftarrow \emptyset$; open cells keyed by their floor edge
2. **for** each vertex $v$ in order **do**
3. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;$e_{lower}, e_{upper}$ ← the edges at $v$; $e_{LOWER}, e_{UPPER}$ ← the edges of $L$ just below and above $v$
4. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** both edges go right: insert both; **if** free above and below — *split*: close the cell between $e_{LOWER}$ and $e_{UPPER}$, open two — **else** *birth*: open one between them
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**if** both edges go left: delete both; **if** free above and below — *merge*: close two, open one — **else** *death*: close the cell between them
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**else** replace the leaving edge by the arriving one; close the cell whose floor or ceiling it was and open its continuation
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;draw the extensions from $v$ to $e_{UPPER}$ and $e_{LOWER}$ that lie in free space; each joins the closed cell to the opened one
8. **end for**

*Boustrophedon* is the same loop with one change: at a pass-through (step 6) the cell is not closed
— the vertex is appended to its floor or ceiling chain — so walls appear only at births, splits,
merges and deaths, the critical points of $h = x$.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="BOUSTROPHEDON COVERAGE (OFFLINE)  —  Choset §6.2.1, Fig. 6.10"
  inputs="a boustrophedon decomposition, effector width 2r, a start point"
  outputs="a coverage path: laps, boundary steps, transits"
  complexity="O(V + E) for the walk; one A* per transit; O(laps) per cell"
>
1. exhaustive walk: depth-first search of the adjacency graph from the start cell, with backtracking — on the bench $0-1-3-2-3-4-6-5-6-4-3-1-0$; cover cells in first-visit order
2. **for** each cell $c = [x_l, x_r]$ in that order **do**
3. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;laps at $\lceil (x_r - x_l)/2r \rceil$ abscissae, the outer ones $r$ inside the ends, the rest evenly spaced
4. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;enter at the corner nearest the robot; transit there by A\* on the *trapezoids* with midpoint–centroid chaining
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;lap floor → ceiling, step along the ceiling to the next lap, lap ceiling → floor, step along the floor, …
6. **end for**
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="CYCLE ALGORITHM (SENSOR-BASED COVERAGE)  —  Choset §6.2.4, Fig. 6.23 [C ref. 11]"
  inputs="a robot with a contact sensor, a boundary follower at standoff δ, a range ring; effector width 2r"
  outputs="a coverage path and the Reeb graph, built incrementally"
  complexity="online; path length within the bound of Derivation 6"
>
1. **seek**: move −x to contact, follow the boundary −x until a turn-back — the first critical point; push it on the DFS stack
2. **while** some node on the stack has an unexplored side **do**
3. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;travel to it over covered ground (A\* on the robot's own trail plus range-ring line-of-sight shortcuts); step $r - \delta$ into the new cell
4. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**repeat** for each gap between laps $x_k$ and $x_{k+1} = x_k + 2r$:
5. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*forward*: follow the boundary the last lap ended on, forward, one lap width (stop at a turn-back event)
6. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;lap to the other boundary; *reverse*: follow it backward one lap width (stop at an event)
7. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*closing*: if that did not return to the previous lap's far end, lap toward it and follow forward to the critical point that explains why
8. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*undo*: retrace the reverse follow; **until** an event closes the cell
9. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;classify the event from the range ring (split, merge, birth, death); match it to a known node or add one; mark the arriving side explored; for a split or merge, drive one full lap through it (the closing lap)
10. **end while**

A *raster scan* is steps 5 and the first half of 6 alone — lap, follow forward, lap, follow
forward on the other side — which is why it never follows a ceiling backward and never sees a split.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="INFORMATION-GRAPH SEARCH  —  Choset §6.3, Figs. 6.38–6.39"
  inputs="a polygonal W_free, a pursuer start (optionally a static second pursuer)"
  outputs="a pursuer path whose information state reaches all zeros, or proof that none exists"
  complexity="BFS over (region, labels): O(regions · 2^gaps) nodes in the worst case"
>
1. conservative regions: group free points by the set of vertices they cannot see; regions are the connected components
2. start node: the start's region, every label 1 (everything unseen is contaminated)
3. **for** each crossing $A \to B$: compute the shadows (hidden components) just inside $A$ and just inside $B$; a shadow in $B$ is contaminated iff it overlaps a contaminated shadow in $A$ — rules 1–4 of §6.3
4. breadth-first search to any node with all labels 0; replay the region path through the contamination flood to confirm
</Algorithm>

## Implementation in Rust

The `cells` crate is introduced here. Its spine is a decomposition — cells with floor and ceiling
chains, and the adjacency graph as a `petgraph` graph whose edges carry the shared wall — and every
algorithm above produces or consumes one.

```rust title="crates/cells/src/lib.rs"
use nalgebra::Point2;
use petgraph::graph::UnGraph;

/// A planar polygonal free space: Choset §6.1's standing assumption. For disc-Rusty the
/// polygons are Chapter 4 C-obstacles; for Reach's pad they are the Workbench's objects.
#[derive(Clone, Debug)]
pub struct PolyWorld {
    pub boundary: Vec<Point2<f64>>,       // counter-clockwise
    pub obstacles: Vec<Vec<Point2<f64>>>, // holes, counter-clockwise, disjoint
}

/// The wall two adjacent cells share: a vertical extension from an event vertex.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug)]
pub struct Boundary {
    pub segment: (Point2<f64>, Point2<f64>),
}

/// A cell: everything between a floor chain and a ceiling chain over an x-interval.
/// Trapezoids have two-point chains; boustrophedon cells bend at pass-through vertices.
#[derive(Clone, Debug)]
pub struct Cell {
    pub floor: Vec<Point2<f64>>,
    pub ceiling: Vec<Point2<f64>>,
    pub x_range: (f64, f64),
}

pub struct Decomposition {
    pub cells: Vec<Cell>,
    pub adjacency: UnGraph<usize, Boundary>,
}

impl Decomposition {
    /// Faces of the planar adjacency graph by Euler, f = 2 − V + E: the holes plus the outside.
    pub fn euler_faces(&self) -> i64 {
        2 - self.adjacency.node_count() as i64 + self.adjacency.edge_count() as i64
    }
}

impl PolyWorld {
    /// Choset assumes distinct vertex abscissae; a rectilinear room violates that on every wall.
    /// The shear x ← x + σy keeps edges straight and polygons simple, and moves nothing by more
    /// than σ·height — a few millimetres in a room of the Apartment.
    pub fn in_general_position(mut self) -> Self {
        const SIGMA: f64 = 1.0e-3 * std::f64::consts::SQRT_2;
        let mut xs: Vec<f64> = self.vertices().map(|p| p.x).collect();
        xs.sort_by(f64::total_cmp);
        if xs.windows(2).all(|w| w[1] - w[0] > 1e-7) {
            return self;
        }
        for p in self.vertices_mut() {
            p.x += SIGMA * p.y;
        }
        self
    }
}
```

The sweep keeps $L$ as a vector sorted by height at the current abscissa — the bookkeeping is
easier to read than a tree keyed by a moving comparator, and the event logic is the same. The
interesting part is the `match`, which is Choset Fig. 6.4.

```rust title="crates/cells/src/trapezoid.rs" {24-48}
use std::collections::HashMap;

#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum EventKind { BothRight, BothLeft, LowerLeftUpperRight, LowerRightUpperLeft }

#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum Rule { Trapezoidal, Boustrophedon }

pub fn sweep(world: &PolyWorld, rule: Rule) -> Decomposition {
    let edges = world.edges();
    let mut events: Vec<Vertex> = world.vertex_list();
    events.sort_by(|a, b| a.p.x.total_cmp(&b.p.x));
    assert!(events.windows(2).all(|w| w[1].p.x > w[0].p.x), "general position: distinct x");

    let mut l: Vec<usize> = Vec::new();                     // crossed edges, bottom to top
    let mut open: HashMap<usize, OpenCell> = HashMap::new(); // open cells keyed by floor edge
    let mut out = Builder::new(rule);

    for v in &events {
        let (e_prev, e_next) = world.incident_edges(v);
        let rest: Vec<usize> = l.iter().copied().filter(|&e| e != e_prev && e != e_next).collect();
        // e_LOWER and e_UPPER: one search at v's height, because every update happens there.
        let i = rest.partition_point(|&e| edges[e].y_at(v.p.x) < v.p.y);
        let (lo, up) = (i.checked_sub(1).map(|j| rest[j]), rest.get(i).copied());
        let (free_up, free_down) = world.free_vertical(v); // the extensions that exist
        match world.event_kind(v) {
            EventKind::BothRight => {
                let (a, b) = world.order_rightward(v, e_prev, e_next); // lower edge first
                l = [&rest[..i], &[a, b], &rest[i..]].concat();
                if free_up && free_down {
                    // Split: the slice interval (lo, up) is cut by the new obstacle.
                    let c = open.remove(&lo.unwrap()).unwrap();
                    out.close(c, v.p.x);
                    out.open_two(&mut open, (lo.unwrap(), a), (b, up.unwrap()), v, true);
                } else {
                    out.open(&mut open, a, b, v); // birth: free space starts between a and b
                }
            }
            EventKind::BothLeft => {
                let (a, b) = world.order_leftward(v, e_prev, e_next);
                l = rest;
                if free_up && free_down {
                    let (below, above) = (open.remove(&lo.unwrap()).unwrap(), open.remove(&b).unwrap());
                    out.merge(below, above, &mut open, lo.unwrap(), up.unwrap(), v); // merge
                } else {
                    out.close(open.remove(&a).unwrap(), v.p.x); // death
                }
            }
            _ => {
                // One edge leaves the line, the other arrives in its slot.
                let (leaving, arriving) = world.left_right(v, e_prev, e_next);
                for e in l.iter_mut() {
                    if *e == leaving { *e = arriving; }
                }
                let floor_side = free_up; // v lies on the floor of the cell above it
                out.pass_through(&mut open, v, leaving, arriving, floor_side, lo, rule);
            }
        }
    }
    out.finish()
}
```

The Morse layer adds the slice function and the Reeb graph. For $h = x$ the boustrophedon
decomposition *is* the sweep with `Rule::Boustrophedon`; the trait is there so that the variable
slices of §6.2.3 share one interface (the raster construction behind the gallery implements it for
the curved ones).

```rust title="crates/cells/src/morse.rs · src/reeb.rs"
use nalgebra::{Point2, Vector2};
use petgraph::graph::UnGraph;

pub trait SliceFn {
    fn h(&self, q: Point2<f64>) -> f64;
    fn grad_h(&self, q: Point2<f64>) -> Vector2<f64>;
}
pub struct Vertical;
impl SliceFn for Vertical {
    fn h(&self, q: Point2<f64>) -> f64 { q.x }
    fn grad_h(&self, _: Point2<f64>) -> Vector2<f64> { Vector2::x() }
}

#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum Kind { Birth, Split, Merge, Death }

#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug)]
pub struct CriticalPoint { pub q: Point2<f64>, pub kind: Kind }

/// Nodes are critical points, edges are cells (the dual of the adjacency graph).
pub struct ReebGraph { pub graph: UnGraph<CriticalPoint, usize> }

impl ReebGraph {
    /// Euler's count for a boustrophedon decomposition: e = v + N_ob − 1.
    pub fn euler_check(&self, n_obstacles: usize) -> bool {
        self.graph.edge_count() + 1 == self.graph.node_count() + n_obstacles
    }

    /// χ(Q_free) from Chapter 8's Morse count with boundary: births (index 0) and merges
    /// (index 1) are the boundary critical points where ∇h points into the free space.
    pub fn morse_chi(&self) -> i64 {
        self.graph.node_weights().map(|c| match c.kind {
            Kind::Birth => 1,
            Kind::Merge => -1,
            Kind::Split | Kind::Death => 0,
        }).sum()
    }
}

pub fn boustrophedon(world: &PolyWorld) -> (Decomposition, ReebGraph) {
    let d = sweep(world, Rule::Boustrophedon);
    let reeb = ReebGraph::from_cells(&d); // one node per critical event, one edge per cell
    (d, reeb)
```

Coverage: the exhaustive walk is a DFS, the laps are a closed-form set of abscissae, and the
transits are Chapter 6's A\* on the trapezoids.

```rust title="crates/cells/src/coverage.rs" {7-15}
pub struct CoveragePath {
    pub points: Vec<Point2<f64>>,
    pub laps: usize,
    pub lap_len: f64, pub boundary_len: f64, pub transit_len: f64,
}

/// ⌈width/2r⌉ laps, the outer ones r inside the cell's ends: every point of the cell is
/// within r of a lap horizontally, which is what the missed-area bound needs.
pub fn lap_xs(x_l: f64, x_r: f64, two_r: f64) -> Vec<f64> {
    let n = ((x_r - x_l) / two_r - 1e-9).ceil().max(1.0) as usize;
    if n == 1 { return vec![0.5 * (x_l + x_r)]; }
    let r = 0.5 * two_r;
    let step = (x_r - x_l - two_r) / (n - 1) as f64;
    (0..n).map(|k| x_l + r + k as f64 * step).collect()
}

pub fn coverage_path(d: &Decomposition, world: &PolyWorld, two_r: f64, start: Point2<f64>) -> CoveragePath {
    let trapezoids = sweep(world, Rule::Trapezoidal); // convex: safe transits (Derivation 2)
    let order = dfs_first_visits(&d.adjacency, d.locate(start).unwrap_or(0));
    let mut path = PathBuilder::at(start);
    for c in order {
        let cell = &d.cells[c];
        let xs = lap_xs(cell.x_range.0, cell.x_range.1, two_r);
        let pattern = Lawnmower::new(cell, &xs).entered_nearest(path.here());
        path.transit(&trapezoids, pattern.first()); // A* + midpoint–centroid chaining
        path.extend(pattern);
    }
    path.finish()
}

/// Δ²/2r + 4ΔN_ce + 5P_total — Choset §6.2.5.
pub fn bound(world: &PolyWorld, two_r: f64, n_cells: usize) -> f64 {
    let delta = world.min_enclosing_circle().radius * 2.0;
    delta * delta / two_r + 4.0 * delta * n_cells as f64 + 5.0 * world.total_perimeter()
}
```

The online coverer is a state machine around Chapter 3's follower. Its `tick` advances one motion
primitive and returns the phase, so the widget and a test harness drive the same code.

```rust title="crates/cells/src/online.rs" {20-38}
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq, Eq)]
pub enum Phase { Forward, Reverse, Closing, Backtrack, Done }

pub struct Coverer<'r> {
    pub robot: &'r mut sim::Rusty,
    pub reeb: ReebGraph,
    pub two_r: f64,
    pub phase: Phase,
    gap_start: Point2<f64>,   // where the current gap's first lap began
    far_end: Point2<f64>,     // the previous lap's other end, T_k
    best_progress: f64,       // max x-progress along the current follow
}

impl Coverer<'_> {
    /// One step of the cycle algorithm. Critical points come from the range ring alone.
    pub fn tick(&mut self, scan: &bugs::RangeScan, dt: f64) -> Phase {
        let progress = self.sweep_dir() * (self.robot.pose().x - self.gap_start.x);
        self.best_progress = self.best_progress.max(progress);
        // Derivation 4: the boundary turned back ⇔ the tangent went vertical ⇔ the minimum
        // ray was parallel to the sweep. Classify by where that ray points.
        if self.following() && progress < self.best_progress - 0.3 * self.standoff() {
            let g = scan.gradient();                        // ∇D = −(minimum ray)
            let convex = g.x * self.moving_dir() > 0.3;
            let node = self.reeb.add_or_match(self.robot.position(), self.classify(convex));
            self.close_cell(node);
            self.phase = Phase::Backtrack;                  // DFS picks the next unexplored edge
            return self.phase;
        }
        self.phase = match self.phase {
            Phase::Forward if progress >= self.two_r => { self.lap_to_other_side(); Phase::Reverse }
            Phase::Reverse if progress <= 0.0 => {
                if (self.robot.position() - self.far_end).norm() > self.close_tol() {
                    self.lap_toward(self.far_end);          // an obstacle lies between: find why
                    Phase::Closing
                } else {
                    self.undo_reverse();                    // retrace, then the next gap
                    Phase::Forward
                }
            }
            Phase::Backtrack => self.travel_to_next_unexplored(),
            p => { self.robot.follow_boundary(dt); p }
        };
        self.phase
    }
}
```

Pursuit is short once visibility is exact: a polygon from rays, and a BFS whose transitions carry
labels by overlap.

```rust title="crates/cells/src/pursuit.rs"
pub fn visibility_polygon(world: &PolyWorld, x: Point2<f64>) -> Vec<Point2<f64>> {
    const EPS: f64 = 1e-5;
    let mut bearings: Vec<f64> = world.vertices()
        .flat_map(|v| { let a = (v.y - x.y).atan2(v.x - x.x); [a - EPS, a, a + EPS] })
        .collect();
    bearings.sort_by(f64::total_cmp);
    bearings.into_iter().map(|a| x + world.ray_cast(x, a) * Vector2::new(a.cos(), a.sin())).collect()
}

pub struct InfoState { pub cell: usize, pub labels: Vec<u8> }

/// BFS on G_i from (start region, all ones) to any all-zero node — Choset Fig. 6.39.
pub fn clear(world: &PolyWorld, start: Point2<f64>, static_pursuer: Option<Point2<f64>>) -> Option<Vec<usize>> {
    let regions = conservative_regions(world, static_pursuer);
    let s0 = InfoState { cell: regions.locate(start)?, labels: regions.all_contaminated(start) };
    bfs(s0, |s| regions.crossings(s.cell).map(|t| t.carry(&s.labels)), |s| s.labels.iter().all(|&b| b == 0))
}
```

## Putting it together: three free spaces, three mowers

The integration lab covers three free spaces three ways: **offline** (the map is known: sweep,
walk, mow), **online** with the cycle algorithm (the map is unknown: sense, mow, build the Reeb
graph), and online with a conventional **raster** scan. The living room is mowed by disc-Rusty
with its own diameter as effector; the Workbench top is wiped by Reach's end-effector pad, 20 cm
wide, around the block, the post and the shelf (reachability is ignored here — Chapter 4 maps it);
the bench is the §3 micro-world.

Three things to read off the table. *Knowing the map is worth a factor of 1.5 to 1.9 in length*:
the cycle algorithm follows both boundaries of every gap and retraces its reverse follows, drives a
closing lap through every split and merge, and travels between cells over ground it has already
covered rather than along the shortest route. *Not knowing it costs
nothing in coverage* — the cycle algorithm is the only method at 100 % everywhere, because it
follows both the floor and the ceiling of every gap, while the offline lawnmower misses the slivers
the missed-area bound allows. *A raster scan's failure is not a constant*: on the Workbench its
lucky phase finds the merges that matter and it misses 0.05 %; in the living room it misses a
fifth of the room. A method whose completeness depends on the phase of its laps is not complete.

Part II closes here, and its scorecard is worth stating. Every planner in Chapters 6–10 is
**complete** — it finds a path when one exists and says so when none does — and most are
**exact**: the visibility graph, the GVD, Canny's roadmap, the cell decompositions represent
$\Qfree$ without approximation. The price is dimension. A trapezoidal sweep is $O(n \log n)$ in
the plane; Canny's roadmap is singly exponential in the dimension of $\Q$; the decompositions of
this chapter have no practical analogue for Reach's six-joint cousins. [Chapter 11](/chapters/ch11-prm)
gives the guarantee up for a probabilistic one and gets the dimension back: it will measure its
roadmaps against exactly the kind of decomposition built here, on spaces small enough for both.
[Chapter 14](/chapters/ch14-many-bodies) reuses the adjacency graph as the discrete layer of task
planning, and the sister book's [exploration chapter](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch24-exploration)
replaces the coverage of a *known-to-be-there* space with the coverage of an unknown one under a
belief — frontiers instead of critical points.
