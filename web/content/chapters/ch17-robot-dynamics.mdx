Parts I through IV planned *paths*: curves in $\Qfree$ with no clock attached. A real motor does
not execute a curve. It applies a torque, and the configuration that results depends on masses,
lever arms, and how fast everything else is already moving. This chapter builds the model that turns
geometry into motion for the rest of Part V.

The idea the chapter unpacks fits in one sentence. **The entire dynamics of an arm is encoded in one
configuration-dependent symmetric matrix $M(q)$.** Kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 \dot q\T M(q)\dot q$;
the Coriolis and centrifugal forces are nothing but derivatives of $M$ — the Christoffel symbols;
gravity is the gradient of a scalar. The reader who watched Reach's Jacobian ellipse collapse in
[Chapter 4](/chapters/ch04-configuration-space-i) now watches a second ellipse — the inertia
ellipse — breathe as the elbow folds, and learns why "force equals mass times acceleration" is false,
term by term, in joint coordinates.

Everything here is Choset's Chapter 10, with one addition the 2005 text did not need because it
assumed a Mathematica pipeline and never integrated anything: the skew-symmetry of $\dot M - 2C$ is
turned into a *test oracle*, and the integrator that powers every widget on this page is required to
conserve energy to a stated tolerance. If the code and the prose ever disagree, the energy bar settles
it.
</Overview>

## The problem: a path says nothing about time

Take a path for Reach — a smooth curve on the torus, the kind every planner in Part III hands
back — and replay it. Then replay it twice as fast.

<Figure caption="The same planned path (purple) replayed on Reach at two speeds, with a torque gauge for each joint. At 1× both gauges stay inside their limits of 20 and 12 N·m. At 2× the accelerations quadruple, the velocity-product terms quadruple, and both motors peg red. The planner that produced this curve never asked what the motors can do.">
  <TorqueGaugeHook />
</Figure>

Nothing about the curve changed. What changed is $\dot q$ and $\ddot q$, and the torque a joint must
supply depends on both — quadratically on $\dot q$, which is why doubling the speed does not double the
torque but quadruples the part of it that comes from motion. A path is a set of configurations; a
trajectory is that set *with a clock*, and only the clocked version can be feasible or infeasible.
Chapter 18 chooses the clock. This chapter builds the thing the clock is measured against: the map from
$(q, \dot q, \ddot q)$ to the torques $\tau$ that produce them.

## Building intuition

### The arm is a different machine in every configuration

Before any formalism, watch Reach fall. The Lagrangian Lab hangs the arm on a vertical Workbench,
releases it from a configuration you choose, and keeps the books.

Three things are worth noticing, and each becomes a theorem in the next section.

**Energy is conserved, exactly, by construction.** The total $E = K + V$ sits on its dashed reference
line while kinetic and potential energy trade places violently. There is no friction in the default
model and no torque, so this is what the physics must do — and the integrator reproduces it to about
one part in $10^8$ over ten seconds. That number is not an accident; it is a unit test, and the end of
the chapter prints it.

**Switch Coriolis off and the physics lies.** The toggle hands the same integrator a model in which the
velocity-product vector $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$ has been zeroed — "a small correction," the folklore says.
Within two swings the energy bar leaves its line. The arm gains or loses energy from nowhere, by more
than twenty percent of what it started with. Those terms are not a correction. They are the only thing
that makes joint-space Newton's law consistent with workspace Newton's law.

**Neither $M\ddot q$ nor $C\dot q$ is a force.** Watch the torque gauge. Under zero applied torque the
three stacks satisfy $M\ddot q + C\dot q + g = 0$ at every instant, and as the arm swings through
different configurations the share carried by $M\ddot q$ and by $C\dot q$ changes while their sum does
not. Choset says this precisely: "neither $M(q)\ddot q$ nor $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$ individually should be
thought of as a generalized force; only their sum is a force."

The orange curve is the set of joint velocities that carry exactly one joule of kinetic energy,

Kinetic energy is a quadratic form in $\dot q$, and the matrix of that form is $M(q)$. Everything
else — the velocity-product forces, the shape of unforced motion, what "orthogonal" means for a
constrained mechanism — is a statement about how $M$ changes with $q$.
</KeyIdea>

## The mathematics

### Definitions

**Generalized coordinates and forces.** A configuration is $q \in \R^n$ locally — joint angles for
Reach, $(x, y, \theta)$ for a planar body — and a generalized force $u$ is anything that pairs with
$\dot q$ to give power: $u\T\dot q$ is the rate at which work is done on the system. Torques pair with
joint rates; a force through the center of mass pairs with its velocity.

**The Lagrangian** is $L(q, \dot q) = K(q, \dot q) - V(q)$ with kinetic energy quadratic in the
velocities and potential energy a function of configuration alone. The Euler–Lagrange equations
(Choset eq. 10.1)

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = u
$$

are the equations of motion. Their derivation from the principle of least action is in any mechanics
text; this chapter takes them as the recipe.

**The inertia matrix** $M(q) = \partial^2 K / \partial\dot q\,\partial\dot q$, so that
$K = \tfrac12\dot q\T M(q)\dot q$ (eqs. 10.13–10.14). It is symmetric by construction and positive
definite because kinetic energy is positive for every nonzero velocity.

**Christoffel symbols.** For each $i$, the $n \times n$ matrix with entries

$$
\Gamma^i_{jk}(q) = \tfrac12\left(\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

(eq. 10.9). Terms with $j = k$ are *centrifugal*; terms with $j \ne k$ are *Coriolis*. There are $n^3$
of them, symmetric in the lower pair.

**A Pfaffian constraint** is $A(q)\dot q = 0$ for a $k \times n$ matrix of full row rank. It is
*holonomic* if each row is $\partial c/\partial q$ for some $c(q) = 0$ — the constraint is really a
constraint on configurations in disguise — and *nonholonomic* otherwise.
[Chapter 20](/chapters/ch20-nonholonomic-i) decides which; this chapter only imposes them.

**The inertia tensor** of a rigid body with density $\rho$ occupying $V$, in a frame at its center of
mass, is $\mathcal{I} = \int_V \rho(r)\,(\|r\|^2\mathbb{1} - r r\T)\,dV$ (eq. 10.42). Its
eigenvectors are the *principal axes*; its eigenvalues the principal moments.

### Euler–Lagrange gives the standard form

<KeyIdea title="Statement">
For $L = \tfrac12\dot q\T M(q)\dot q - V(q)$, the Euler–Lagrange equations are
<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-robot}{M(q)\ddot q} + \htmlClass{term-cobstacle}{C(q, \dot q)\dot q} + g(q) = u,
\qquad
C_{ij} = \sum_k \Gamma^i_{jk}\,\dot q_k,\qquad g = \frac{\partial V}{\partial q}.
$$
</LinkedMath>
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="From the Lagrangian to M q̈ + C q̇ + g = u" result="\text{eq. (10.7), with } C \text{ from the Christoffel symbols}">
**Step 1 — the momentum.** $\partial L/\partial\dot q = M(q)\dot q$, because $K$ is a quadratic form
and $V$ has no $\dot q$ in it.

**Step 2 — its time derivative.** $\tfrac{d}{dt}(M\dot q) = M\ddot q + \dot M\dot q$, and since $M$
depends on $t$ only through $q$,
$\dot M_{ij} = \sum_k (\partial M_{ij}/\partial q_k)\,\dot q_k$.

**Step 3 — the configuration derivative.**
$\partial L/\partial q_i = \tfrac12\dot q\T(\partial M/\partial q_i)\dot q - \partial V/\partial q_i$.

**Step 4 — subtract and symmetrize.** The $i$-th equation reads

$$
\sum_j M_{ij}\ddot q_j + \sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k}\dot q_j\dot q_k - \tfrac12\sum_{j,k}\frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\dot q_j\dot q_k + \frac{\partial V}{\partial q_i} = u_i .
$$

The middle sum is a quadratic form in $\dot q$ whose coefficient matrix $\partial M_{ij}/\partial q_k$
is *not* symmetric in $(j, k)$. A quadratic form only sees the symmetric part, so replace it by
$\tfrac12(\partial M_{ij}/\partial q_k + \partial M_{ik}/\partial q_j)$. Together with the third sum
that is exactly $\sum_{jk}\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k$ — the symmetrization *is* where the
three-term Christoffel formula comes from.

**Step 5 — read off the pieces.** Define $c_i = \sum_{jk}\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k$ (eq. 10.8),
$g_i = \partial V/\partial q_i$, and any matrix $C$ with $C\dot q = c$; the choice
$C_{ij} = \sum_k\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q_k$ is the one the rest of the chapter uses.
$\blacksquare$

**The RP arm as a check (Choset Examples 10.1.2, 10.2.1).** With $M = \operatorname{diag}(I_1 + I_2 + m_1 r_1^2 + m_2 q_2^2,\; m_2)$,
the only nonzero symbols are $\Gamma^1_{12} = \Gamma^1_{21} = m_2 q_2$ and $\Gamma^2_{11} = -m_2 q_2$,
and the velocity-product vector is $(2 m_2 q_2\dot q_1\dot q_2,\; -m_2 q_2\dot q_1^2)$ — eqs.
(10.5)–(10.6) exactly. The cast of this book is revolute, so the RP arm survives only here.

**$C$ is not unique; $C\dot q$ is.** Add to $C$ any matrix $S$ with $S\dot q = 0$ — for $n = 2$,
$S = s\,[\dot q_2, -\dot q_1]\T[\dot q_2, -\dot q_1]$ for any scalar $s$ — and the equations of motion
are unchanged. What *is* changed is the property the next derivation proves, which is why the
Christoffel choice is the one worth naming.
</Derivation>

### Ṁ − 2C is skew-symmetric, so energy is conserved exactly

<KeyIdea title="Statement">
With $C_{ij} = \sum_k\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q_k$, the matrix $\dot M - 2C$ is skew-symmetric. Hence along
any motion $\dot E = \dot q\T(u - b)$: with no applied torque and no dissipation, $E = K + V$ is
constant.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Why the Christoffel choice conserves energy" result="\dot q\T(\dot M - 2C)\dot q = 0,\quad \dot E = \dot q\T(u - b)">
**Step 1 — write both pieces with the same partials.** From Step 2 above,
$\dot M_{ij} = \sum_k\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k}\dot q_k$. From the definition,
$2C_{ij} = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial M_{ij}}{\partial q_k} + \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j} - \frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i}\right)\dot q_k$.

**Step 2 — the first terms cancel.** Subtracting leaves
$(\dot M - 2C)_{ij} = \sum_k\left(\frac{\partial M_{jk}}{\partial q_i} - \frac{\partial M_{ik}}{\partial q_j}\right)\dot q_k$.

**Step 3 — what remains is antisymmetric.** Swap $i$ and $j$ and the bracket changes sign:
$(\dot M - 2C)_{ji} = -(\dot M - 2C)_{ij}$. So $\dot q\T(\dot M - 2C)\dot q = 0$ for every $\dot q$,
since a quadratic form of a skew matrix vanishes identically.

**Step 4 — differentiate the energy.** $E = \tfrac12\dot q\T M\dot q + V$, so
$\dot E = \dot q\T M\ddot q + \tfrac12\dot q\T\dot M\dot q + \dot q\T g$. Substitute
$M\ddot q = u - C\dot q - g - b$:

$$
\dot E = \dot q\T(u - b) + \tfrac12\dot q\T(\dot M - 2C)\dot q = \dot q\T(u - b). \qquad\blacksquare
$$

**Why this is the test the `dynamics` crate runs.** The derivation used only (i) the correct $M$,
(ii) the correct $\Gamma$ obtained from it, and (iii) $g = \partial V/\partial q$. An error in any of
the three breaks the identity, and an integrator run from rest with $u = b = 0$ will show it as
energy drift. That is one assertion covering the whole derivation. The library check
`dynamics: released from rest … E stays within 1e-6 relative under RK4` is exactly this.

**Why dropping $C$ breaks it.** With $C \equiv 0$ the Step 4 remainder is $\tfrac12\dot q\T\dot M\dot q$,
which is not zero — it is the rate at which the configuration-dependence of $M$ pumps energy in or out.
That is the Lagrangian Lab's lie, and the sign of the drift depends on whether $\dot M$ is positive or
negative along the motion (Exercise 4).
</Derivation>

### The 2R arm, worked in full

This is Choset's Problem 10.2 — Reach in a vertical plane — and the model every later chapter in
Part V runs.

<KeyIdea title="Statement">
For links of length $L_i$, mass $m_i$, inertia $I_i$ about a center of mass at distance $r_i$ from
joint $i$, with gravity $a_g$ along $-x_2$ and $h(q) = m_2 L_1 r_2\sin q_2$:
<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-robot}{M(q)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
I_1 + I_2 + m_1 r_1^2 + m_2(L_1^2 + r_2^2 + 2L_1 r_2\cos q_2) & I_2 + m_2 r_2^2 + m_2 L_1 r_2\cos q_2 \\
I_2 + m_2 r_2^2 + m_2 L_1 r_2\cos q_2 & I_2 + m_2 r_2^2
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\htmlClass{term-cobstacle}{C\dot q} =
\begin{pmatrix} -h\dot q_2^2 - 2h\dot q_1\dot q_2 \\ h\dot q_1^2 \end{pmatrix},
$$
</LinkedMath>
$$
g(q) = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1 r_1 + m_2 L_1)\,a_g\cos q_1 + m_2 r_2\, a_g\cos(q_1 + q_2) \\ m_2 r_2\, a_g\cos(q_1 + q_2)\end{pmatrix},
$$
and the only nonzero Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^1_{12} = \Gamma^1_{21} = \Gamma^1_{22} = -h$ and
$\Gamma^2_{11} = h$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The Lagrange recipe applied to Reach" result="M, \Gamma, g \text{ of the statement}">
**Step 1 — centers of mass by forward kinematics.** Chapter 2's $\varphi$ evaluated at $r_i$ instead
of $L_i$: $c_1 = r_1(\cos q_1, \sin q_1)$ and
$c_2 = (L_1\cos q_1 + r_2\cos(q_1 + q_2),\; L_1\sin q_1 + r_2\sin(q_1 + q_2))$.

**Step 2 — center-of-mass Jacobians.** Differentiating, $J_{c_1} = r_1\begin{pmatrix}-\sin q_1 & 0\\ \cos q_1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and

$$
J_{c_2} = \begin{pmatrix} -L_1\sin q_1 - r_2\sin(q_1 + q_2) & -r_2\sin(q_1 + q_2) \\ L_1\cos q_1 + r_2\cos(q_1 + q_2) & r_2\cos(q_1 + q_2)\end{pmatrix}.
$$

These are Chapter 4's Jacobian construction applied to interior points of the links.

**Step 3 — kinetic energy.** Each link contributes translation of its center of mass plus rotation
about it: $K = \sum_i \tfrac12\left(m_i\|J_{c_i}\dot q\|^2 + I_i\omega_i^2\right)$ with
$\omega_1 = \dot q_1$ and $\omega_2 = \dot q_1 + \dot q_2$, i.e. $J_{\omega_1} = (1, 0)$,
$J_{\omega_2} = (1, 1)$. Hence

$$
M = \sum_i \left( m_i J_{c_i}\T J_{c_i} + I_i J_{\omega_i}\T J_{\omega_i}\right).
$$

Multiplying out: $\|J_{c_2}\dot q\|^2 = (L_1^2 + r_2^2 + 2L_1 r_2\cos q_2)\dot q_1^2 + 2(r_2^2 + L_1 r_2\cos q_2)\dot q_1\dot q_2 + r_2^2\dot q_2^2$,
using $\cos q_1\cos(q_1{+}q_2) + \sin q_1\sin(q_1{+}q_2) = \cos q_2$. Collect by $\dot q_i\dot q_j$ and the
three entries of $M$ in the statement appear.

**Step 4 — differentiate for $\Gamma$.** Only $q_2$ appears in $M$, and only through $\cos q_2$:
$\partial M_{11}/\partial q_2 = -2h$, $\partial M_{12}/\partial q_2 = -h$, every other partial is zero.
Feeding these into the three-term formula: $\Gamma^1_{12} = \tfrac12(\partial M_{11}/\partial q_2) = -h$;
$\Gamma^1_{22} = \tfrac12(2\,\partial M_{12}/\partial q_2 - \partial M_{22}/\partial q_1) = -h$;
$\Gamma^2_{11} = \tfrac12(2\,\partial M_{21}/\partial q_1 - \partial M_{11}/\partial q_2) = h$;
$\Gamma^2_{12} = \tfrac12(\partial M_{21}/\partial q_2 + \partial M_{22}/\partial q_1 - \partial M_{12}/\partial q_2) = 0$.
Contracting, $c_1 = -2h\dot q_1\dot q_2 - h\dot q_2^2$ and $c_2 = h\dot q_1^2$ (Problem 10.3).

**Step 5 — gravity.** $V = m_1 a_g r_1\sin q_1 + m_2 a_g\left(L_1\sin q_1 + r_2\sin(q_1 + q_2)\right)$,
and $g = \partial V/\partial q$ is the statement. $\blacksquare$

**The same recipe for 3R.** Nothing in Steps 1–5 used $n = 2$ except the trigonometric collapse in
Step 3. For Reach's three-link variant there are three center-of-mass Jacobians, nine entries of $M$
and 27 symbols — too many to differentiate by hand without error, so the library assembles $M$ from the
Jacobians and takes the Christoffel symbols by central differences. The 2R case is the check: the
finite-difference symbols agree with the closed form above to $10^{-7}$ at fifty random states.
</Derivation>

### Constrained dynamics and the projection P

Rusty's axle, Hitch's wheels, a knife-edge on ice: each may move along its heading and spin but not
slide sideways. That is a constraint on *velocities*, and it enters the equations of motion through a
Lagrange multiplier.

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset eqs. 10.16–10.20)">
With $A(q)\dot q = 0$, the constrained equations are
$M\ddot q + C\dot q + g = u + A\T\lambda$ and $A\ddot q + \dot A\dot q = 0$: $n + k$ equations for the
$n + k$ unknowns $(\ddot q, \lambda)$. Eliminating $\lambda$ gives
$P\ddot q = PM^{-1}(u - C\dot q - g)$ with
$P = \mathbb{1} - M^{-1}A\T(AM^{-1}A\T)^{-1}A$ of rank $n - k$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Eliminating the multipliers" result="P \ddot q = P M^{-1}(u - C\dot q - g)">
**Step 1 — differentiate the constraint.** $A\dot q = 0$ holds for all time, so
$\tfrac{d}{dt}(A\dot q) = A\ddot q + \dot A\dot q = 0$ (eq. 10.17).

**Step 2 — solve the force balance for $\ddot q$.** From (10.16),
$\ddot q = M^{-1}(u + A\T\lambda - C\dot q - g)$.

**Step 3 — substitute into the differentiated constraint.**
$\dot A\dot q + AM^{-1}(u + A\T\lambda - C\dot q - g) = 0$.

**Step 4 — solve for $\lambda$.** The $k \times k$ matrix $AM^{-1}A\T$ is invertible because $A$ has
full row rank and $M \succ 0$:
$\lambda = (AM^{-1}A\T)^{-1}\left(-\dot A\dot q + AM^{-1}(C\dot q + g - u)\right)$.

**Step 5 — substitute back and factor.** Using $-\dot A\dot q = A\ddot q$ and collecting,
$(\mathbb{1} - A\T(AM^{-1}A\T)^{-1}AM^{-1})(M\ddot q + C\dot q + g - u) = 0$. Call the bracket
$P_u$ (eq. 10.18); then $P = M^{-1}P_u M$ (eq. 10.19) and rearranging gives the statement
(eq. 10.20). $\blacksquare$

**Orthogonality with respect to $M$.** $P$ and $\mathbb{1} - P$ split a velocity into a part that
satisfies the constraints and a part in the constrained directions, and
$(P\dot q)\T M(\mathbb{1} - P)\dot q = 0$ for every $\dot q$. The inner product is $M$, not the
identity: Choset is explicit that this is "the appropriate one when discussing dynamics," because
$M$ carries the metric of coordinates that mix lengths and angles. Also $P = P_u\T$, which the
library checks numerically alongside the rank.

**The knife-edge (Example 10.3.1).** $q = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$ is contact point and heading,
$M = \operatorname{diag}(m, m, I)$, $A = [\sin q_3, -\cos q_3, 0]$, $\dot A = \dot q_3[\cos q_3, \sin q_3, 0]$.
Then $P = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2 q_3 & \sin q_3\cos q_3 & 0\\ \sin q_3\cos q_3 & \sin^2 q_3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$,
rank 2, and the closed forms (10.25)–(10.28) follow — including the constraint force
$\lambda_1 = (u_2 - m\dot q_1\dot q_3)\cos q_3 - (u_1 + m\dot q_2\dot q_3)\sin q_3$. The library solves
the $(n + k) \times (n + k)$ system directly and reproduces those four formulas to $10^{-9}$ at thirty
random states. This is Rusty's no-side-slip constraint, and Chapter 20 will show it is nonholonomic.
</Derivation>

### Euler's equation in the body frame

For a spinning rigid body no choice of three angles gives a smooth global coordinate system — the
same fact Chapter 5 made about $\SOthree$ — so Choset does not pick one. He takes $R \in \SOthree$ as
the orientation and the body-frame angular velocity $\omega$ as the velocity, and derives the dynamics
directly.

<KeyIdea title="Statement (Choset eqs. 10.43–10.44)">
With $\mathcal{I}$ the constant body-frame inertia tensor,
$$
\dot R = R\hat\omega, \qquad \tau = \hat\omega\,\mathcal{I}\omega + \mathcal{I}\dot\omega .
$$
In particular $\dot\omega \ne 0$ when $\tau = 0$ unless $\omega$ is along a principal axis.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="From the spatial frame to the body frame" result="\tau = \hat\omega \mathcal{I}\omega + \mathcal{I}\dot\omega">
**Step 1 — kinetic energy in the spatial frame.** A point at $r_s$ moves at $\omega_s \times r_s$, so
$K = \tfrac12\int_V\rho\,\|\omega_s \times r_s\|^2\,dV = \tfrac12\omega_s\T\mathcal{I}_s\omega_s$ with
$\mathcal{I}_s = \int_V\rho(\|r_s\|^2\mathbb{1} - r_s r_s\T)\,dV$ (eqs. 10.34–10.36). Because it is
written in a fixed frame, $\mathcal{I}_s$ changes as the body turns.

**Step 2 — angular momentum and its rate.** $P = \mathcal{I}_s\omega_s$ and $\tau_s = \dot P$. The
density does not change, so $\dot{\mathcal{I}}_s = \omega_s \times \mathcal{I}_s$ comes only from the
rotation, giving $\tau_s = \omega_s \times \mathcal{I}_s\omega_s + \mathcal{I}_s\dot\omega_s$ — Euler's
equation in the inertial frame (10.37), or $\tau_s = \hat\omega_s\mathcal{I}_s\omega_s + \mathcal{I}_s\dot\omega_s$
in matrix form (10.38).

**Step 3 — change frames.** $\omega_s = R\omega$, $\tau_s = R\tau$, $\mathcal{I}_s = R\mathcal{I}R\T$,
$\hat\omega_s = \dot R R\T$, and $\dot R = R\hat\omega$ (10.39, 10.41).

**Step 4 — substitute.** $R\tau = \dot R R\T R\mathcal{I}R\T R\omega + R\mathcal{I}R\T(\dot R\omega + R\dot\omega)$.

**Step 5 — kill one term.** $\dot R\omega = R\hat\omega\omega = R(\omega \times \omega) = 0$.

**Step 6 — premultiply by $R\T$.** $\tau = R\T\dot R\,\mathcal{I}\omega + \mathcal{I}\dot\omega = \hat\omega\mathcal{I}\omega + \mathcal{I}\dot\omega$. $\blacksquare$

**The principal-axis form (10.45).** Align the body frame with the eigenvectors of $\mathcal{I}$ and
$\tau_x = I_{xx}\dot\omega_x + (I_{zz} - I_{yy})\omega_y\omega_z$, with the two cyclic permutations.
Set $\tau = 0$: $\dot\omega_x = (I_{yy} - I_{zz})\omega_y\omega_z/I_{xx}$ is zero only if two of the
components vanish. Spin about the axis of *intermediate* inertia and a small perturbation in the other
two components grows exponentially — the flip the next widget shows. The parallel-axis theorem
(10.33 planar, 10.46 spatial) and the composite-body rule $\mathcal{I} = \sum_i R_i(\mathcal{I}_i + m_i(\|r_i\|^2\mathbb{1} - r_i r_i\T))R_i\T$
are functions in the library, not sections here.
</Derivation>

### RK4 on the first-order state, and what its energy drift means

Write $x = (q, \dot q)$ and $\dot x = (\dot q,\; M^{-1}(u - C\dot q - g - b))$. Classical RK4 has local
error $O(h^5)$ and global error $O(h^4)$, and the energy error inherits that order. Three honesty
items the prose must keep:

1. **RK4 is not symplectic.** Its energy error is bounded by $c\,h^4$ over the horizons we use because
   the global error is, not because of any structural guarantee. Run it for an hour and it will drift.
2. **The bound is empirical.** The test asserts $|E(t) - E(0)|/E(0) < 10^{-6}$ for $h = 1$ ms over
   10 s on the micro-example released from rest; the measured value is about $10^{-8}$.
3. **Explicit Euler drifts linearly** and is kept only as the bad example: on the same run it loses
   track of the energy by more than ten percent. Semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler keeps the error
   bounded, but at first order the bound is also around ten percent on this violently swinging arm —
   cheap, not accurate.

## The algorithm

Choset numbers none of these; the names follow his text.

<Algorithm
  name="lagrange_recipe(links, φ, a_g)"
  inputs="link parameters (L_i, r_i, m_i, I_i), forward kinematics, gravitational acceleration"
  outputs="M(q), Γ(q), g(q) as callables"
  complexity="O(n²) Jacobian products for M; O(n³) Christoffel symbols"
>
1. for each link $i$: $c_i(q) \leftarrow$ position of its center of mass by $\varphi$ at $r_i$; $J_{c_i}(q) \leftarrow \partial c_i/\partial q$; $J_{\omega_i} \leftarrow (1, \dots, 1, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $i$ ones
2. $M(q) \leftarrow \sum_i \left(m_i J_{c_i}\T J_{c_i} + I_i J_{\omega_i}\T J_{\omega_i}\right)$
3. $V(q) \leftarrow \sum_i m_i a_g\, [c_i(q)]_y$; $\;g(q) \leftarrow \sum_i m_i a_g\,[J_{c_i}(q)]_{y,\cdot}$
4. $\Gamma^i_{jk}(q) \leftarrow \tfrac12(\partial_k M_{ij} + \partial_j M_{ik} - \partial_i M_{jk})$, by closed form or central differences
5. return $(M, \Gamma, g)$
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="forward_dynamics(q, q̇, u)"
  inputs="state and generalized force"
  outputs="q̈"
  complexity="one n×n solve, O(n³)"
>
1. $c \leftarrow \dot q\T\Gamma(q)\dot q$; $\;r \leftarrow u - c - g(q) - b(q, \dot q)$
2. return $M(q)^{-1} r$ by Cholesky or pivoted elimination — Choset eq. (11.46)
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="constrained_forward_dynamics(q, q̇, u, A, Ȧ)"
  inputs="state, force, constraint matrix and its rate"
  outputs="(q̈, λ)"
  complexity="one (n+k)×(n+k) KKT solve"
>
1. assemble $\begin{pmatrix} M & -A\T \\ A & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\ddot q\\ \lambda\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u - C\dot q - g - b \\ -\dot A\dot q\end{pmatrix}$ — eqs. (10.16)–(10.17)
2. solve; return $(\ddot q, \lambda)$. The constraint force on the system is $A\T\lambda$.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="rk4_step(f, x, h)"
  inputs="ẋ = f(x), state, step"
  outputs="x(t + h)"
  complexity="4 dynamics evaluations"
>
1. $k_1 \leftarrow f(x)$; $k_2 \leftarrow f(x + \tfrac h2 k_1)$; $k_3 \leftarrow f(x + \tfrac h2 k_2)$; $k_4 \leftarrow f(x + h k_3)$
2. return $x + \tfrac h6(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)$
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="euler_body_step(R, ω, τ, 𝓘, h)"
  inputs="orientation as a unit quaternion, body angular velocity, body torque, body inertia tensor, step"
  outputs="(R, ω) after h"
  complexity="O(1)"
>
1. state $x \leftarrow (R, \omega)$ with $\dot R = \tfrac12 R \otimes (0, \omega)$ and $\dot\omega = \mathcal{I}^{-1}(\tau - \omega \times \mathcal{I}\omega)$ — eqs. (10.43)–(10.44)
2. one `rk4_step` on $x$; renormalize $R$
</Algorithm>

with four physical callbacks and two derived operations.

```rust title="crates/dynamics/src/lib.rs"
use nalgebra::{SMatrix, SVector};

/// A fully actuated second-order mechanical system in generalized coordinates.
/// Everything downstream (time scaling, iLQR, kinodynamic RRT) talks to this trait.
pub trait Dynamics<const N: usize> {
    /// M(q): symmetric positive definite. We return the full matrix — the
    /// Cholesky factorization is cheap for N ≤ 3 and keeps the code readable.
    fn mass(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>) -> SMatrix<f64, N, N>;

    /// The velocity-product vector C(q, q̇) q̇ = q̇ᵀ Γ(q) q̇ — returned as a vector,
    /// because the matrix C is not unique and only the product is a generalized
    /// force (Choset §10.2).
    fn velocity_product(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>) -> SVector<f64, N>;

    /// g(q) = ∂V/∂q.
    fn gravity(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>) -> SVector<f64, N>;

    /// V(q), so E = K + V can be tracked. K = ½ q̇ᵀ M q̇ is provided below.
    fn potential_energy(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>) -> f64;

    /// Dissipation b(q, q̇); default: none.
    fn dissipation(&self, _q: &SVector<f64, N>, _qd: &SVector<f64, N>) -> SVector<f64, N> {
        SVector::zeros()
    }

    /// q̈ = M⁻¹(u − C q̇ − g − b). Default impl; override only for a faster recursive form.
    fn forward(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>, u: &SVector<f64, N>)
        -> SVector<f64, N>
    {
        let rhs = u - self.velocity_product(q, qd) - self.gravity(q) - self.dissipation(q, qd);
        self.mass(q).cholesky().expect("M(q) must be positive definite").solve(&rhs)
    }

    /// u = M q̈ + C q̇ + g + b — the torque that produces a given acceleration.
    fn inverse(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>, qdd: &SVector<f64, N>)
        -> SVector<f64, N>
    {
        self.mass(q) * qdd + self.velocity_product(q, qd) + self.gravity(q) + self.dissipation(q, qd)
    }

    fn kinetic_energy(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>) -> f64 {
        0.5 * qd.dot(&(self.mass(q) * qd))
    }

    fn energy(&self, q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>) -> f64 {
        self.kinetic_energy(q, qd) + self.potential_energy(q)
    }
}
```

```rust title="crates/dynamics/src/reach2r.rs" {30-38,41-47}
use nalgebra::{Matrix2, Vector2};
use crate::Dynamics;

/// Choset Problem 10.2: planar 2R arm in a vertical plane. Units: kg, m, s.
pub struct Reach2R {
    pub l1: f64, pub r1: f64, pub m1: f64, pub i1: f64,
    pub l2: f64, pub r2: f64, pub m2: f64, pub i2: f64,
    pub a_g: f64, // 0.0 lays the Workbench flat
}

impl Reach2R {
    /// Uniform rods: r_i = L_i/2, I_i = m_i L_i²/12.
    pub fn uniform(l1: f64, m1: f64, l2: f64, m2: f64, a_g: f64) -> Self {
        Self {
            l1, r1: l1 / 2.0, m1, i1: m1 * l1 * l1 / 12.0,
            l2, r2: l2 / 2.0, m2, i2: m2 * l2 * l2 / 12.0,
            a_g,
        }
    }

    /// h(q) = m₂ L₁ r₂ sin q₂ — every velocity-product term is built from it.
    fn h(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>) -> f64 {
        self.m2 * self.l1 * self.r2 * q[1].sin()
    }

    /// Closed form: the four nonzero symbols Γ¹₁₂ = Γ¹₂₁ = Γ¹₂₂ = −h, Γ²₁₁ = h.
    pub fn christoffel(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>) -> [Matrix2<f64>; 2] {
        let h = self.h(q);
        [Matrix2::new(0.0, -h, -h, -h), Matrix2::new(h, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)]
    }
}

impl Dynamics<2> for Reach2R {
    fn mass(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>) -> Matrix2<f64> {
        let c2 = q[1].cos();
        let m22 = self.i2 + self.m2 * self.r2 * self.r2;
        let m12 = m22 + self.m2 * self.l1 * self.r2 * c2;
        let m11 = self.i1 + self.i2 + self.m1 * self.r1 * self.r1
            + self.m2 * (self.l1 * self.l1 + self.r2 * self.r2 + 2.0 * self.l1 * self.r2 * c2);
        Matrix2::new(m11, m12, m12, m22)
    }

    fn velocity_product(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>, qd: &Vector2<f64>) -> Vector2<f64> {
        let h = self.h(q);
        Vector2::new(-h * qd[1] * qd[1] - 2.0 * h * qd[0] * qd[1], h * qd[0] * qd[0])
    }

    fn gravity(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>) -> Vector2<f64> {
        let (c1, c12) = (q[0].cos(), (q[0] + q[1]).cos());
        Vector2::new(
            (self.m1 * self.r1 + self.m2 * self.l1) * self.a_g * c1 + self.m2 * self.r2 * self.a_g * c12,
            self.m2 * self.r2 * self.a_g * c12,
        )
    }

    fn potential_energy(&self, q: &Vector2<f64>) -> f64 {
        self.m1 * self.a_g * self.r1 * q[0].sin()
            + self.m2 * self.a_g * (self.l1 * q[0].sin() + self.r2 * (q[0] + q[1]).sin())
    }
}
```

```rust title="crates/dynamics/src/lagrange.rs"
use nalgebra::{SMatrix, SVector};

/// Γ^i_jk = ½(∂M_ij/∂q_k + ∂M_ik/∂q_j − ∂M_jk/∂q_i) (Choset eq. 10.9), with the
/// partials by central differences. O(n³) symbols from 2n evaluations of M.
/// The 3R arm has no other path; the 2R arm's closed form checks this one.
pub fn christoffel<const N: usize>(
    mass: impl Fn(&SVector<f64, N>) -> SMatrix<f64, N, N>,
    q: &SVector<f64, N>,
    step: f64,
) -> [SMatrix<f64, N, N>; N] {
    let mut d_m = [SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::zeros(); N]; // d_m[k] = ∂M/∂q_k
    for k in 0..N {
        let (mut qp, mut qm) = (*q, *q);
        qp[k] += step;
        qm[k] -= step;
        d_m[k] = (mass(&qp) - mass(&qm)) / (2.0 * step);
    }
    let mut gamma = [SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::zeros(); N];
    for i in 0..N {
        for j in 0..N {
            for k in 0..N {
                gamma[i][(j, k)] = 0.5 * (d_m[k][(i, j)] + d_m[j][(i, k)] - d_m[i][(j, k)]);
            }
        }
    }
    gamma
}

/// max |(Ṉ − 2C) + (Ṉ − 2C)ᵀ| — zero when C is the Christoffel choice. A
/// diagnostic for a model, not a step of any algorithm.
pub fn skew_symmetry_defect<const N: usize>(
    mass: impl Fn(&SVector<f64, N>) -> SMatrix<f64, N, N> + Copy,
    q: &SVector<f64, N>,
    qd: &SVector<f64, N>,
) -> f64 {
    let gamma = christoffel(mass, q, 1e-6);
    let mut c = SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::zeros();
    let mut m_dot = SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::zeros();
    for i in 0..N {
        for j in 0..N {
            for k in 0..N {
                c[(i, j)] += gamma[i][(j, k)] * qd[k];
            }
        }
    }
    // Ṁ = Σ_k ∂M/∂q_k q̇_k, recomputed here so the two sides share no code path.
    for k in 0..N {
        let (mut qp, mut qm) = (*q, *q);
        qp[k] += 1e-6;
        qm[k] -= 1e-6;
        m_dot += (mass(&qp) - mass(&qm)) / 2e-6 * qd[k];
    }
    let s = m_dot - 2.0 * c;
    (s + s.transpose()).abs().max()
}
```

```rust title="crates/dynamics/src/integrate.rs"
use nalgebra::SVector;
use crate::Dynamics;

/// One classical RK4 step on x = (q, q̇) with u held over the step.
pub fn rk4_step<const N: usize>(
    dyn_: &impl Dynamics<N>,
    q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>, u: &SVector<f64, N>, h: f64,
) -> (SVector<f64, N>, SVector<f64, N>) {
    let f = |q: &SVector<f64, N>, qd: &SVector<f64, N>| (*qd, dyn_.forward(q, qd, u));
    let (k1q, k1v) = f(q, qd);
    let (k2q, k2v) = f(&(q + k1q * (h / 2.0)), &(qd + k1v * (h / 2.0)));
    let (k3q, k3v) = f(&(q + k2q * (h / 2.0)), &(qd + k2v * (h / 2.0)));
    let (k4q, k4v) = f(&(q + k3q * h), &(qd + k3v * h));
    (
        q + (k1q + k2q * 2.0 + k3q * 2.0 + k4q) * (h / 6.0),
        qd + (k1v + k2v * 2.0 + k3v * 2.0 + k4v) * (h / 6.0),
    )
}
```

## Putting it together

The integration lab drops the same `Reach2R` into Chapter 2's Workbench with gravity on and a PD
controller holding a planned waypoint:

$$
\tau = g(q^*) + K_p(q^* - q) - K_d\,\dot q .
$$

Hold the micro-example pose. The gravity compensation term alone is $g(q^*) = (10.405, -3.468)$ N·m —
more than half the shoulder motor's 20 N·m budget just to stand still. Raise $K_p$ and the arm
overshoots on release; the overshoot is governed by $M(q)$, which is why a gain that is crisp with the
arm folded ($M_{11} = 1$) rings with it stretched ($M_{11} = 3$). The torque trace this lab produces,
peak and all, is the limit Chapter 18's time scaler inherits.

Here is where the chapter closes and the next one opens. Take any planned path $q(s)$, substitute
$\dot q = q'\dot s$ and $\ddot q = q''\dot s^2 + q'\ddot s$ into the standard form, and the $n$
equations collapse to

$$
a(s)\,\ddot s + b(s)\,\dot s^2 + c(s) = \tau, \qquad a = M q',\; b = M q'' + q'\T\Gamma q',\; c = g .
$$

A path plus these equations is a *one-dimensional* dynamics in the path parameter. The whole of
[Chapter 18](/chapters/ch18-trajectory-planning) lives in the $(s, \dot s)$ plane that equation
defines. [Chapter 19](/chapters/ch19-trajectory-optimization) linearizes `forward` along a trajectory
for iLQR. [Chapter 20](/chapters/ch20-nonholonomic-i) reuses $A(q)\dot q = 0$ and asks whether the
knife-edge constraint can be integrated. All three import this crate and none of them re-derives a
line of it.
