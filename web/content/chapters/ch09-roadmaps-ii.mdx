Chapter 8 ended on a cliff. In the plane, "equidistant to the two nearest obstacles" is a curve,
and the curves form a roadmap. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ the same condition is one equation in three
unknowns and cuts out a *sheet* — and a union of sheets is not a roadmap: the problem is only one
dimension smaller than before. This chapter climbs down two ways, and they turn out to be one
idea. The first way intersects more equations: equidistance to *three* obstacles in
$\mathbb{R}^3$ is a curve again, the **generalized Voronoi graph**, provided the sheets cross
cleanly — *transversally*. The curves need not connect, and patching them, the hierarchical GVG,
is the real work. The second way sweeps a slice through the space and tracks the extrema of a
function on it: **Canny's silhouette curves**, recursing wherever the trace breaks. Both obey
Chapter 8's Morse vocabulary: critical points are exactly where a roadmap can change connectivity.

The practical surprise is that a robot can build a roadmap *without a map*. The quantity $d_1 -
d_2$ is something a range sensor measures directly, and so is its gradient; a control law that
drives $\Gamma = \tfrac12 G^\top G$ to zero — a Lyapunov function, the book's first stability
proof — lets Rusty trace the generalized Voronoi graph with no predictor, no corrector, and no
equation of the curve. Exploration is then depth-first search over a graph the robot is still
drawing.

The price of exactness closes the chapter. Canny's roadmap is complete and its running time is
singly exponential in the dimension; the generalized mover's problem is PSPACE-hard. Both facts are
why Part III gives completeness up for speed. Everything on this page runs: the Apartment's graph
is built from a simulated range ring and lands on all twenty-six meet points of Chapter 8's exact
diagram, the sphere's silhouette is its equator to $10^{-6}$, and the chapter's one-step Lyapunov
example prints $\dot\Gamma = -0.7918$ to four decimals in both the TypeScript port and the Rust.
</Overview>

## The problem: a map nobody drew

Drop Rusty into the Apartment with no floor plan, only its range ring. Twenty seconds into the
replay — under three minutes of simulated driving — a graph of the whole floor exists: every room,
every doorway, the corridor, drawn by the robot driving along it.

Nothing in that animation knows where a wall is. Each step the robot reads its 360 rays, finds the
local minima of the range — the distances $d_i$ to the nearby obstacles and, from their bearings,
the gradients $\nabla d_i$ — and commands one velocity. The blue curve is Chapter 8's generalized
Voronoi diagram; the robot does not compute it, it *lives on it*, because staying on it is a
condition the sensor can check: two of the ranges are equal. The run ends when every curve leaving
every meet point has been driven: $27$ meet points, $33$ boundary points where a curve runs into a
corner, $62$ edges and $103.4$ m of roadmap after $205.0$ m of driving.

Two questions organize the rest of the chapter. *Why does this work* — what makes a velocity law
converge onto a curve it cannot see? And *what happens in three dimensions*, where "two readings
tie" is a surface and the robot would be wandering on sheets? The first answer is a Lyapunov
function. The second is a story about transversality, disconnection, and slices — and it ends in a
complexity bound that sends us to sampling.

## Building intuition

### Driving the graph

Watch the explorer in phases. **Access**: from wherever it starts, Rusty backs away from the
nearest return — gradient ascent of $D$, exactly Chapter 8's Lemma 5.2.1 — until a second return is
as short as the first. **Trace**: now it holds the difference of the two shortest returns at zero
while moving perpendicular to the chord between their two closest points. The $\Gamma$ sparkline
under the floor plan is the square of that difference: it spikes when the robot is pushed off the
curve (the parabolic edges near door jambs curve faster than the law can follow at a gentle gain)
and decays back. **Home**: when a third return closes in, a meet point is near; the robot switches
law and converges on the point where all three tie. **Branch**: each meet point in the plane has
three departing edges. The hollow node's number counts those not yet driven. **Backtrack**: when a
node has none left, the robot drives along edges it already knows to the nearest node that still
has one — depth-first search over a graph whose nodes are being discovered by the search itself.
**Terminate**: when no node has an undriven edge, the graph is complete.

The faint blue curves underneath are Chapter 8's exact GVD of the same walls, traced from geometry.
The sensor's graph lands on them: every meet point the robot homes onto is three-way equidistant by
exact wall geometry to $10^{-6}$, and all twenty-six exact meet points are found within five
centimetres. Two more — in the corridor at $(6.00, 4.30)$ and in the bedroom at $(7.85, 6.79)$ — are
genuine meet points that Chapter 8's lattice-seeded tracer missed, which the same exact test
confirms. The headline control is $\beta$, the stiffness with which the robot is pulled back onto
the curve. The misconception this widget kills is the one in the chapter title: *you do not need a
map to build a roadmap*. The roadmap is a sensor quantity.

### From sheets to curves

In the plane one equation, $d_i = d_j$, leaves one free direction: a curve. In $\mathbb{R}^3$ it
leaves two: a sheet. Imagine the Apartment extruded upward into a box-shaped room with a floor and
a ceiling. The set of points equally far from the floor and the ceiling is the horizontal plane
halfway up; equally far from the floor and the west wall is a tilted plane; and so on. These sheets
form Chapter 8's GVD in three dimensions, and it is a perfectly good *two*-dimensional retract — but
a planner searching a two-dimensional set has not gained much.

So intersect more. Points equidistant to the floor, the ceiling *and* the west wall lie on two
sheets at once, and two planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$ meet in a line. That is a generalized Voronoi
graph edge: think of a ball rolling along the room, touching the floor, the ceiling and one wall at
once; its centre traces the edge. Where a fourth obstacle ties, four sheets meet at a point — a
meet point. Two conditions must hold for the picture to be right: the sheets must actually cross
rather than graze, and the resulting curves must connect. The first is transversality; the second,
it turns out, fails.

### A slice through a doughnut

Canny's idea comes at the same problem from the other side. Sweep a plane through the
configuration space and look at what the plane cuts out: a few closed curves. On each curve mark
the extreme points in some fixed direction. As the plane moves those marks move, and their traces
are curves — *silhouettes* — that a planner can follow. On a torus lying flat, a plane sweeping
across it first touches the outer rim, cuts one oval, and then, as it reaches the hole, the oval
pinches and splits into two. That moment is a *critical slice*: the silhouette breaks there, and
the algorithm repairs it by solving the same problem one dimension down, inside the critical slice.
The Silhouette Sweep in the mathematics section plays this out.

### The generalized Voronoi graph

<KeyIdea title="Definition — generalized Voronoi graph (Choset §5.3.1, eq. 5.3)">
In $\mathbb{R}^3$ let $S_{ijk} = \{q : (d_i - d_j)(q) = 0 \text{ and } (d_i - d_k)(q) = 0\}$ — the
third equation $d_j = d_k$ follows — and $SS_{ijk} = SS_{ij} \cap SS_{ik} \cap SS_{jk}$, the
points of $S_{ijk}$ where the three gradients $\nabla d_i, \nabla d_j, \nabla d_k$ are pairwise
distinct. A **GVG edge** is $F_{ijk} = \{q \in SS_{ijk} : d_i(q) \le d_h(q)\ \forall h\}$ and the
**GVG** is $\bigcup_i \bigcup_j \bigcup_k F_{ijk}$. In $\mathbb{R}^m$ the GVG is the set of points
equidistant to $m$ closest obstacles with distinct gradients; it is one-dimensional, and its edges
end at **meet points** (equidistant to $m + 1$) and **boundary points**. In the plane the GVG and
the GVD coincide.
</KeyIdea>

Note what the definition does *not* say: $SS_{ijk}$ is not $SS_{ij} \cap SS_{ik}$. Transitivity of
equality gives $d_j = d_k$ for free, but not $\nabla d_j \ne \nabla d_k$, and all three gradients
must differ for the preimage theorem to apply. Choset's Table 5.2 compares the two structures in
$\mathbb{R}^m$:

| Structure | Dimension | Codimension | Equidistant to | Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GVD | $m - 1$ | $1$ | $2$ | $F_{i_1 i_2}$ |
| GVG | $1$ | $m - 1$ | $m$ | $F_{i_1 \dots i_m}$ |

<KeyIdea title="Definition — transversality (Choset §5.3.1)">
Submanifolds $M_1, M_2 \subset M$ intersect **transversally** if $T_x M_1 + T_x M_2 = T_x M$ at every
point $x$ of the intersection. Then codimensions add: $\mathrm{codim}(T_x M_1 \cap T_x M_2) =
\mathrm{codim}\, T_x M_1 + \mathrm{codim}\, T_x M_2$. Transversal intersections are *stable* — a small
perturbation of either manifold leaves an intersection of the same dimension — and a
non-transversal intersection becomes transversal after a generic small perturbation. The
transversality assumption generalizes the general-position assumption of computational geometry.
</KeyIdea>

<Figure caption="The transversality trio (Choset Fig. 5.17). Two lines in the plane meet not at all, at a point, or along a line. Hover a panel to perturb the teal line: the parallel and overlapping pairs change the dimension of their intersection; the crossing pair still meets at a point. The number in each panel is the smallest singular value of the matrix whose rows are the two lines' normals — the transversality margin the GVG code computes for its sheets — and it is zero exactly in the unstable cases.">
  <TransversalityTrio />
</Figure>

Two lines in the plane each have codimension one; meeting transversally, their intersection has
codimension two — a point. Two planes in $\mathbb{R}^3$ meet transversally in a line, a plane and
a line in a point; two lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can never meet transversally, because a generic
nudge separates them. Two planes in $\mathbb{R}^4$, codimension two each, meet in a point. That
arithmetic is the whole content of Table 5.2.

<LinkedMath>
$$
G(q) = \begin{bmatrix} d_i - d_j \\ d_i - d_k \end{bmatrix}\!(q),
\qquad
DG(q) = \begin{bmatrix} (\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j)^{\top} \\ (\nabla d_i - \nabla d_k)^{\top} \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\htmlClass{term-graph}{SS_{ijk}} \subset G^{-1}(0) \;\; \text{is a 1-manifold where } \operatorname{rank} DG = 2.
$$
</LinkedMath>

<Derivation title="GVG edges in R³ are one-dimensional" result="SS_{ijk} = G^{-1}(0) \cap \{\text{distinct gradients}\},\quad \dim = 3 - 2 = 1">
**Statement** (Choset §5.3.1). With $G = [d_i - d_j,\ d_i - d_k]^\top : \mathbb{R}^3 \to
\mathbb{R}^2$, the set $SS_{ijk}$ is a one-dimensional manifold, provided the sheets $SS_{ij}$
and $SS_{ik}$ intersect transversally.

**Step 1 — each row is nonzero.** On $SS_{ij}$ the first row $\nabla d_i - \nabla d_j$ is nonzero by
definition (Chapter 8: that is what the extra $S$ in $SS$ means); likewise the second row on
$SS_{ik}$.

**Step 2 — rank loss means proportional rows.** A $2 \times 3$ matrix with nonzero rows loses rank
exactly when $\nabla(d_i - d_j) = \alpha\, \nabla(d_i - d_k)$ for some $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Step 3 — proportional rows are a non-transversal crossing.** The rows are the normals of the two
sheets. Proportional normals mean equal tangent planes, $T_q SS_{ij} = T_q SS_{ik}$, whose sum is a
plane and not $\mathbb{R}^3$: the sheets are tangent there. Under the transversality assumption this
does not happen, and if it did, a small perturbation of either sheet would remove it.

**Step 4 — the preimage theorem.** $DG$ has full rank two on $SS_{ijk}$, so $0$ is a regular value
there and $SS_{ijk}$ is a submanifold of dimension $3 - 2 = 1$, with tangent $\ker DG$ — the cross
product of the two rows. $\blacksquare$

*The numbers.* The chapter's synthetic room — a $10 \times 8 \times 3$ box, Choset's Fig. 5.15 —
is explored by the same tracer that runs in the Apartment, with exact plane distances in place of a
scan. From one start it finds the four meet points $(1.5, 1.5, 1.5)$, $(8.5, 1.5, 1.5)$, $(8.5,
6.5, 1.5)$, $(1.5, 6.5, 1.5)$ — each four-way equidistant to $10^{-9}$ and of degree four — and
eight spokes into the corners, thirteen edges in all. Every edge sample is three-way equidistant to
$10^{-6}$, and the smallest singular value of $DG$ along the edges is at least $0.87 =
\sqrt{3 - \sqrt5}$, the value on the rectangle's long edges: comfortably transversal everywhere.
</Derivation>

### Why the GVG falls apart, and the hierarchical GVG

In the room the GVG is connected. Put a box in the middle of the room — Choset's Fig. 5.19 — and it
is not. The outer network is unchanged; but around the box there is a second component, a *halo*:
the loop of points equally far from the floor, the ceiling and the box. No edge of the outer network
touches it.

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — GVG cycle, second-order edge, period, HGVG (Choset §5.3.2, eq. 5.4)">
A **GVG cycle** is a GVG edge homeomorphic to $S^1$. A **second-order GVG edge** on the sheet $F_{ij}$
is $F_{kl}\big|_{F_{ij}} = \{q : d_i = d_j \le d_k = d_l \le d_h\ \forall h \ne i, j, k, l,\
\nabla d_i \ne \nabla d_j,\ \nabla d_k \ne \nabla d_l\}$: $\QO_i, \QO_j$ are the closest pair and
$\QO_k, \QO_l$ the second-closest. Second-order edges end at **second-order meet points**
$F_{klp}\big|_{F_{ij}}$. A **period** is a closed loop of second-order edges sharing a common
second-closest obstacle. The **hierarchical GVG** (HGVG) is the union of the GVG and the
second-order GVG.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="The GVG is generically disconnected, and the HGVG repairs it" result="\dot c = -\pi_{T F_{ij}} \nabla d_k(c) \text{ from a period reaches } d_i = d_j = d_k">
**Statement** (Choset §5.3.2). In a punctured three-dimensional free space there is in general no
one-dimensional deformation retract [Choset ref. 63], so the GVG can be disconnected. The
two-dimensional GVD is connected; GVG edges lie on the boundaries of its sheets, $F_{ijk} =
\partial F_{ij} \cap \partial F_{ik} \cap \partial F_{jk}$; and a path that keeps $d_i = d_j$ while
decreasing $d_k$ leads from a period to the missing cycle.

**Step 1 — the hole.** In the box-in-a-room, the floor–ceiling sheet $F_{fc}$ is the plane halfway
up, with a hole where the box is nearer than the floor and the ceiling. The boundary of that hole is
the halo: the GVG edge $F_{fcb}$, a cycle.

**Step 2 — the trouble is on sheet boundaries.** The GVD is a two-dimensional deformation retract of
the free space and therefore connected. If the boundary of every sheet were connected, the GVG would
be too. The floor–ceiling sheet's boundary is not: its outer rim belongs to the outer network, its
hole to the halo.

**Step 3 — draw a planar GVG on the sheet.** On $F_{fc}$, look at which obstacles are *second*
closest and where two of them tie. Those are the second-order edges of eq. (5.4) — planar GVG edges
drawn on the sheet — and they are one-manifolds by the preimage theorem, because of the gradient
conditions on the second line of (5.4).

**Step 4 — a period announces a cycle.** Around the box the second-order edges whose tied pair
includes the box close into a loop: a period. Its common second-closest obstacle, the box, is the
clue that a GVG component involving the box exists somewhere inside it.

**Step 5 — descend to it.** Starting on the period, follow $\dot c = -\pi_{T_c F_{ij}} \nabla
d_k(c)$: decrease the distance to the box while staying on the sheet. Since $d_k > d_i = d_j$ at the
start, the path reaches $d_i = d_j = d_k$ — a GVG edge — unless the projected gradient vanishes
first, in which case no such edge exists and the robot returns to the period. $\blacksquare$

*Honesty.* Full HGVG connectivity is "tedious and challenging" in Choset's words and is proved in
Choset and Burdick [Choset ref. 106]; this book cites it and does not prove it. The HGVG is
*retract-like*, not a retract. *The numbers.* The check builds Fig. 5.19 with a unit box at the
centre of the $10 \times 8 \times 3$ room. The outer network never touches the box. The explorer,
run on the floor–ceiling sheet with the sheet's own nearest-obstacle query, traces nine
second-order edges; the four whose tied pair includes the box close into a period around it;
descending $-\pi \nabla d_{box}$ from the period reaches $d_{box} = d_{floor} = d_{ceiling} = 1.5$,
and from there the halo traces as a closed GVG cycle $13.42$ m long, three-way equidistant to
$10^{-6}$.
</Derivation>

### Tracing without a predictor: the Lyapunov law

Chapter 3 traced curves with a discrete predictor–corrector: step along the tangent, then Newton
back onto the curve. Choset §5.3.3 replaces both by one velocity,

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-robot}{\dot q} = \alpha\, \htmlClass{term-graph}{v} + \beta\, \htmlClass{term-path}{(DG)^{\dagger} G},
\qquad
v \in \mathrm{Null}(DG),\ \|v\| = 1,
\qquad
(DG)^{\dagger} = DG^{\top} \big(DG\, DG^{\top}\big)^{-1},
$$
</LinkedMath>

with $G = d_1 - d_2$ in the plane and $G = [d_1 - d_2,\ d_1 - d_3]^\top$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$
(eq. 5.5). On the GVG $G = 0$ and the law reduces to $\alpha v$: move along the tangent. Off it, the
second term pulls back — Choset calls $\alpha$ and $\beta$ "spring constants": $\alpha$ is the speed
along the edge, $\beta$ the stiffness of the return. Both terms need only $G$ and $DG$, and
$DG$'s rows are differences of distance gradients — unit vectors pointing back along the shortest
rays. That is the epigraph: the planner can compute $\nabla G$ solely from range sensor information.

<KeyIdea title="Definition box — Lyapunov function (introduced here; Appendix E has the full treatment)">
Let $\dot q = X(q)$ be a dynamical system and $A$ a closed invariant set — here, the GVG. A $C^1$
function $\Gamma \ge 0$ defined on a neighborhood $U$ of $A$ is a **Lyapunov function** for $A$ if
$\Gamma = 0$ exactly on $A$ and $\dot\Gamma = \nabla \Gamma \cdot X < 0$ on $U \setminus A$. Then
$A$ is **asymptotically stable**: trajectories that start close enough to $A$ stay close and converge
to it. Choset's gloss: think of a Lyapunov function as "an error function whose minimal value is
zero". The guarantee is *local* — it holds only where $\dot\Gamma < 0$ has been shown.
</KeyIdea>

</Deferred>

The funnel is the chapter's micro-world: two point obstacles at $(0, 0)$ and $(4, 0)$, whose GVG
is the bisector $x = 2$, and Rusty at $(2.5, 1)$. The shading is $\Gamma = \tfrac12 G^2$ — a trough
along the bisector — and the robot slides down its wall while advancing along its floor. The
sparkline plots $\Gamma(t)$ on the curve $\Gamma_0 e^{2\beta t}$, which the derivation below says it
must follow; slide $\beta$ above zero and the robot climbs out instead. The misconception this
widget kills: *the controller needs the GVG's equation*. It needs $G$ and $DG$ at the point where
the robot is, and nothing else.

<Derivation title="The tracing law is Lyapunov-stable" result="\dot\Gamma = \beta\, G^{\top} G \le 0 \text{ for } \beta < 0,\ \text{strict off the GVG}">
**Statement** (Choset §5.3.3, eq. 5.5). Along $\dot q = \alpha v + \beta (DG)^\dagger G$ with $v \in
\mathrm{Null}(DG)$, the function $\Gamma = \tfrac12 G^\top G$ satisfies $\dot\Gamma = \beta\,
G^\top G$. For $\beta < 0$ it is a Lyapunov function for the GVG wherever $DG\, DG^\top$ is
invertible, so the robot converges onto the GVG.

**Step 1 — the chain rule.** $\dot\Gamma = G^\top \dot G = G^\top DG\, \dot q$.

**Step 2 — the tangent term vanishes.** $DG\, (\alpha v) = \alpha\, DG\, v = 0$, because $v$ lies in
the null space of $DG$. Moving along the edge does not change $G$ to first order.

**Step 3 — the pseudoinverse is a right inverse.** $DG\, (DG)^\dagger = DG\, DG^\top (DG\,
DG^\top)^{-1} = I$, so $G^\top DG\, \beta (DG)^\dagger G = \beta\, G^\top G$.

**Step 4 — the sign.** $\dot\Gamma = \beta\, G^\top G = 2 \beta\, \Gamma \le 0$ for $\beta < 0$,
with equality only where $G = 0$, on the GVG. In fact $\Gamma(t) = \Gamma(0)\, e^{2\beta t}$ exactly
along the continuous flow.

**Step 5 — the neighborhood.** All of this needs $(DG\, DG^\top)^{-1}$. Near the interior of a GVG
edge the rows of $DG$ are independent (Derivation 1), and they stay independent in a neighborhood
[Choset ref. 108] — the neighborhood the definition box asks for. $\blacksquare$

*Where it stops holding.* The argument needs two things: a fixed set of closest obstacles, so that
$G$ is smooth, and an invertible $DG\, DG^\top$. Both fail at meet points. There a further obstacle
ties, the identity of the "closest" ones switches, $G$ is not even continuous across the switch, and
the Lyapunov argument has nothing to say; and wherever two gradients become parallel — a
non-transversal point — $DG\, DG^\top$ is singular and the pseudoinverse does not exist. The tracer's
guarantee is local, and at meet points a different law, homing, takes over.
*The sign of $v$* is the planner's choice (Choset's footnote 4): the null space is a line and the
tracer keeps the direction that continues the previous heading. *Sensing $\nabla G$*: fit a
codimension-one plane through the $n$ closest points on the $n$ closest obstacles; the tangent is
its normal. In the plane, $\nabla d_1 - \nabla d_2$ is perpendicular to the chord through the two
closest points, and the tangent is along the chord's perpendicular — Chapter 8's Fig. 5.13.
</Derivation>

### Meet-point homing

<Derivation title="Homing is the same law with an empty null space" result="\dot q = \beta\, (DG)^{-1} G \;\to\; \text{the centre of the circle (sphere) through the closest points}">
**Statement** (Choset §5.3.3). With $G = [d_1 - d_2,\ d_1 - d_3]^\top$ in the plane (three rows,
$[d_1 - d_2, d_1 - d_3, d_1 - d_4]^\top$, in $\mathbb{R}^3$), $DG$ is square, $\mathrm{Null}(DG) =
\{0\}$, and $\dot q = \beta (DG)^\dagger G = \beta\, DG^{-1} G$ converges to the meet point.

**Step 1 — Derivation 3 with $v = 0$.** The computation of $\dot\Gamma$ never used $v$ except to
kill it; with $v = 0$ it reads $\dot\Gamma = \beta\, G^\top G$ unchanged.

**Step 2 — $G = 0$ only at the meet point.** Two independent equations in the plane, three in
$\mathbb{R}^3$, cut out isolated points: the meet points, where $m + 1$ obstacles tie.

**Step 3 — the geometry of the step.** Linearize each $d_i$ about $q$: the step $-DG^{-1} G$ goes to
the point where the linearized distances agree, the centre of the circle through the three closest
points (the sphere through four in $\mathbb{R}^3$). Choset: "The velocity vector points toward the
center of this sphere." $\blacksquare$

*Higher-order laws* come from swapping $G$: a second-order edge uses $G = [d_i - d_j,\ d_k -
d_l]^\top$, a second-order meet point $[d_i - d_j,\ d_k - d_l,\ d_k - d_p]^\top$. *The numbers.* On
Chapter 8's slab — pegs at $(0, 0)$ and $(4, 0)$, a wall along $y = 4$ — homing from three seeds
converges to the meet point $(2, 1.5)$ with clearance $2.5$ to $10^{-9}$. For the wall the
linearization is exact; for the pegs it is first order, so the aim sharpens with distance: from
$0.57$ m away the homing velocity points within $45°$ of the meet point, from $0.06$ m away within
$3°$.
</Derivation>

### Beyond a point robot: the rod

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — rod, rod distance, R-edge, piecewise retract (Choset §5.4)">
A **rod** is a line segment moving in the plane; its configuration space is $SE(2)$, three
dimensional. Distance is measured *in the workspace*: $d_i(q) = \min_{r \in R(q),\ c \in \WO_i}
d(r, c)$, which is what sensors on the rod measure. The **rod two-equidistant face** is $RF_{ij} =
\{q \in SE(2) : d_i(q) = d_j(q),\ \nabla d_i(q) \ne \nabla d_j(q)\}$, and a **rod-GVG edge** is
$RF_{ijk} = RF_{ij} \cap RF_{ik} \cap RF_{jk}$. An **R-edge** is the set of rod placements tangent to
the planar point-GVG. The **rod-HGVG**, rod-GVG edges joined by R-edges, is a **piecewise retract**:
a union of deformation retracts of *junction regions* $J_{ijk}$, linked.
</KeyIdea>

The rod-GVG edges play the role of meet points, but each is a whole curve of orientations: a small
rod can spin a full turn while staying three-way equidistant, so $RF_{ijk}$ is homeomorphic to
$S^1$ (Choset's footnote 5). The R-edges inherit adjacency from the plane. The chapter keeps the rod
to a definition and a check — Exercise 6 builds it — and the check confirms the first claim: a
$0.3$ m rod centred near the Workbench's widest meet point (clearance $1.539$) stays three-way
equidistant through all $36$ orientations sampled, Newton in $(x, y)$ at fixed $\theta$ converging
to $10^{-10}$, and $130$ R-edge placements are sampled from Chapter 8's point-GVD.

### Silhouettes: Canny's roadmap

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — slice, critical slice, silhouette (Choset §5.5, §5.5.1)">
The **slice** at $\lambda$ is $Q_\lambda = \pi_1^{-1}(\lambda) = \{q : q_1 = \lambda\}$; sweeping
$\lambda$ sweeps it through $\Q$. On each slice take the extrema of $\pi_2(q) = q_2$ restricted to
the free-space boundary in the slice. The traces of those extrema as $\lambda$ varies are the
**silhouette curves**. A **critical slice** is one where the number of silhouette curves changes;
its $\lambda$ is a **critical value**, and the points where a silhouette curve is tangent to the
critical slice are **critical points**. On each critical slice the algorithm recurses one dimension
down, sweeping along $q_2$ and extremizing $q_3$, until no critical points remain or the slice is
one-dimensional — and "the roadmap of a one-dimensional set is the set itself". The union of all
silhouettes is **Canny's roadmap**.
</KeyIdea>

<KeyIdea title="Lemma 5.5.1 (Lagrange multiplier) and Lemma 5.5.2 (generalized, Canny)">
If $S = f^{-1}(c) \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with $\nabla f \ne 0$ on $S$, and $p$ is an extremum of a
smooth $h$ on $S$, then $\nabla h(p) = \mu\, \nabla f(p)$ for some $\mu$. For vector-valued $f :
\mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^p$ and $h : \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$, $x$ is a critical point of
$h|_{f^{-1}(\cdot)}$ if and only if the stacked differential $D(f, h)(x)$ — the rows of $Df$, then
the rows of $Dh$ — loses rank. **Slice lemma:** $\Sigma(\pi_{12}|_S) = \bigcup_\lambda \Sigma\big(\pi_2|_{\pi_1^{-1}(\lambda)}\big)$ — the silhouette is the critical set of $\pi_{12}$.
</KeyIdea>

</Deferred>

The sweep makes the definitions concrete on three bodies. The **sphere** has one silhouette, its
equator — traced as two curves, the maxima and the minima of $q_2$ — born at $\lambda = -r$ and dying
at $\lambda = r$. The **torus** lying flat ($R = 2$, $r =
0.7$) is Choset's Figs. 5.31–5.32: the slice is one closed curve for $|\lambda| > R - r$ and two for
$|\lambda| < R - r$, so the critical values are $\pm 2.7$ (birth and death) and $\pm 1.3$ (pinch).
Its silhouette is four arcs — the $q_2 > 0$ and $q_2 < 0$ halves of the outer and inner equators — and
they are not connected to one another: four components. The linking curves on the two pinch slices join them
into one. The **ellipsoid with a bent hole** is Fig. 5.25 simplified: an ellipsoid with semi-axes
$3$, $2$ and $1.5$ and a hole of radius $0.7$ drilled along $q_3$ at $q_1 = 0.6$, its axis bending as $y =
0.5 z^2$. The bend matters. With a straight hole the wall is a cylinder, $q_2$ is constant along its
vertical lines, and the extrema are degenerate — not Morse. Bent, each slice's wall curve is a
parabola with one nondegenerate extremum. Choset describes the silhouette as the ellipsoid's
equator, the perimeter of the hole and the two curves along its side; the sweep finds it in six
pieces, and the linking curves join them into one.
That is the misconception the widget kills: *the silhouette is not the outline* of the body as you
see it. It is the critical set of a projection, and it lives where the surface's tangent plane
contains the $q_3$ direction.

<LinkedMath>
$$
D(f, \pi_{12})(q) = \begin{bmatrix} 2q_1 & 2q_2 & 2q_3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\det D(f, \pi_{12}) = 2 q_3 = 0 \iff \htmlClass{term-graph}{q_3 = 0}\ \text{(the equator)}.
$$
</LinkedMath>

<Derivation title="Silhouettes are critical sets, and connectivity changes only at critical points" result="\Sigma(\pi_{12}|_S) = \textstyle\bigcup_\lambda \Sigma(\pi_2|_{Q_\lambda}),\quad \text{slice topology constant between adjacent critical values}">
**Statement** (Choset §5.5.1; Lemmas 5.5.1–5.5.2, the slice lemma, Morse theory [Choset ref.
315]). For $S = f^{-1}(0)$, a point $x \in S$ is on the silhouette iff $D(f, \pi_{12})(x)$ loses
rank; the silhouette is the union over $\lambda$ of the critical points of $\pi_2$ on each slice; and
between adjacent critical values the slice topology does not change, so the silhouette's
connectivity can change only at critical slices.

**Step 1 — Lagrange.** At an extremum $p$ of $h$ on $f^{-1}(c)$, every direction tangent to the
surface leaves $h$ stationary to first order, so $\nabla h(p)$ has no tangential component:
$\nabla h(p) = \mu \nabla f(p)$ (Lemma 5.5.1).

**Step 2 — stacking.** For vector-valued constraints and functions the same condition says the rows
of $Dh$ are combinations of the rows of $Df$ on the tangent space — rank loss of the stacked matrix
$D(f, h)$ (Lemma 5.5.2). For two real-valued functions, rank loss of a two-row matrix *is*
parallelism, and Lemma 5.5.1 is recovered.

**Step 3 — the slice lemma.** Fixing $q_1 = \lambda$ and extremizing $q_2$ on the slice is the same
as asking that the map $q \mapsto (q_1, q_2)$ restricted to $S$ fail to be a submersion: the rows
$e_1$ (the slice) and $e_2$ (the extremized coordinate) together with $\nabla f$ lose rank. So
$\Sigma(\pi_{12}|_S) = \bigcup_\lambda \Sigma(\pi_2|_{\pi_1^{-1}(\lambda)})$.

**Step 4 — the sphere.** For $f = q_1^2 + q_2^2 + q_3^2 - r^2$ the stacked matrix above has
determinant $2 q_3$, zero exactly on the equator. The check sweeps the unit sphere: every
silhouette sample has $|q_3| < 10^{-6}$ and $|\det| = 2|q_3| < 10^{-6}$ there, while at $(0, 0.6,
0.8)$, off the equator, $|\det| = 1.6$; the only critical slices are birth and death at $\lambda =
\pm 1$.

**Step 5 — critical slices are where the roadmap's tangent lies in the slice.** At a critical point
of $\pi_1$ restricted to the roadmap, the roadmap's tangent is orthogonal to $\nabla \pi_1 = e_1$:
it lies in the slice. There the number of silhouette pieces can change, and by Morse theory it can
change *only* there: between adjacent critical values a diffeomorphism carries one slice's
intersection with $S$ to the next (Choset Fig. 5.33). $\blacksquare$

*The recursion.* Each critical slice is a lower-dimensional problem of the same kind: sweep it along
$q_2$, extremize $q_3$, find its critical slices, recurse, down to dimension one. Accessibility and
departability come from treating the slices through $\qstart$ and $\qgoal$ as critical, and
connectivity is proved by induction on dimension [Choset refs. 90–92]. The demonstrator in this
chapter recurses once: in a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ a critical slice meets the surface in curves,
and those curves *are* their own roadmap — the linking curves. *The numbers.* The torus sweep finds
its critical values at $-2.7, -1.3, 1.3, 2.7$ to $2 \times 10^{-3}$, with two slice curves and four
extrema just inside $|\lambda| = 1.3$ and one curve with two extrema just outside, and four silhouette
components that the linking curves join into one. The bent-hole ellipsoid has six critical values,
at $-2.999$, $-0.122$, $-0.109$, $1.307$, $1.322$ and $2.992$. The slice-curve count changes exactly
twice, where the hole punches through at $0.6 - 0.7$ and closes at $0.6 + 0.7$; the smoothing of
the rim (the solid is a smooth maximum of the ellipsoid and the tube, so that Newton has a
gradient) adds a "dimple" critical slice just outside each. Midway through the hole the slice is two
curves with six extrema. Six silhouette components, one with the linking curves.
</Derivation>

Choset's ellipsoid has four critical points, $Cp_1$ to $Cp_4$, because his hole goes down and comes
back up, punching through the slice twice on the way in and twice on the way out. Ours goes straight
through, so it has two; the honest bookkeeping above is what the sweep finds, not what the figure
shows.

### The opportunistic path planner and the generalized gradient

Canny's silhouettes extremize a coordinate. Canny and Lin's **opportunistic path planner** (OPP)
extremizes something a robot cares about: the distance to the nearest obstacle, restricted to the
slice.

<KeyIdea title="Definitions — freeway, channel, bridge curve, interesting critical point (Choset §5.5.2, eqs. 5.7–5.8)">
Write $q = [\lambda, p]^\top$ with $p$ the slice coordinates. The **slice-restricted distances** are
$\tilde d_i(p; \lambda) = d_i(q)$ and $\tilde D(p; \lambda) = \min_i \tilde d_i(p; \lambda)$. A
**freeway** is the trace of the local maxima of $\tilde D$ on the slices as $\lambda$ sweeps. A
**channel** is a connected component of the free part of a slice. An **interesting critical point**
is where channels join or split; a **bridge curve** joins neighbouring freeways near an interesting
critical point, or leads away from a **bifurcation point** where a freeway ends in free space. The
union of freeways and bridges is the OPP roadmap.
</KeyIdea>

<KeyIdea title="Definition — generalized gradient (Choset §5.5.2, after Clarke)">
Where $D = \min_i d_i$ is not differentiable — at ties — its **generalized gradient** is the convex
hull of the active gradients,
$\partial D(q^\star) = \mathrm{Co}\{\nabla d_i(q^\star) : i \in Z(q^\star)\}$,
with $Z(q^\star)$ the indices of the closest obstacles. If $0 \in \partial D(q^\star)$ then
$q^\star$ is a local maximum; if $\partial D(q^\star) = \{0\}$, a local minimum. No second
derivative is needed: first-order information characterizes the extremum.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="OPP freeways are ties, characterized by the generalized gradient" result="q^\star \text{ a slice maximum of } \tilde D \iff 0 \in \mathrm{Co}\{\partial \tilde d_i / \partial p : i \in Z(q^\star)\}">
**Statement** (Choset §5.5.2). Slice maxima of $\tilde D$ trace freeways joined by bridges near
interesting critical points. Since $\tilde D$ is nonsmooth at ties, a maximum is characterized by
$0 \in \partial \tilde D$; for convex obstacles every freeway point is a tie, hence a GVD point.

**Step 1 — smooth between ties.** On a slice, $\tilde D = \tilde d_i$ wherever $\QO_i$ is the unique
closest obstacle, and it inherits the smoothness of $\tilde d_i$ there (Choset Fig. 5.37).

**Step 2 — at a tie, the convex hull.** Approaching a tie from either side gives the limiting
gradients $\nabla \tilde d_i$ of the tied obstacles; the generalized gradient is their convex hull.

**Step 3 — a maximum means no ascent direction.** At a slice maximum no direction along the slice
increases every active $\tilde d_i$; by a separating-hyperplane argument that is exactly $0 \in
\mathrm{Co}\{\nabla \tilde d_i\}$. On a one-dimensional slice: the active slopes bracket zero.

**Step 4 — bridges at merges and splits.** Freeways are traces of maxima *within a channel*; where
two channels merge or split — where the slice's normal $\nabla h$ is parallel to an obstacle's
surface normal, the rank loss of $D(f, h)$ again (Choset Fig. 5.35) — maxima appear or vanish, and a
bridge along the slice joins them. $\blacksquare$

*Problem 12, for convex obstacles.* Each $d_i$ is convex when $\QO_i$ is (Choset Problem 19), so its
restriction to a slice is convex and has no interior maximum; a maximum of $\tilde D$ must therefore
be a tie of two or more obstacles with slopes of both signs — a point of the GVD, for *any* slice
direction. Exercise 2 asks for the general proof. *The numbers.* In the chapter's tilted $10 \times 6$
room with two convex obstacles, the sweep finds $181$ freeway points on $4$ freeways; at every one of
them the active slopes bracket zero and two or more obstacles tie. The channel count changes at the
interesting critical values $\lambda = 2.2, 4.6, 6.0, 8.2$, and the $10$ bridges there and at the
bifurcation points connect the four freeways into one roadmap. The room's walls are tilted on
purpose: a slice parallel to a wall makes $\tilde D$ constant along it, every point a degenerate
maximum, and the Morse assumption fails — the tilt is the general-position fix.
</Derivation>

### What exactness costs

Canny's algorithm was the first general roadmap algorithm, and with it came the complexity bound
that made roadmap theory a theory: for a configuration space of dimension $n$ whose obstacles are
bounded by $p$ polynomials of degree at most $w$, any path-planning query can be answered in

<LinkedMath>
$$
p^{\,n} (\log p)\, w^{O(n^4)}
$$
</LinkedMath>

time [Choset refs. 90–92]. That is *singly* exponential in $n$, where the earlier general method of
Schwartz and Sharir — cylindrical algebraic decomposition — was doubly exponential. It is still
enormous. Taking the constant in the $O(\cdot)$ as one, $p = 10$ and $w = 2$, the base-ten logarithm
of the bound is $7.2$ for $n = 2$, $27.7$ for $n = 3$, $81.4$ for $n = 4$ and $396.5$ for $n = 6$ — a
six-joint arm among quadric obstacles: more operations than there are atoms in the observable
universe, by over three hundred orders of magnitude. And no algorithm can do fundamentally better in
general: Reif showed that the generalized mover's problem — many independently moving parts — is
PSPACE-hard (Appendix D). Exact, complete planning in high dimensions is not going to get cheap.

Canny's roadmap is complete, and it has never been a practical planner; the opportunistic planner,
which is, gives up nothing of the theory but runs on a sweep that still scales with the volume of
the space. The honest reading is that this chapter's exact structures are tools for low
dimensions and for *understanding*: the GVG for a mobile robot that must explore, silhouettes as the
reason sweep-based coverage works (Chapter 10), critical points as the place where everything
interesting happens. For a six-joint arm, Part III trades completeness for probabilistic
completeness and the problem becomes tractable.

## The algorithm

<Algorithm
  name="SENSOR-BASED GVG EXPLORATION  —  Choset §5.2.5, §5.3.3 steps (1)–(5)"
  inputs="a nearest-obstacle query (a range ring's local minima of ρ with their bearings, or exact geometry); gains α > 0, β < 0; step dt; tolerances meetTol, boundaryTol, nodeTol"
  outputs="a graph whose nodes are meet, access and boundary points and whose edges are the driven polylines"
  complexity="one traversal per GVG edge plus the backtracking of a depth-first search; one scan and one 2×2 (3×3) solve per step"
>
1. **access**: drive along $\nabla d_1$ until $d_2 - d_1 <$ `accessTol`; in $\mathbb{R}^3$ continue along the two-equidistant sheet, increasing $d$, until a third reading ties. Record an access node with two departure slots
2. **depart**: pick an unexplored slot at the current node, step `departStep` along it, start a new edge
3. **trace**: apply the Lyapunov law $\dot q = \alpha v + \beta (DG)^\dagger G$ with $G$ from the $m$ nearest readings and $v$ signed to continue the heading; append $q$ to the edge; log $\Gamma$
4. **detect**: if the $(m+1)$-th reading comes within `meetTol` of the first — "a sudden change in one of the two closest obstacles" — go to **home**; if $d_1 <$ `boundaryTol`, close the edge at a boundary point and go to **backtrack**
5. **home**: Newton on $G = [d_1 - d_2, \ldots, d_1 - d_{m+1}]$, i.e. the homing law with $\beta\,dt = -1$, to `homeTol`; if a node lies within `nodeTol`, close the edge there and mark the arriving slot explored; otherwise create a meet node with the $m + 1$ departure directions (each omits one obstacle, signed so the omitted one falls behind) and close the edge there
6. **branch**: if the node has an unexplored slot go to **depart**; else **backtrack**
7. **backtrack**: breadth-first search over known edges to the nearest node with an unexplored slot; drive its polylines; go to **depart**. If no such node exists: **done**

*Rejected ties.* A homing that converges somewhere other than a three-way (four-way) tie, or a tie
whose gradients coincide, is reported as spurious and tracing resumes after a short cooldown — the
"sudden change" detector is a heuristic, and with noisy ranges it fires early, late or twice.
*Degenerate meet points.* When four or more readings tie to within the sensor's resolution — the
Apartment's mirrored doorways produce meet points two millimetres apart — the node gets one departure
per *adjacent* pair of closest points around the robot.
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="LYAPUNOV TRACER AND MEET-POINT HOMING  —  Choset eq. (5.5), §5.3.3"
  inputs="the m (edge) or m + 1 (homing) nearest readings d_i, ∇d_i; gains α, β; the previous heading"
  outputs="the commanded velocity q̇ and Γ = ½GᵀG"
  complexity="O(m³) for the (m−1)×(m−1) or m×m inverse"
>
1. $G \leftarrow [d_1 - d_2, \ldots, d_1 - d_k]$, $DG \leftarrow$ rows $(\nabla d_1 - \nabla d_j)^\top$
2. $(DG)^\dagger \leftarrow DG^\top (DG\, DG^\top)^{-1}$; **if** $DG\, DG^\top$ is singular **return** "undefined" (a meet point for the edge law, or parallel gradients)
3. **if** $k = m$: $v \leftarrow$ unit vector of $\mathrm{Null}(DG)$ — the perpendicular of the row in the plane, the cross product of the rows in $\mathbb{R}^3$ — negated if $v \cdot$ heading $< 0$; **else** $v \leftarrow 0$
4. **return** $\dot q = \alpha v + \beta (DG)^\dagger G$ and $\Gamma = \tfrac12 G^\top G$; along it $\dot\Gamma = \beta G^\top G$
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="HGVG PERIOD FOLLOWING  —  Choset §5.3.2"
  inputs="a sheet F_ij (here the floor–ceiling plane z = h/2) with its nearest-obstacle query; a candidate obstacle k"
  outputs="a period around k, a link to the GVG cycle, the cycle"
  complexity="one planar exploration of the sheet; one projected-gradient descent"
>
1. explore the planar GVG of the sheet — the second-order edges $F_{kl}|_{F_{ij}}$ — with the exploration algorithm above, using the sheet's own query (obstacles $i, j$ left out)
2. collect the second-order edges whose tied pair includes $k$; if they close into a loop around $k$ (angular gaps small), it is a **period**
3. from a point of the period, follow $\dot c = -\pi_{T F_{ij}} \nabla d_k(c)$ until $d_k = d_i = d_j$ (stop: a GVG edge) or $\|\pi_{T F_{ij}} \nabla d_k\| \to 0$ (stop: no edge; return to the period)
4. trace the GVG edge reached; if it closes on itself, it is the missing **GVG cycle**; link it to the period
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="CANNY'S ROADMAP (demonstrator)  —  Choset §5.5.1"
  inputs="an implicit surface S = f⁻¹(0) in R³ with ∇f; a box; the number of slices"
  outputs="silhouette curves, critical slices with their before/after signatures, linking curves; component counts"
  complexity="general algorithm: p^n (log p) w^{O(n⁴)}; the demonstrator: one 2-D curve trace per slice"
>
1. **for** $\lambda$ on a grid over the $q_1$-range: trace every closed curve of $f(\lambda, p) = 0$ with Chapter 3's predictor–corrector, seeded from a lattice and deduplicated
2. on each curve, locate the extrema of $q_2$ as the sign changes of $\partial f / \partial q_3$ (the tangent $(-f_3, f_2)$ has no $q_2$-component there — Lemma 5.5.2), refined by bisection along the chord with Newton projection
3. **signature** of a slice = (number of curves, number of extrema); between grid values with different signatures, bisect on $\lambda$ to locate the **critical value**
4. chain the extrema across slices into **silhouette curves** (nearest neighbour, maxima with maxima)
5. **recurse once**: on each critical slice the roadmap of the one-dimensional intersection is the intersection itself — take the slice curves just either side of $\lambda^\star$ as **linking curves**
6. count connected components of the silhouette alone and with the linking curves
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="OPPORTUNISTIC PATH PLANNER  —  Choset §5.5.2 (Canny and Lin)"
  inputs="a planar polygonal world in general position; a slice direction"
  outputs="freeways, interesting critical values, bridge curves"
  complexity="one 1-D maximization per slice; bisection on the channel count"
>
1. **for** each slice $x = \lambda$: split the slice into **channels**; on each, find the strict local maxima of $\tilde D$ by sampling and golden-section refinement
2. at each maximum record the active set $Z$ and the slopes $\partial \tilde d_i / \partial y$, and test $0 \in \mathrm{Co}\{\cdot\}$
3. chain maxima across slices into **freeways**
4. locate **interesting critical values** by bisection where the channel count changes; on the merged side, add the slice segment joining the freeway points of the splitting channels as a **bridge**
5. where a freeway ends in free space (a bifurcation point), bridge it along its last slice to the nearest freeway point in the same channel
6. access and departure: slice-constrained gradient ascent of $\tilde D$ from $\qstart$ and $\qgoal$
</Algorithm>

## Implementation in Rust

This chapter adds the `gvg` submodule to Chapter 8's `roadmap` crate: `scan.rs` turns a range ring
into the $k$ nearest obstacles, `tracer.rs` is eq. (5.5), `explore.rs` the five-step exploration,
`hgvg.rs` the period following, `canny.rs` the silhouette demonstrator and `opp.rs` the freeways. The
tracer is written once for any number of readings; const generics carry the sizes, and the
pseudoinverse returns `None` exactly where the Lyapunov argument stops holding.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvg/tracer.rs" {16-20,31-38}
use nalgebra::{SMatrix, SVector, Vector2};

/// The k nearest hits in a range ring: distance and unit direction away from each (C §4.3.1).
/// `obstacle_id` is `None` from a scan — the sensor cannot name what it sees, and the law never asks.
pub struct Nearest<const K: usize> {
    pub d: [f64; K],
    pub grad: [Vector2<f64>; K],
    pub obstacle_id: [Option<usize>; K],
}

pub struct Twist { pub v: Vector2<f64> }
pub struct GvgTracer { pub alpha: f64, pub beta: f64 }

/// DG† = DGᵀ (DG DGᵀ)⁻¹ for a full-row-rank differential. `None` where the Gram matrix is singular:
/// two sheet normals parallel (a non-transversal crossing) or, for the edge law, a meet point —
/// exactly where Lyapunov's neighborhood ends and homing has to take over.
pub fn pseudo_inverse<const R: usize, const C: usize>(dg: &SMatrix<f64, R, C>) -> Option<SMatrix<f64, C, R>> {
    let gram = dg * dg.transpose();
    gram.try_inverse().map(|inv| dg.transpose() * inv)
}

/// G and DG from the first R + 1 readings: rows d₁ − d_j and ∇d₁ − ∇d_j.
fn constraint<const K: usize, const R: usize>(n: &Nearest<K>) -> (SVector<f64, R>, SMatrix<f64, R, 2>) {
    let g = SVector::<f64, R>::from_fn(|j, _| n.d[0] - n.d[j + 1]);
    let dg = SMatrix::<f64, R, 2>::from_fn(|j, c| n.grad[0][c] - n.grad[j + 1][c]);
    (g, dg)
}

impl GvgTracer {
    /// Edge tracing, C eq. (5.5): q̇ = α v + β (DG)† G with v ∈ Null(DG), ‖v‖ = 1, signed to keep
    /// going the way we were going (Choset's footnote 4). Also returns Γ = ½ GᵀG; Γ̇ = β GᵀG.
    pub fn trace_step(&self, n: &Nearest<2>, heading: Vector2<f64>) -> Option<(Twist, f64)> {
        let (g, dg) = constraint::<2, 1>(n);
        let dg_pinv = pseudo_inverse(&dg)?;
        let row = dg.row(0);
        let mut v = Vector2::new(-row[1], row[0]).normalize();
        if v.dot(&heading) < 0.0 { v = -v; }
        let correction = self.beta * (dg_pinv * g);
        Some((Twist { v: self.alpha * v + correction }, 0.5 * g.norm_squared()))
    }

    /// Meet-point homing: DG is square, Null(DG) = {0}, so the law is a Newton-like step
    /// β DG⁻¹ G toward the centre of the circle through the three closest points.
    pub fn home_step(&self, n: &Nearest<3>) -> Option<(Twist, f64)> {
        let (g, dg) = constraint::<3, 2>(n);
        let dg_pinv = pseudo_inverse(&dg)?;
        Some((Twist { v: self.beta * (dg_pinv * g) }, 0.5 * g.norm_squared()))
    }

    /// Access (Ch. 8, Lemma 5.2.1): climb ∇d₁ until the second reading ties. Unit speed, so the
    /// ascent takes d₂ − d₁ seconds at most from any start in a bounded room.
    pub fn access_step(&self, n: &Nearest<2>) -> Twist {
        Twist { v: self.alpha * n.grad[0] }
    }
}
```

The tracer returns `Option` rather than a bare `(Twist, f64)` so that the one place the derivation
fails — a singular $DG\, DG^\top$ — is a value the caller must handle, not a
`NaN` that drives the robot into a wall. The explorer is a state machine around the tracer, and its
graph is `petgraph`'s.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvg/explore.rs" {28-40}
use petgraph::graph::{NodeIndex, UnGraph};

pub struct MeetNode { pub q: Vector2<f64>, pub clearance: f64, pub departures: Vec<Vector2<f64>>, pub explored: Vec<bool> }
pub struct Edge { pub polyline: Vec<Vector2<f64>>, pub length: f64 }
/// The incrementally built topological map; `frontier` lists (node, slot) pairs not yet driven.
pub struct GvgGraph { pub graph: UnGraph<MeetNode, Edge>, pub frontier: Vec<(NodeIndex, usize)> }
pub enum Event { OnEdge, MeetPoint(NodeIndex), BoundaryPoint, Done }
enum Phase { Access, Depart, Trace, Home, Backtrack(Vec<Vector2<f64>>), Done }

pub struct Explorer<'r> {
    pub robot: &'r mut sim::Rusty,
    pub tracer: GvgTracer,
    pub graph: GvgGraph,
    phase: Phase, heading: Vector2<f64>, edge: Vec<Vector2<f64>>, from: NodeIndex,
    pub gamma_log: Vec<f64>,
}

impl Explorer<'_> {
    /// One tick: scan; access / trace / home by phase; DFS to the next unexplored slot.
    pub fn tick(&mut self, dt: f64) -> Event {
        let near: Nearest<3> = scan::nearest_from_scan(&self.robot.scan());
        match &self.phase {
            Phase::Access => {
                if near.d[1] - near.d[0] > ACCESS_TOL { self.drive(self.tracer.access_step(&near.first2()), dt); return Event::OnEdge; }
                self.from = self.add_node(self.robot.position(), access_departures(&near));
                self.phase = Phase::Depart;
                Event::OnEdge
            }
            Phase::Trace => {
                // "A sudden change in one of the two closest obstacles": a third reading closes in.
                if near.d[2] - near.d[0] < MEET_TOL { self.phase = Phase::Home; return Event::OnEdge; }
                if near.d[0] < BOUNDARY_TOL { return self.close_at_boundary(); }
                let Some((tw, gamma)) = self.tracer.trace_step(&near.first2(), self.heading) else {
                    self.phase = Phase::Home; // DG DGᵀ singular: we are at (or past) a meet point
                    return Event::OnEdge;
                };
                self.gamma_log.push(gamma);
                self.drive(tw, dt);
                self.edge.push(self.robot.position());
                Event::OnEdge
            }
            Phase::Home => match newton_home(&mut *self.robot, &self.tracer, HOME_TOL) {
                Some(q) => {
                    let node = self.node_near(q, NODE_TOL).unwrap_or_else(|| self.add_meet_node(q, &near));
                    self.close_edge(node);
                    self.next_from(node);
                    Event::MeetPoint(node)
                }
                None => { self.phase = Phase::Trace; Event::OnEdge } // spurious tie: carry on
            },
            Phase::Depart => self.depart(dt),
            Phase::Backtrack(_) => self.follow_backtrack(dt),
            Phase::Done => Event::Done,
        }
    }

    /// DFS bookkeeping: an unexplored slot here, or BFS over known edges to the nearest node with one.
    fn next_from(&mut self, n: NodeIndex) {
        self.phase = if self.graph.graph[n].explored.iter().any(|e| !e) { Phase::Depart }
            else { match self.path_to_frontier(n) { Some(p) => Phase::Backtrack(p), None => Phase::Done } };
    }
}
```

Canny's demonstrator works on implicit surfaces. Each slice is a planar curve-tracing problem, so
Chapter 3's tracer does the work; the extrema are the sign changes of one partial derivative.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvg/canny.rs" {20-27}
use nalgebra::{SVector, Vector2};

pub struct Silhouette {
    pub curves: Vec<Vec<SVector<f64, 3>>>,
    pub critical_points: Vec<SVector<f64, 3>>,
    pub linking: Vec<Vec<SVector<f64, 3>>>,
    pub depth: usize,
}
struct Slice { lambda: f64, pieces: Vec<Vec<Vector2<f64>>>, extrema: Vec<(SVector<f64, 3>, bool)> }

/// Sweep π₁ slices; π₂-extrema where D(f, π₁₂) = [∇f; e₁; e₂] loses rank, i.e. ∂f/∂q₃ = 0
/// (Lemma 5.5.2); recurse once on the critical slices, where the (curves, extrema) count changes.
pub fn canny(f: &dyn Fn(SVector<f64, 3>) -> f64, grad_f: &dyn Fn(SVector<f64, 3>) -> SVector<f64, 3>,
             bounds: [(f64, f64); 3], n_slices: usize) -> Silhouette {
    let lambdas: Vec<f64> = (0..=n_slices).map(|k| bounds[0].0 + (bounds[0].1 - bounds[0].0) * k as f64 / n_slices as f64).collect();
    let slices: Vec<Slice> = lambdas.iter().map(|&l| slice(f, grad_f, l, bounds)).collect();
    let mut sil = Silhouette { curves: chain_extrema(&slices), critical_points: vec![], linking: vec![], depth: 1 };
    for w in slices.windows(2) {
        if signature(&w[0]) == signature(&w[1]) { continue; }
        // Bisect on λ for the critical value; the slice there is pinched, so the linking curves
        // are taken just either side — the recursion's roadmap of a one-dimensional set is the set.
        let l_star = bisect_signature(f, grad_f, bounds, w[0].lambda, w[1].lambda, 8);
        let eps = 0.5 * (w[1].lambda - w[0].lambda);
        for l in [l_star - eps, l_star + eps] {
            sil.linking.extend(slice(f, grad_f, l, bounds).pieces.into_iter().map(|c| lift(l, &c)));
        }
        sil.critical_points.push(changed_extremum(&w[0], &w[1], l_star));
        sil.depth = 2;
    }
    sil
}

/// The curves of f(λ, ·) = 0 by Chapter 3's predictor–corrector, and the sign changes of ∂f/∂q₃
/// along each: there the tangent (−f₃, f₂) has no q₂-component, so q₂ is extremal.
fn slice(f: &dyn Fn(SVector<f64, 3>) -> f64, grad_f: &dyn Fn(SVector<f64, 3>) -> SVector<f64, 3>,
         lambda: f64, bounds: [(f64, f64); 3]) -> Slice {
    let g = |p: Vector2<f64>| f(SVector::<f64, 3>::new(lambda, p.x, p.y));
    let dg = |p: Vector2<f64>| { let n = grad_f(SVector::<f64, 3>::new(lambda, p.x, p.y)); Vector2::new(n[1], n[2]) };
    let pieces = trace_all_closed(&g, &dg, bounds[1], bounds[2], bugs::TraceParams { step: 0.03, ..Default::default() });
    let f3 = |p: &Vector2<f64>| grad_f(SVector::<f64, 3>::new(lambda, p.x, p.y))[2];
    let extrema = pieces.iter().flat_map(|c| sign_changes(c, f3).map(|(p, is_max)| (lift_point(lambda, p), is_max))).collect();
    Slice { lambda, pieces, extrema }
}
```

The opportunistic planner's one new idea fits in a function: a slice maximum of a nonsmooth
minimum is a convex-hull test on first derivatives.

```rust title="crates/roadmap/src/gvg/opp.rs"
/// 0 ∈ ∂D̃(q*) = Co{∂d̃_i/∂y : i ∈ Z(q*)} on a vertical slice: the active slopes bracket zero.
/// For one active obstacle this is the smooth condition ∂d̃/∂y = 0; at a tie it is a kink maximum.
pub fn generalized_gradient_has_zero(slopes: &[f64], tol: f64) -> bool {
    let lo = slopes.iter().cloned().fold(f64::INFINITY, f64::min);
    let hi = slopes.iter().cloned().fold(f64::NEG_INFINITY, f64::max);
    lo <= tol && hi >= -tol
}

pub struct FreewayPoint { pub q: Point2<f64>, pub d: f64, pub active: Vec<usize>, pub slopes: Vec<f64>, pub channel: usize }

/// Strict local maxima of D̃(·; λ) on each channel of the slice x = λ, golden-section refined.
pub fn slice_maxima(world: &OppWorld, lambda: f64, samples: usize) -> Vec<FreewayPoint> {
    channels(world, lambda).iter().enumerate().flat_map(|(ci, &(lo, hi))| {
        let d = |y: f64| world.nearest(Point2::new(lambda, y)).d;
        strict_maxima(d, lo, hi, samples).into_iter().map(move |y| world.freeway_point(lambda, y, ci))
    }).collect()
}
```

### The worked example, and its printed output

```rust title="crates/roadmap/examples/lyapunov_step.rs"
use nalgebra::Vector2;
use roadmap::gvg::{GvgTracer, Nearest};

/// Two point sites; Nearest in *site* order (site 1 at the origin), as the text labels them.
fn nearest(q: Vector2<f64>, sites: &[Vector2<f64>; 2]) -> Nearest<2> {
    let d = sites.map(|c| (q - c).norm());
    let grad = sites.map(|c| (q - c).normalize());
    Nearest { d, grad, obstacle_id: [Some(0), Some(1)] }
}

fn main() {
    let sites = [Vector2::new(0.0, 0.0), Vector2::new(4.0, 0.0)];
    let tracer = GvgTracer { alpha: 1.0, beta: -1.0 };
    let q = Vector2::new(2.5, 1.0);
    let n = nearest(q, &sites);
    let g = n.d[0] - n.d[1];
    let dg = n.grad[0] - n.grad[1];
    let dg_pinv = dg / dg.norm_squared();
    let v = Vector2::new(-dg.y, dg.x).normalize();
    let (tw, gamma) = tracer.trace_step(&n, Vector2::new(0.0, 1.0)).unwrap();
    println!("G = {g:.4}  DG = ({:.4}, {:.4})  DG+ = ({:.4}, {:.4})  v = ({:.4}, {:.4})", dg.x, dg.y, dg_pinv.x, dg_pinv.y, v.x, v.y);
    println!("correction = ({:.4}, {:.4})  qdot = ({:.4}, {:.4})  Gamma = {gamma:.4}  dGamma = {:.4}",
             tracer.beta * dg_pinv.x * g, tracer.beta * dg_pinv.y * g, tw.v.x, tw.v.y, tracer.beta * g * g);

    // The Lyapunov box: integrate, watch Γ. The edge x = 2 is vertical, so "keep going up" is the
    // heading for the whole run.
    let heading = Vector2::new(0.0, 1.0);
    let (mut q, mut prev) = (q, f64::INFINITY);
    for _ in 0..200 {
        let (tw, gamma) = tracer.trace_step(&nearest(q, &sites), heading).unwrap();
        assert!(gamma <= prev + 1e-12, "Γ must not increase");
        prev = gamma;
        q += tw.v * 0.05;
    }
    println!("200 steps of dt = 0.05: Gamma monotone, |x - 2| < 1e-3: {}", (q.x - 2.0).abs() < 1e-3);
}
```

<Callout type="info">
Three things the port had to learn from the range ring. First, a ring of 360 rays reports the local
minima of $\rho$ *on its rays*, not on the obstacles: the closest point can sit half a degree away,
and the homing then converges to a point a centimetre off the true meet point. The port refines each
minimum's bearing by golden-section search between its neighbouring rays — Chapter 3's "more rays
where they matter" — and the meet points then land on the exact ones to $10^{-6}$. Second, one wall
seen across the $0/2\pi$ seam, or a flat minimum split in two, produces two readings with the same
gradient: $DG$ is singular and the meet detector fires on nothing. Readings within $0.05$ rad and
$10^{-6}$ m of each other are merged. Third, transversality says exactly degenerate meet points have
probability zero; the Apartment's mirrored doorways produce two meet points two millimetres apart
all the same, and the robot cannot tell them apart. Such a node gets one departure per adjacent pair
of closest points, which is what the geometry would give if the two points were merged.
</Callout>

## Putting it together: the floor plan Rusty drew

The integration lab uses the explored graph as what Chapter 8 said a roadmap is for. Rusty explores
the Apartment from room A with the ideal ring, and the graph it draws — $27$ meet points, $62$
edges — is turned into a roadmap: every polyline sample a node, consecutive samples joined, meet
points shared. Getting on is Chapter 8's gradient ascent, the same access the explorer used; the
landing point is on the GVD, and the nearest explored sample is never more than $2$ cm away, which
is the honest measure of how completely the sensor-built graph covers the exact diagram. Riding is
Chapter 6's A\*. Getting off is the goal's ascent, reversed.

Chapter 8's fifty seeded start–goal pairs — the same generator, the same filter, twenty-nine pairs
surviving it — are answered on the explored GVG and on the visibility graph for a $0.22$ m disc.
All twenty-nine are answered; the explored graph is one connected component. The GVG paths average
$10.88$ m against $6.68$ m for the shortest paths: $1.62$ times as long on average and $2.46$ times
in the worst case — the price of riding the middle of every room and doorway. They average $0.48$ m of minimum clearance against $0.22$ m — the shortest path grazes the
inflated walls, which is what shortest means. The sensor-built graph's mean path length is within two
percent of Chapter 8's grid-GVD paths for the same queries ($10.70$ m): the robot's map is as good a
highway as the geometer's.

The sister book's frontier exploration (*Probabilistic Robotics via Rust*,
[Chapter 24](https://prvr.shannon.id/chapters/ch24-exploration))
is the probabilistic cousin of this lab: it drives toward the boundary between known and unknown
cells of an occupancy grid, while the GVG explorer drives along a topological skeleton and needs no
grid at all — but it trusts its range readings, and the GVG Explorer's noise slider shows what that
trust costs (Exercise 4). What a range ring actually returns, and how noisy, is the sister book's
[Chapter 10](https://prvr.shannon.id/chapters/ch10-sensor-models).
Maps that keep a Euclidean distance field (the sister book's
[Chapter 19](https://prvr.shannon.id/chapters/ch19-map-representations)
surveys map representations) yield the GVD as the field's ridge, exactly as Chapter 8's brushfire
did; and Choset and Nagatani's topological SLAM, which localizes a robot by where it is *on* the
explored GVG, is the direct descendant of this chapter's explorer.

Three pointers close Part II's roadmaps. [Chapter 10](/chapters/ch10-cell-decompositions) takes the
critical points of this chapter literally: the boustrophedon decomposition's cell boundaries are the
slices through Canny's critical points, found by a robot with Lemma 5.5.2 in its sensor loop. The
complexity bound sends us to [Chapter 11](/chapters/ch11-prm), where a roadmap is built by throwing
darts and the contract of Definition 5.0.2 is restated with probability one. And the capstone,
[Chapter 23](/chapters/ch23-capstone), explores an unknown Apartment again.
