Fourteen chapters of planners assumed the planner knew $q$. This chapter admits that Rusty does not.
Encoders drift, landmarks are noisy, and the configuration a planner imagines is only a *belief* about
the state the robot occupies. Choset's Chapter 8 teaches the Kalman filter as no other robotics text
does — not as Bayes' rule with Gaussians, which the sister book does completely, but as an
**observer**: a copy of the system corrected toward the set of states consistent with the latest
measurement.

The whole chapter is one picture. An ellipse of plausible states touches a line of
measurement-consistent states, and the point of contact is the update. Once that picture is in
place, the Kalman gain, the innovation covariance, data association by $\chi^2$, and Kalman SLAM all
fall out of it — and so does the warning that an optimal observer still cannot learn a direction the
sensors never see. Everything deeper is a link into the sister volume. What stays here is exactly the
estimation vocabulary Chapters 16 and 23 consume.
</Overview>

## The problem: a planner that does not know where it is

Put Rusty in the Apartment corridor and ask it to drive a loop on encoders alone. Each tick the wheels
report how far they turned; integrate that and you have a position. The orange track below is that
integral. The gray dashed track is where the robot actually went.

<Deferred>

</Deferred>

Two things go wrong, and they are different in kind. The right wheel is a fraction of a percent larger
than the odometry believes, so every straight run bends into an arc — a *bias*, invisible to any
amount of averaging. On top of it the wheels slip by a random amount each tick — *noise*, which
random-walks the heading and, through the heading, the position. Choset's Figure 8.1 shows the same
thing on a B21: a rotational error approaching 45° after one lap. The command-integration estimate
"is continuously losing information about its location," and nothing in the integration can tell you
how much.

The cure is also in the widget. Tick *landmark fixes*. Whenever a mapped corner comes within range,
one range-and-bearing sighting pulls the estimate back and collapses the ellipse. That pull is the
subject of this chapter: how hard to tug, in which direction, and what the tug can and cannot fix.

One sentence of bookkeeping, said once for all of Part IV. For Rusty the planner's configuration
$q \in \SEtwo$ and the filter's state $x_t$ are the same object; the sister book writes $x_t, u_t,
z_t$ and this book keeps Choset's $x(k), u(k), y(k)$ because his geometry reads better in it. The
notation table below sits the two side by side so nobody translates twice.

## Building intuition

### The knob the filter turns

Strip the problem to Choset's §8.2.6 toy: a robot on a rail with state $x = [x_r, v_r]\T$, a force
input, Newton's law sampled at $T = 0.5$ s, and a noisy velocity sensor. An **observer** for it is a
copy of the dynamics plus a correction proportional to the innovation — the difference between what
the sensor said and what it would have said had the prediction been exact:

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-prediction}{\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)} = F\,\hat x(k \mid k) + G\,u(k),
\qquad
\htmlClass{term-posterior}{\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1)} = \htmlClass{term-prediction}{\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)}
+ K\,\bigl[\,\htmlClass{term-measurement}{y(k{+}1)} - H\,\htmlClass{term-prediction}{\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)}\,\bigr].
$$
</LinkedMath>

$K$ is a knob. Turn it to zero and you have dead reckoning; turn it up and the estimate chases the
sensor, noise and all; turn it far enough and the estimate oscillates and diverges. The Observer Bench
lets you turn it. Then it lets the filter turn it for you.

<Deferred>
  <ObserverBench />
</Deferred>

Watch three things. The ellipse in the phase plane grows orange on every predict and squishes purple
on every update, always toward the green line of states consistent with the reading. The dots on the
unit disc are the eigenvalues of the error dynamics; with the velocity sensor one of them sits at
exactly $1$ no matter what you do, and the position variance climbs forever. And when you tick *let
the filter choose*, the gain stops being a constant: it becomes a sequence $R(k)$ that starts large,
settles, and the "equivalent knob" readout drifts to $1$ on its own. **The Kalman gain is not a
tuning constant. It is a trajectory, and the knob you were turning is what the filter computes.**

Here is the one picture the chapter is built on, drawn from the numbers of Choset's example. The
orange ellipse is the predicted belief. The green line is $\Omega$: every state that would produce the
reading exactly. Where should the estimate go?

The naive answer is the gray foot: drop perpendicularly onto $\Omega$, correct the velocity the sensor
measured and leave the position alone. The right answer is the purple foot: perpendicular *in the
metric of the covariance*. Position and velocity are correlated in $P$ — a robot that was going faster
than we thought has also gone farther — so correcting the velocity should drag the position with it,
and the correlated, more uncertain direction moves more. Add sensor noise and the line itself moves to
$\Omega^*$ through the most likely output $y^*$; the Kalman update is the Mahalanobis foot on *that*
line. Three derivations, one picture.

## The mathematics

### Definitions

An **observer** for the system $x(k{+}1) = F x(k) + G u(k)$, $y(k) = H x(k)$ is a recursion that
reconstructs $x(k)$ from the known signals $u(\cdot), y(\cdot)$ and the model $(F, G, H)$ when $H$ is
not invertible — which it usually is not, being $p \times n$ with $p < n$ (§8.2.1; App. J.4). The
Kalman filter is both an observer and a filter: it reconstructs the state *and* suppresses the noise.

The **innovation** $\nu = y(k{+}1) - H\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)$ is what the sensor said minus what it
would have said had the prediction been exact. The **consistent set**
$\Omega = \{x \in \R^n : H x = y(k{+}1)\}$ is the affine subspace of states that reproduce the output;
its dimension is $n - p$ when $H$ has full row rank, which Choset assumes throughout §8.2.

The **Mahalanobis distance** $d_M(x, \hat x) = \norm{x - \hat x}_M$ with
$\norm{x}_M^2 = x\T P(k{+}1 \mid k)^{-1} x$ measures distance in standard deviations under the
predicted covariance (§8.2.3, footnote 5). It comes from an inner product
$\langle x_1, x_2 \rangle_M = x_1\T P^{-1} x_2$, so "orthogonal" has a meaning under it.

A linear system is **observable** when the initial state is determined by $u, y$ over a finite window;
for LTI systems, iff $\rank Q = n$ (App. J.4, Thm. J.4.1). The **Luenberger observer** (eq. J.9) is
$\dot{\hat x} = A\hat x + Bu + K(y - C\hat x)$, with error dynamics $\dot e = (A - KC)e$; its discrete
cousin is what the Bench runs.

**Data association** is the choice of $a(i)$, the landmark that explains measurement $i$ (§8.3.2).

### The simple observer is an orthogonal projection

<KeyIdea title="Statement (§8.2.2)">
With no noise, the update closest to the prediction in the Euclidean sense is
$\hat x^+ = \hat x^- + H\T (HH\T)^{-1}\,\nu$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Projecting the prediction onto Ω" result="Δx = Hᵀ(HHᵀ)⁻¹ ν">
Write $\Delta x = \hat x(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1) - \hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)$ and ask for the shortest $\Delta x$
that lands in $\Omega$.

**Step 1 — the true state lies in $\Omega$.** With no measurement noise, $Hx(k{+}1) = y(k{+}1)$
exactly. So the update should land in $\Omega$, and since the prediction is our best guess, it should
be the point of $\Omega$ nearest the prediction.

**Step 2 — shortest means orthogonal.** The shortest vector from a point to an affine subspace is
orthogonal to it: $a\T \Delta x = 0$ for every $a$ parallel to $\Omega$. A vector is parallel to
$\Omega$ iff $Ha = 0$, i.e. $a \in \operatorname{null}(H)$ (Choset's problems 4–5).

**Step 3 — orthogonal to the null space means in the column space of $H\T$.** This is the fundamental
theorem of linear algebra (problem 6): $\operatorname{null}(H)^\perp = \operatorname{column}(H\T)$.
So $\Delta x = H\T\gamma$ for some $\gamma \in \R^p$.

**Step 4 — guess $\gamma$ linear in the innovation.** Try $\gamma = K\nu$ with $K \in \R^{p \times p}$,
so $\Delta x = H\T K \nu$. Enforce membership in $\Omega$: $H(\hat x^- + \Delta x) = y$, i.e.
$H\Delta x = \nu$.

**Step 5 — solve.** $HH\T K\nu = \nu$ for every $\nu$ forces $K = (HH\T)^{-1}$, which exists because
$H$ has full row rank. The guess is verified and the observer is complete.

*Why it is not enough.* The update is always perpendicular to $\Omega$, so only the component of the
state that directly affects the current reading is corrected. Errors parallel to $\Omega$ — position,
for a velocity sensor — are never touched, and the estimate does not in general converge. The
covariance is what fixes this.
</Derivation>

### Prediction grows the ellipse; the update is the Mahalanobis-closest point

<KeyIdea title="Statement (§8.2.3)">
$P(k{+}1 \mid k) = F P(k \mid k) F\T + V$. With noiseless outputs, the most likely point of $\Omega$
under the predicted Gaussian is reached by $\Delta x = P H\T (HPH\T)^{-1}\nu$, and
$P(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1) = P - RHP$ with $R = PH\T(HPH\T)^{-1}$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Covariance prediction and the skewed projection" result="Δx = PHᵀ(HPHᵀ)⁻¹ ν">
**Step 1 — expand the definition.** $P(k{+}1 \mid k) = \E[(x(k{+}1) - \hat x(k{+}1 \mid k))(\cdot)\T]$.
Substitute $x(k{+}1) = Fx + Gu + v$ and $\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k) = F\hat x + Gu$; the input cancels and
the error is $F(x - \hat x) + v$.

**Step 2 — the cross terms vanish.** $v(k)$ is zero-mean and independent of both $x(k)$ and
$\hat x(k \mid k)$, so $\E[F(x - \hat x)v\T] = F\,\E[x - \hat x]\,\E[v]\T = 0$. What remains is
$F\,\E[(x - \hat x)(x - \hat x)\T]F\T + \E[vv\T] = FPF\T + V$. This is eq. (8.12). Note that $FPF\T$
can shrink if $F$ is contractive; prediction only ever *adds* uncertainty because of $+V$.

**Step 3 — most likely point on $\Omega$.** The Gaussian's exponent is
$-\tfrac12 (x - \hat x^-)\T P^{-1}(x - \hat x^-)$, so maximizing it over $\Omega$ means minimizing
$\norm{\Delta x}_M$ subject to $\hat x^- + \Delta x \in \Omega$. Replace Euclidean orthogonality by
$\langle a, \Delta x\rangle_M = a\T P^{-1}\Delta x = 0$ for all $a \in \operatorname{null}(H)$.

**Step 4 — the column space moves.** $P^{-1}\Delta x \in \operatorname{column}(H\T)$, hence
$\Delta x \in \operatorname{column}(PH\T)$: $\Delta x = PH\T\gamma$. Figure 8.3 draws this: the
Mahalanobis-equidistant ellipses around the prediction grow until one touches $\Omega$, and the touch
point is not under the prediction unless $P$ is a multiple of the identity.

**Step 5 — solve as before.** Guess $\gamma = K\nu$, enforce $H\Delta x = \nu$:
$HPH\T K\nu = \nu$, so $K = (HPH\T)^{-1}$ and $R = PH\T(HPH\T)^{-1}$ — eq. (8.21). The covariance
update $P^+ = P - RHP$, eq. (8.15), is Choset's problem 7 and this chapter's Exercise 1.

*Why it is still not enough.* Noiseless outputs make $P^+$ singular in the measured directions —
exactly zero uncertainty along $H$ — and a singular $P$ makes the Mahalanobis norm meaningless on the
next step. The last ingredient is sensor noise.
</Derivation>

### The Kalman filter: most likely output, then project

<KeyIdea title="Statement (§8.2.4)">
With $w \sim \Normal(0, W)$:
$R = P^- H\T (HP^-H\T + W)^{-1}$, $\;\hat x^+ = \hat x^- + R\nu$, $\;P^+ = P^- - RHP^-$.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="From Ω to Ω*: Theorem 8.2.1 and the final gain" result="R = P⁻Hᵀ(HP⁻Hᵀ + W)⁻¹">
**Step 1 — project the prediction into output space.** The state Gaussian $(\hat x^-, P^-)$ maps
under $H$ to an output-space Gaussian with mean $\hat y = H\hat x^-$ and covariance
$\hat W = HP^-H\T$.

**Step 2 — the measurement is a second Gaussian there.** The reading $y(k{+}1)$ carries noise of
covariance $W$, so the sensor's claim about the output is $\Normal(y, W)$. The two are independent.

**Step 3 — Theorem 8.2.1 (product of Gaussians).** The product of Gaussians with pairs $(z_1, C_1)$
and $(z_2, C_2)$ is proportional to a Gaussian with
$z_3 = z_1 + \mathcal{Q}(z_2 - z_1)$, $C_3 = C_1 - \mathcal{Q}C_1$, $\mathcal{Q} = C_1(C_1 + C_2)^{-1}$.
The proof (Choset's problem 8, Exercise 2 here) multiplies the exponentials and matches the terms
quadratic and linear in $z$.

**Step 4 — the most likely output.** Apply it with $(z_1, C_1) = (\hat y, \hat W)$ and
$(z_2, C_2) = (y, W)$: the peak is $y^* = \hat y + \hat W(\hat W + W)^{-1}(y - \hat y)$. Define
$\Omega^* = \{x : Hx = y^*\}$ — Figure 8.4's second line.

**Step 5 — project onto $\Omega^*$ as before.** The noiseless result of the previous derivation gives
$\Delta x = P^-H\T(HP^-H\T)^{-1}(y^* - H\hat x^-)$. Substitute $y^* - \hat y = \hat W(\hat W + W)^{-1}\nu$
with $\hat W = HP^-H\T$: the two inverses collapse,
$$
\Delta x = P^-H\T\,(HP^-H\T)^{-1}\,HP^-H\T\,(HP^-H\T + W)^{-1}\,\nu = P^-H\T\,(HP^-H\T + W)^{-1}\,\nu .
$$
That is eq. (8.24) with $R$ as stated, and $P^+ = P^- - RHP^-$ follows as in problem 7.

*Two honesty notes Choset insists on.* For Gaussian noise these are not merely the "best" estimates
but the *correct* posterior: if the belief at $k$ is Gaussian, so is the belief at $k{+}1$, and this is
it. For non-Gaussian noise the same equations give the best *linear* estimator, and a nonlinear one
may do better. The sister book reaches the same formulas by completing the square — see
[its Chapter 6](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch06-kalman-filters)
for that route, the information form, and the proof the two coincide.
</Derivation>

### The Kalman filter is a Luenberger observer with a scheduled gain

This is the chapter's lens, and Choset implies it across §8.2 and Appendix J without stating it.

<KeyIdea title="Statement">
Substituting the prediction into the update,
$$
\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1) = F\hat x(k \mid k) + Gu(k) + R(k{+}1)\bigl[\,y(k{+}1) - H\bigl(F\hat x(k \mid k) + Gu(k)\bigr)\bigr],
$$
a Luenberger observer whose gain $R(k{+}1)$ is recomputed from $P$ each step. With any *fixed* gain
$K$ the error obeys $e(k{+}1) = (I - KH)F\,e(k) + (I - KH)v(k) - Kw(k)$, stable iff every eigenvalue
of $(I - KH)F$ lies inside the unit disc (Thm. J.5.1). Among all gains, the Kalman gain is the one
minimizing $\tr P^+$ at each step.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Error dynamics of the fixed-gain observer" result="e(k+1) = (I − KH)F e(k) + noise">
**Step 1 — write the observer.** $\hat x^+ = F\hat x + Gu + K(y - H(F\hat x + Gu))$.

**Step 2 — subtract from the true dynamics.** $x^+ = Fx + Gu + v$ and $y = Hx^+ + w = H(Fx + Gu + v) + w$.
The inputs cancel.

**Step 3 — collect the error** $e = x - \hat x$:
$e^+ = Fe + v - K\bigl(HFe + Hv + w\bigr) = (I - KH)Fe + (I - KH)v - Kw$.

**Step 4 — read off stability.** The noise-free part is the unforced linear system
$e^+ = (I - KH)Fe$; by Thm. J.5.1 it decays iff $|\lambda_i| < 1$ for every eigenvalue of
$(I - KH)F$. Choosing $K$ is choosing where those eigenvalues sit — Thm. J.3.2 transposed. For a
single output this is Ackermann's formula on the pair $(F, HF)$, which `placeObserverGain` implements,
and which *fails* exactly when $(F, H)$ is unobservable: for the velocity-sensor rail robot the
position eigenvalue is pinned at $1$ and no $K$ moves it.

**Step 5 — the filter's choice.** Take the error covariance of the fixed-gain observer under the noise
model, in the form valid for *any* $K$ (the Joseph form):
$P^+(K) = (I - KH)P^-(I - KH)\T + KWK\T$. Differentiating $\tr P^+(K)$ in $K$ and setting it to zero
gives $K = P^-H\T(HP^-H\T + W)^{-1}$ — the Kalman gain. For a scalar state with $P^- = 1.7$, $H = 1$,
$W = 0.5$ a brute-force search over $K$ lands on $0.7727 = 1.7/2.2$ with $P^+ = 0.3864$; the check file
does this search so you need not trust the calculus.

*From knob to schedule.* Iterate predict and update on $P$ alone and, for an observable pair, $P^-$
converges to the fixed point of the Riccati recursion; the gain converges with it to a constant $R_\infty$.
That constant is a perfectly good Luenberger gain — the Bench's knob is scaled so that $g = 1$ is
$R_\infty$. For the position sensor, $R_\infty = [0.589, 0.287]\T$ and the error eigenvalues are
$0.634 \pm 0.097i$, modulus $0.641$. For the velocity sensor $P$ never converges but $R$ still does,
to $[0.500, 0.358]\T$, with eigenvalues $\{1, 0.642\}$ — the $1$ is the position direction nobody
measures.
</Derivation>

### Observability: the rank test

<KeyIdea title="Theorem 8.2.2 (Observability Test)">
The LTI system $x(k{+}1) = Fx(k) + Gu(k) + v(k)$, $y(k) = Hx(k) + w(k)$ is observable iff
$$
Q = \begin{bmatrix} H \\ HF \\ HF^2 \\ \vdots \\ HF^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}
\quad\text{has rank } n.
$$
For any observable linear system the Kalman estimate converges in the sense that the expected error
between the actual and estimated state is bounded for all time.
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Why stacking powers of F decides everything" result="rank Q = n ⇔ x(0) recoverable">
**Step 1 — stack the outputs.** With zero noise, $y(0) = Hx(0)$, $y(1) = HFx(0) + HGu(0)$,
$y(2) = HF^2x(0) + \text{known input terms}$, and so on. Move the known input terms to the left: the
stacked vector $\tilde y = Q\,x(0)$.

**Step 2 — recoverability is injectivity.** $x(0)$ is determined by $\tilde y$ iff $Q$ has a trivial
null space, i.e. rank $n$. Knowing $x(0)$ and the inputs, every later state follows.

**Step 3 — higher powers add nothing.** By Cayley–Hamilton $F^n$ is a linear combination of
$I, F, \dots, F^{n-1}$, so rows $HF^k$ for $k \ge n$ lie in the span of those already stacked.

**Step 4 — duality.** $(F, H)$ is observable iff $(F\T, H\T)$ is controllable (App. J.5.2), which is
why the same rank test, transposed, appears in Chapter 20 as the controllability matrix.

*The dead-reckoning case.* Velocity sensor: $Q = [0\ 1;\ 0\ 1]$, rank $1$. The null direction is
$[1, 0]\T$ — position — and the position variance grows without bound through optimal updates. Choset's
verdict: "This failure is not the fault of the Kalman filter … The problem instead lies with the system
itself." Position sensor: $Q = [1\ 0;\ 1\ 0.5]$, rank $2$, and both variances settle.

*For nonlinear systems* the test is applied to the linearized pair $(\partial f/\partial x,
\partial h/\partial x)$ at the current estimate and is only *local* — true at this pose, this input,
these landmarks. The Observability Probe recomputes it every frame for that reason.
</Derivation>

### Two hand-checked steps

Choset's §8.2.6 robot, with his numbers: $T = 0.5$, $m = 1$,
$F = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & 0.5\\ 0 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$, $G = [0,\, 0.5]\T$, $u = 0$,
$V = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0.2 & 0.05\\ 0.05 & 0.1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$, $H = [0\ \ 1]$,
$W = 0.5$, $\hat x(k \mid k) = [2, 4]\T$, $P(k \mid k) = \diag(1, 2)$. He reports no measurement value;
this chapter fixes $y(k{+}1) = 2.7$ and $y(k{+}2) = 2.0$. These two readings are ours, not his.

**Step 1.** Predict: $\hat x^- = F[2, 4]\T = [4, 4]\T$ and
$P^- = FPF\T + V = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1.7 & 1.05\\ 1.05 & 2.1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$.
Update: $\nu = 2.7 - 4 = -1.3$, $S = 2.1 + 0.5 = 2.6$, $R = [1.05,\ 2.1]\T / 2.6 = [0.4038,\ 0.8077]\T$,
so $\nu / S = -0.5$ exactly and

<LinkedMath>
$$
\htmlClass{term-posterior}{\hat x^+} = \htmlClass{term-prediction}{[4,\ 4]\T} + R\,\htmlClass{term-measurement}{(-1.3)} = [3.475,\ 2.95]\T,
\qquad
\htmlClass{term-posterior}{P^+} = \htmlClass{term-prediction}{P^-} - R\,[1.05\ \ 2.1]
= \begin{bmatrix}1.2760 & 0.2019\\ 0.2019 & 0.4038\end{bmatrix}.
$$
</LinkedMath>

**Step 2.** Predict: $\hat x^- = [4.95,\ 2.95]\T$,
$P^- = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1.7788 & 0.4538\\ 0.4538 & 0.5038\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$. Update:

### EKF for range-and-bearing localization

<KeyIdea title="Statement (§8.3, §8.3.1)">
For $x = [x_r, y_r, \theta_r]\T$ with $f(x, u) = [x_r + u_1\cos\theta_r,\ y_r + u_1\sin\theta_r,\
\theta_r + u_2]\T$ and
$h_j(x) = [\rho_j,\ \operatorname{atan2}(y_r - y_{\ell j},\, x_r - x_{\ell j}) - \theta_r]\T$,
the EKF is the Kalman filter with $F = \partial f/\partial x$ at $\hat x(k \mid k)$ and
$H_j = \partial h_j/\partial x$ at $\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)$ (eqs. 8.37–8.45).
</KeyIdea>

<Derivation title="Linearizing the unicycle and the landmark sensor" result="F and H_j by first-order Taylor">
**Step 1 — Taylor about the estimate.** Expand $f$ about $\hat x(k \mid k)$ and $h$ about
$\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)$ to first order; the Kalman equations then apply to the deviation. Nothing else
changes: the EKF is eqs. (8.26)–(8.32) with $f, h$ for the means and their Jacobians for the covariances.

**Step 2 — the motion Jacobian.**
$$
F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -u_1\sin\theta_r \\ 0 & 1 & u_1\cos\theta_r \\ 0 & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The third column is the whole story of heading error: a small $\delta\theta$ becomes a position error
$u_1\,\delta\theta$ sideways to the motion, every step.

**Step 3 — the sensor Jacobian.** With $\Delta = \hat x - x_{\ell j}$ and $\rho = \norm{\Delta}$,
$$
H_j = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x/\rho & \Delta y/\rho & 0 \\ -\Delta y/\rho^2 & \Delta x/\rho^2 & -1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Choset prints the bearing row before simplifying, as the raw derivative of $\operatorname{atan2}$
through $1/(1 + (\Delta y/\Delta x)^2)$; the check file keeps both forms and confirms they agree to
$10^{-15}$, and both agree with a central-difference Jacobian to $10^{-9}$. Read the second row: bearing
sensitivity to position falls off as $1/\rho$ while its sensitivity to heading is exactly $-1$ at any
range. A distant landmark is nearly a pure heading sensor.

**Step 4 — stack the visible landmarks.** $p(k)$ sightings give a $2p \times 3$ matrix $H$ by stacking
$H_{a(1)}, \dots, H_{a(p)}$ and a block-diagonal $W$. For block-diagonal $W$ this equals applying the
sightings one at a time.

**Step 5 — range-only is a deletion.** Drop the bearing rows from $h$ and $H$ (§8.3.3). The
observability consequences are the subject of the Probe below.

*Wrap the bearing innovation.* The 2005 text subtracts angles; near $\pm\pi$ that produces an innovation
of $2\pi$ and a catastrophic update. The implementation here takes the residual through `angleDiff` and
the heading through $\bplus$. The sister book's
[Chapter 7](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch07-ekf-ukf-manifolds)
does this properly — the UKF and the error-state EKF on $\SEtwo$ — and its
[Chapter 11](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch11-localization-gaussian)
covers gating and multi-hypothesis tracking when association is genuinely ambiguous.
</Derivation>

## The algorithm

Choset numbers none of Chapter 8's algorithms; the names below follow his section titles.

<Algorithm
  name="kalman_filter(x̂(k|k), P(k|k), u(k), y(k+1); F, G, H, V, W)"
  inputs="the current belief, the input applied, the output observed, the model"
  outputs="x̂(k+1|k+1), P(k+1|k+1), and (ν, S, R) for association and diagnostics"
  complexity="O(n³) in general; O(n²p + p³) when p ≪ n"
>
1. $\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k) = F\hat x(k \mid k) + Gu(k)$ — eq. 8.26
2. $P(k{+}1 \mid k) = FP(k \mid k)F\T + V$ — eq. 8.27
3. $\nu = y(k{+}1) - H\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k)$ — eq. 8.30
4. $S = HP(k{+}1 \mid k)H\T + W$ — eq. 8.31
5. $R = P(k{+}1 \mid k)H\T S^{-1}$ — eq. 8.32
6. $\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1) = \hat x(k{+}1 \mid k) + R\nu$ — eq. 8.28
7. $P(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1) = P(k{+}1 \mid k) - RHP(k{+}1 \mid k)$ — eq. 8.29; in code, the Joseph form, which equals this at the optimal gain and stays positive-definite under round-off
8. return $\hat x(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1),\ P(k{+}1 \mid k{+}1),\ (\nu, S, R)$
</Algorithm>

Choset's reading of $R$: if $R$ is "large" the sensor is more believable than the prediction and the
update leans on it; if "small", the reading barely moves the estimate. Line 5 says exactly how large —
the ratio of predicted uncertainty to total innovation uncertainty, in matrix form.

<Algorithm
  name="observability_rank(F, H)"
  inputs="the LTI pair, or the Jacobian pair of a nonlinear system at the current estimate"
  outputs="rank Q, and a basis of null(Q): the directions no output can correct"
  complexity="one rank computation on an np × n matrix"
>
1. $Q \leftarrow H$
2. for $k = 1, \dots, n-1$: append $HF^k$ to $Q$
3. return $\rank Q$ under a relative tolerance; observable iff it equals $n$ — Thm. 8.2.2
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="ekf_localize(x̂, P, u, {y_i}; f, h, ∂f, ∂h, V, W)"
  inputs="belief, input, the sightings this step, the nonlinear models and their Jacobians"
  outputs="updated belief and the association map a(·)"
  complexity="as kalman_filter plus the Jacobians; association O(n_ℓ p³) per sighting"
>
1. $\hat x^- = f(\hat x, u)$; $F = \partial f/\partial x|_{\hat x}$; $P^- = FPF\T + V$ — eqs. 8.37–8.39
2. for each sighting $y_i$ and each landmark $j$: $\nu_{ij} = y_i - h_j(\hat x^-)$, $S_{ij} = H_jP^-H_j\T + W_i$, $\chi^2_{ij} = \nu_{ij}\T S_{ij}^{-1}\nu_{ij}$ — §8.3.2
3. $a(i) = \arg\min_j \chi^2_{ij}$; reject the sighting if the minimum exceeds a gate, found a new landmark if it exceeds a high threshold (§8.4.2)
4. stack $h_{a(i)}$, $H_{a(i)}$ over accepted sightings; wrap bearing residuals
5. one `kalman_filter` update with the stacked $(H, W)$ — eqs. 8.40–8.45
6. return $\hat x^+, P^+, a$
</Algorithm>

Choset minimizes $\chi^2_{ij}$ alone. When the candidates' gates differ in size this is not quite
maximum likelihood: the $\log\det S_{ij}$ term matters, and the sister's `mlAssociate`, which the
range–bearing path here delegates to, keeps it. The range-only path uses Choset's rule verbatim because
the sister's routine assumes a bearing row.

### The ellipse that never shrinks

Now the question the Probe was built to answer. Rusty drives a square in room A of the Apartment while
the EKF above localizes it against known landmarks. How many does it need?

<Deferred minHeight={560}>

</Deferred>

With one range-only landmark the rank of the linearized pair is $2$. The single range row and its
products with $F$ all share the same $(x, y)$ direction — radial from the landmark — so the direction
*tangent* to the constant-range circle never appears in $Q$. The posterior ellipse stretches along that
circle and stays stretched: after 240 steps its trace is $0.482$ against $0.034$ with two landmarks, an
order of magnitude, and no number of further iterations closes the gap. Switch to two non-collinear
landmarks and the rank is $3$; put both landmarks and the robot on one line and it falls to $2$ again
(Exercise 4). Range *and* bearing from one landmark is observable while the robot moves — the third
column of $HF$ carries $u_1$ — and loses a rank the moment it stops: two numbers cannot pin three.
The NEES tile reads near $3$ for the observable cases, which is what a consistent 3-DOF filter should
report ([sister Ch. 6](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch06-kalman-filters)
on NEES and NIS).

### Kalman SLAM

If the landmarks are unknown, put them in the state (§8.4; Smith, Self and Cheeseman). For an
omnidirectional vehicle measuring the *relative displacement* to $n_\ell$ uniquely identified landmarks,
the system is linear:
$x = [x_r, y_r, x_{\ell 1}, y_{\ell 1}, \dots]\T$, $F = I$, $G = [I_2; 0]$, $V = \operatorname{blkdiag}(V_r, 0, \dots)$,
and each $H_i$ has $-1$ in the robot columns and $+1$ in landmark $i$'s. The sister's plain `Kf` runs
it unchanged. Its observability matrix has rank $4$ of $6$ for two landmarks: the two null directions
shift robot and map together. The filter learns the map's *shape* — in the check, the covariance of
$x_{\ell 1} - x_r$ falls monotonically from trace $0.079$ to $0.020$ — while the map's absolute
covariance stalls at $0.059$ and goes no lower. No sighting can say where the world is.

For range and bearing on the unicycle (§8.4.2) the state is $[x_r, y_r, \theta_r, x_{\ell 1}, \dots]$
and $H_i$ is eq. (8.47): dense in the first three columns, two nonzero entries at landmark $i$, zero
elsewhere. Association is eq. (8.48) against every landmark in the state, and a minimum $\chi^2$ above
a high threshold founds a new one. The *forget the map* toggle in the Probe runs this: three landmarks
founded from their first sightings, $588$ of the remaining $597$ sightings matched, the worst map error
$0.12$ m at $1.6\sigma$. Honest caveats: the covariance update is $O(n_\ell^2)$ per step, and on long
loops the linearization makes the filter inconsistent. The sister book's
[Chapter 14](https://shannon-dynamics.github.io/ProbabilisticRoboticRust/chapters/ch14-ekf-slam) shows
the cost and the inconsistency, and its Chapter 15 shows what replaced it.

## Implementation in Rust

The `estimate` crate is thin by design: every file is either an adapter over the sister book's
vendored filters or a piece Choset has and the sister does not. No filter is re-implemented.

```rust title="crates/estimate/src/lti.rs"
use nalgebra::{DMatrix, SMatrix};

/// An LTI system in Choset's letters. Every field names the Thrun symbol the
/// sister book uses, because the two conventions collide in the worst place:
/// Choset's `R` is the Kalman *gain*; Thrun's `R` is the process *covariance*.
pub struct Lti<const N: usize, const U: usize, const M: usize> {
    pub f: SMatrix<f64, N, N>, // A_t
    pub g: SMatrix<f64, N, U>, // B_t
    pub h: SMatrix<f64, M, N>, // C_t, assumed full row rank
    pub v: SMatrix<f64, N, N>, // R_t  (process covariance)
    pub w: SMatrix<f64, M, M>, // Q_t  (measurement covariance)
}

impl<const N: usize, const U: usize, const M: usize> Lti<N, U, M> {
    /// App. J.5: F = I + T A, G = T B, H = C.
    pub fn discretize(a: SMatrix<f64, N, N>, b: SMatrix<f64, N, U>, c: SMatrix<f64, M, N>, t: f64,
                      v: SMatrix<f64, N, N>, w: SMatrix<f64, M, M>) -> Self {
        Self { f: SMatrix::identity() + t * a, g: t * b, h: c, v, w }
    }

    /// Thm. 8.2.2: Q = [H; HF; …; HF^{N−1}]. Dynamic because N·M is not a
    /// const expression — the one place this crate leaves the stack.
    pub fn observability_matrix(&self) -> DMatrix<f64> {
        let mut q = DMatrix::zeros(N * M, N);
        let mut hf = self.h;
        for k in 0..N {
            q.view_mut((k * M, 0), (M, N)).copy_from(&hf);
            hf = hf * self.f; // Cayley–Hamilton says stopping at N−1 loses nothing
        }
        q
    }

    /// rank Q == N under a *relative* SVD tolerance — a parameter, not a magic
    /// number: a 0/1 matrix needs none, a linearized Jacobian mixing metres
    /// and radians needs a generous one.
    pub fn is_observable(&self, tol: f64) -> bool {
        self.observability_matrix().rank(tol) == N
    }
}
```

The observer is the knob. Its `step` is three lines; `error_eigs` is the unit-disc inset, and
`place_gain` is Theorem J.3.2 transposed — Ackermann's formula on the pair $(F, HF)$, which is why it
can only succeed when $(F, H)$ is observable.

```rust title="crates/estimate/src/observer.rs" {18-24}
use nalgebra::{Complex, SMatrix, SVector};
use crate::lti::Lti;

/// Fixed-gain Luenberger observer, App. J.4 discretized by J.5:
///   x̂⁺ = F x̂ + G u + K (y − H(F x̂ + G u)).
pub struct Observer<const N: usize, const U: usize, const M: usize> {
    pub sys: Lti<N, U, M>,
    pub k: SMatrix<f64, N, M>,
    pub xhat: SVector<f64, N>,
}

impl<const N: usize, const U: usize, const M: usize> Observer<N, U, M> {
    pub fn step(&mut self, u: &SVector<f64, U>, y: &SVector<f64, M>) {
        let predicted = self.sys.f * self.xhat + self.sys.g * u;
        let nu = y - self.sys.h * predicted; // the innovation: what the sensor saw minus what it should have
        self.xhat = predicted + self.k * nu;
    }

    /// e(k+1) = (I − K H) F e(k): inside the unit disc ⇔ the error decays (Thm. J.5.1).
    pub fn error_matrix(&self) -> SMatrix<f64, N, N> {
        (SMatrix::identity() - self.k * self.sys.h) * self.sys.f
    }

    pub fn error_eigs(&self) -> Vec<Complex<f64>> {
        self.error_matrix().complex_eigenvalues().iter().copied().collect()
    }

    /// The error covariance a fixed gain *actually* produces (Joseph form) —
    /// valid for any K, not only the optimal one. The Kalman gain is the
    /// argmin of its trace; see `kf::kalman_step`, which never calls this.
    pub fn covariance_update(&self, p: &SMatrix<f64, N, N>) -> SMatrix<f64, N, N> {
        let ikh = SMatrix::<f64, N, N>::identity() - self.k * self.sys.h;
        ikh * p * ikh.transpose() + self.k * self.sys.w * self.k.transpose()
    }
}
```

The Kalman step itself is an adapter. `filters::kalman::Kf` is the sister book's filter, vendored
unchanged; this function only renames its inputs into Choset's letters and hands back $(\nu, S, R)$
because data association and the widgets need them.

```rust title="crates/estimate/src/kf.rs"
pub use filters::kalman::{Gaussian, Kf}; // sister Ch. 6 — not re-derived, not re-implemented
use nalgebra::{SMatrix, SVector};
use crate::lti::Lti;

/// One Choset step, eqs. 8.26–8.32, through the sister's `Kf`.
pub fn kalman_step<const N: usize, const U: usize, const M: usize>(
    sys: &Lti<N, U, M>, bel: &mut Gaussian<N>, u: &SVector<f64, U>, y: &SVector<f64, M>,
) -> (SVector<f64, M>, SMatrix<f64, M, M>, SMatrix<f64, N, M>) {
    let mut kf = Kf::from(bel.clone());
    kf.predict(&sys.f, &sys.v, Some((&sys.g, u)));            // Thrun: A, R, (B, u)
    let info = kf.update(y, &sys.h, &sys.w);                   // Thrun: C, Q
    *bel = kf.belief();
    (info.innovation, info.s, info.k)                          // Choset: ν, S, R
}

/// §8.2.6 constants: T = 0.5, m = 1, V, W = 0.5, x̂ = [2, 4], P = diag(1, 2).
pub fn dead_reckoning() -> (Lti<2, 1, 1>, Gaussian<2>) {
    let (t, m) = (0.5, 1.0);
    let sys = Lti {
        f: SMatrix::<f64, 2, 2>::new(1.0, t, 0.0, 1.0),
        g: SMatrix::<f64, 2, 1>::new(0.0, t / m),
        h: SMatrix::<f64, 1, 2>::new(0.0, 1.0),                 // velocity sensor
        v: SMatrix::<f64, 2, 2>::new(0.2, 0.05, 0.05, 0.1),
        w: SMatrix::<f64, 1, 1>::new(0.5),
    };
    (sys, Gaussian { x: SVector::<f64, 2>::new(2.0, 4.0), p: SMatrix::from_diagonal(&[1.0, 2.0].into()) })
}
```

The landmark models carry Choset's conventions — the bearing is measured *from* the landmark, a

```rust title="crates/estimate/src/landmarks.rs"
use filters::nonlinear::MeasurementModel; // sister Ch. 7
use geom::SE2;
use nalgebra::{SMatrix, SVector};
use sim::Landmark;

/// h_j = [ρ, atan2(y_r − y_ℓ, x_r − x_ℓ) − θ_r]; rows of ∂h/∂x: [Δx/ρ, Δy/ρ, 0], [−Δy/ρ², Δx/ρ², −1].
pub struct RangeBearing<'m> { pub map: &'m [Landmark] }
/// §8.3.3: the same model with the bearing row removed.
pub struct RangeOnly<'m> { pub map: &'m [Landmark] }

impl MeasurementModel<SE2, 3, 2> for RangeBearing<'_> {
    fn h(&self, x: &SE2, j: usize) -> SVector<f64, 2> {
        let (dx, dy) = (x.x() - self.map[j].x, x.y() - self.map[j].y);
        SVector::new((dx * dx + dy * dy).sqrt(), geom::wrap(dy.atan2(dx) - x.theta()))
    }
    fn jacobian(&self, x: &SE2, j: usize) -> SMatrix<f64, 2, 3> {
        let (dx, dy) = (x.x() - self.map[j].x, x.y() - self.map[j].y);
        let q = (dx * dx + dy * dy).max(1e-12);
        let rho = q.sqrt();
        SMatrix::new(dx / rho, dy / rho, 0.0, -dy / q, dx / q, -1.0)
    }
    /// The innovation wraps: the one place where z − h(x) is wrong.
    fn residual(&self, z: &SVector<f64, 2>, zhat: &SVector<f64, 2>) -> SVector<f64, 2> {
        SVector::new(z[0] - zhat[0], geom::wrap(z[1] - zhat[1]))
    }
}

/// §8.3.2: a(i) = argmin_j ν_ijᵀ S_ij⁻¹ ν_ij. `None` above `chi2_new` ⇒ found a new landmark (§8.4.2).
pub fn associate_ml(y_i: &SVector<f64, 2>, bel: &Gaussian<3>, map: &[Landmark],
                    w: &SMatrix<f64, 2, 2>, chi2_new: f64) -> Option<usize> {
    let model = RangeBearing { map };
    let pose = SE2::from_vector(&bel.x);
    let best = (0..map.len()).map(|j| {
        let h = model.jacobian(&pose, j);
        let s = h * bel.p * h.transpose() + w;
        let nu = model.residual(y_i, &model.h(&pose, j));
        (j, (nu.transpose() * s.try_inverse().expect("S is PD") * nu)[(0, 0)])
    }).min_by(|a, b| a.1.total_cmp(&b.1))?;
    (best.1 <= chi2_new).then_some(best.0)
}
```

## Putting it together

The integration lab is the two widgets above, run in sequence on the Apartment, and it ends with the
object Chapter 16 inherits.

**Dead reckoning.** Rusty's encoders through `odometryDelta` give Choset's input $u = [\Delta s,
\Delta\theta]$ each tick. `ChosetEkf::predict` alone is Figure 8.1: the orange track in the hook bends
off the gray one and the ellipse grows without bound. Its claimed $\sigma$ tracks the actual error only
as long as the noise model is honest — the wheel-radius bias is *not* in the model, and when you raise
it the error outruns the ellipse. That gap is what the sister book's NEES instrument measures, and the
reason Chapter 16 will care about beliefs that are not Gaussian.

**One landmark.** In the Probe with one range-only landmark the rank reads $2$ and the ellipse's major
axis runs along the constant-range circle. After 240 steps the pose trace is $0.482$. The filter is
doing nothing wrong; "the problem lies with the system." A planner handed this belief should read the
long axis and plan a motion that *changes the geometry* — toward a second landmark, or around the
first — because no amount of sitting still will shrink it.

**Two landmarks.** Rank $3$, trace $0.034$, mean NEES $3.8$ against the expected $3$. This is the
belief a planner wants: a Gaussian $\bel(x_t)$ with a covariance it can read, in the same $\SEtwo$
coordinates its configuration lives in.

**Forget the map.** Kalman SLAM founds the three landmarks from their first sightings and matches
$588$ of $597$ later ones, with the worst landmark $0.12$ m off at $1.6\sigma$. The covariance is now
$9 \times 9$ and grows by two rows per landmark; the correlations between landmarks are what let a
re-sighting of one improve all the others. The state is also what makes the filter's eventual
inconsistency possible, which is the sister book's story, not this one.

What a planner takes from here is small and exact: a mean in $\SEtwo$, a $3 \times 3$ covariance, an
innovation covariance $S$ for each candidate sighting, and the rank test to tell whether the next
action can even reduce the uncertainty. Chapter 16 asks what happens when the belief is not Gaussian,
and how to plan on it.
