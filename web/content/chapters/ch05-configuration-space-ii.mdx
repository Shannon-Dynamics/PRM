[Chapter 4](/chapters/ch04-configuration-space-i) drew Reach's configuration space as a square with
amber blobs and kept saying "the edges are identified." This chapter says what that means, why a
planner must care, and how to make the compiler enforce it.

The reason not to skip Choset's most abstract pages is concrete. Every planner in the rest of this
book needs three things from a configuration space: a *distance* between two configurations, an
*interpolation* between them, and a way to *sample* them. All three are wrong if you pretend the
torus $T^2$ is the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, or that $\SEtwo$ is $\mathbb{R}^3$ — not wrong by a
little, wrong by the width of the whole space, as the first worked example shows with two numbers.
The cure is one sentence, and it is a Rust trait: **a configuration space is a manifold — local
coordinates, usually no global ones — so a planner must be written against the operations the
manifold provides, `dist`, `interpolate`, `sample`, and never against the coordinates.**

Along the way the chapter collects what Choset's §3.4–3.6 and Appendices B, C and E say about
mappings, charts, connectedness, compactness, metric spaces, matrix groups and rotations, keeps
the examples that carry the pedagogy — the circle, the ellipse and the racetrack; the four charts
of $S^1$; the three frames $A$, $B$, $C$ worked by hand — and closes by reading Reach's Jacobian
as the differential $D\varphi$, a linear map that does not care which chart you used.
</Overview>

## The problem: a path in two pieces

Here is the Chapter 4 picture of Reach's configuration space again — the square $[-\pi, \pi]^2$
with the C-obstacles of three disc obstacles on the Workbench — and a purple path between two
configurations. On the square the path is two disconnected pieces. Watch the square fold.

When the square has become a doughnut the two pieces are one curve, and a short one. Nothing
about the configurations changed; the picture of the space changed, from a *chart* to the *space*.
The readout underneath says the same thing in numbers. For the default endpoints the chart's
Euclidean distance is about six, the torus distance well under one, and the difference is entirely
the seam: the path crosses the dashed edge where $\theta_1 = \pi$ becomes $\theta_1 = -\pi$.

Three things in the widget are the whole chapter.

**The seam is an artifact of the chart, not a feature of the space.** The square is one way of
assigning two numbers to a configuration of a two-joint arm. It is a good way almost everywhere
and a bad way along four edges, and a planner that works with the numbers inherits the bad edges.
Choset's §3.4.2 names what the square is — a *chart* — and what the torus needs instead — an
*atlas*.

**Distance belongs to the space.** `dist` in the readout is $d_{T^2}$, the metric this chapter
defines on the torus. It is shorter than the chart distance exactly when the short way crosses a
seam. Chapter 11's nearest-neighbor queries, Chapter 12's tree extensions and Chapter 13's rewiring
radii all call this function, and all of them would quietly plan around a wall that does not exist
if they called the chart's distance instead.

**Joint limits change the topology.** Switch them on. The square stops wrapping, the surface on the
right is a disc, and the component count in the readout changes: the band of C-obstacle that cut
the square into two components, which the torus had glued back into one, now separates them for
good. Choset's §3.4.3 says this in one line — *compact and noncompact spaces cannot be
diffeomorphic* — and the lab at the end of the chapter measures it.

<KeyIdea title="The one sentence version">
A configuration space is a manifold: locally like $\mathbb{R}^k$, usually with no global
coordinates. A planner must ask the space for distances, interpolations and samples, and must
never do arithmetic on the coordinates — because every chart has a seam, and only the space knows
where it is.
</KeyIdea>

## Building intuition

### An angle is not a number

Start with the simplest manifold that is not a Euclidean space: the circle $S^1$, the
configuration space of one revolute joint. Choset's §3.4.2 covers it with four charts — the open
half-circles $U_1 = \{x_2 > 0\}$, $U_2 = \{x_2 < 0\}$, $U_3 = \{x_1 > 0\}$, $U_4 = \{x_1 < 0\}$,
each coordinatized by whichever Cartesian coordinate varies monotonically on it. The widget hands
a point from chart to chart.

</Deferred>

Every point of the circle lies in at least one chart — the four domains *cover*. Wherever a point
lies in two, both coordinates exist and are related by a smooth function, the *transition map*; on
$U_1 \cap U_3$ it is $\varphi_1 \circ \varphi_3^{-1}(z) = \sqrt{1 - z^2}$, drawn at lower right.
That smoothness is what lets a planner, or a derivative, pass from one chart to the next without a
jolt. The two-chart angle atlas is the one `S1` uses in code: the angle measured from $+x$ on the
circle minus $(-1, 0)$, and the angle measured from $-x$ on the circle minus $(1, 0)$, whose
transition map is a translation by $\pi$.

Then try the single-chart attempt, $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. It assigns a number to every point, and
it is not a chart: drag through $\theta = 0$ and the coordinate tears from $6.28$ to $0$. A chart's
domain has to be *open*, so that every point has a neighborhood on which the map is continuous, and
no neighborhood of $(1, 0)$ maps continuously to an interval. Chapter 4's "single angle in
$[0, 2\pi)$" was a chart with a seam, not an atlas, and the seam is where its arithmetic lies.

### A transform is not a position plus an angle

The second intuition is about rigid bodies. Choset's §3.5.1 works one example completely — three
frames $A$, $B$, $C$ on a unit grid and a point $w$ — and it is the one place in his book where the
group $\SEtwo$ can be checked by hand. The widget keeps his numbers as its reset state.

Two rules generate everything in the readout. *Subscript cancellation*: $T_{AB}\,T_{BC} = T_{AC}$,
and $T_{BC}\,w_C = w_B$ — the inner subscripts annihilate, and the result is the outer pair. And
*non-commutativity*: the same transform $T_1$ multiplied on the right of $T_{AB}$ moves frame $B$
in its own coordinates (a *body-frame* transformation, $B'$), multiplied on the left it moves $B$
in $A$'s coordinates (a *world-frame* transformation, $B''$), and the two frames land in different
places. Autoplay sweeps $T_1$'s heading so you can watch $B'$ and $B''$ diverge; reset returns to
Choset's $T_1$ and the two placements $(-2, -1, 0)$ and $(2, 1, 0)$. A pose is an element of a
group. The order of multiplication is the whole content.

The third intuition — that gimbal lock is a missing chart — waits for the rotation section, where
it has its own widget.

### Mappings: onto, one-to-one, and the two kinds of "the same"

Let $\phi : S \to T$ be a mapping. It is **surjective** (onto) if $\phi(S) = T$, **injective**
(one-to-one) if $\phi(s_1) = \phi(s_2)$ forces $s_1 = s_2$, and **bijective** if both. A bijection
has an inverse everywhere on $T$, and this is what lets us move back and forth between a
configuration space, whose geometry may be complicated, and a Euclidean space, whose geometry is
not. Choset's example: $\sin : (-\pi/2, \pi/2) \to (-1, 1)$ is bijective; $\sin : \mathbb{R} \to
[-1, 1]$ is only surjective.

<KeyIdea title="Definition 3.4.1 and 3.4.2 — homeomorphism, diffeomorphism">
A bijection $\phi : S \to T$ with $\phi$ and $\phi^{-1}$ both continuous is a **homeomorphism**,
and $S$, $T$ are **homeomorphic**. A bijection with $\phi$ and $\phi^{-1}$ both smooth ($C^\infty$)
is a **diffeomorphism**, and $S$, $T$ are **diffeomorphic**. Every diffeomorphism is a
homeomorphism; the converse fails.
</KeyIdea>

The converse fails on Choset's three curves (his figure 3.12): the unit circle
$M_c = \{x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$, the ellipse $M_e = \{x^2/4 + y^2 = 1\}$, and the *racetrack* $M_r$ —
two half-circles of radius one joined by straight segments, $f_r(x,y) = 0$ with

$$
f_r(x, y) =
\begin{cases}
x - 1 & -1 \le y \le 1,\; x > 0 \\
(y+1)^2 + x^2 - 1 & y < -1 \\
(y-1)^2 + x^2 - 1 & y > 1 \\
x + 1 & -1 \le y \le 1,\; x < 0 .
\end{cases}
$$

All three are homeomorphic: radial projection $\phi(x, y) = (x, y)/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ carries the
ellipse onto the circle and is continuous both ways. The circle and ellipse are diffeomorphic — that
same $\phi$ is smooth both ways. But no diffeomorphism reaches the racetrack: its curvature jumps
at $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$, where straight meets round, and a diffeomorphism would have to carry the
circle's constant curvature onto it smoothly. Topology sees one closed curve; differential
structure sees two kinds.

### Neighborhoods, and what "locally" means

A **neighborhood** of a point $p$ in a metric space is any set $U \ni p$ such that every point of
$U$ has an open ball $B_\epsilon(p') = \{p'' : d(p', p'') < \epsilon\}$ inside $U$; an open ball is
itself a neighborhood. $S$ is **locally diffeomorphic** to $T$ if every point of $S$ has a
neighborhood diffeomorphic to an open subset of $T$. The sphere is locally diffeomorphic to the
plane — which is Choset's remark about the flat earth in this chapter's epigraph — and not
globally: there is no bijection from the sphere onto the plane that is continuous both ways.

The two robots of Chapter 4 sit on either side of this distinction. A disc translating in the plane
has configuration space $\mathbb{R}^2$, globally diffeomorphic to its workspace by the identity. The
two-joint arm has $T^2$, which is *locally* diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$ — every configuration
has a neighborhood that looks like a patch of the plane — and not globally, because $T^2$ is
compact and $\mathbb{R}^2$ is not (§3.4.3 below). Give the arm strict joint limits
$\theta_i^\ell < \theta_i < \theta_i^u$ and its configuration space becomes an open rectangle,
which *is* globally diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$: stretch each open interval over the line with
$\tan$. Joint limits are not a detail. They change the topology.

### Manifolds, charts, atlases

<KeyIdea title="Definitions 3.4.3–3.4.5 — manifold, chart, C∞-related charts, atlas">
A set $S$ is a **$k$-dimensional manifold** if it is locally homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^k$: every
point has a neighborhood homeomorphic to an open set of $\mathbb{R}^k$. A **chart** is a pair
$(U, \phi)$ with $U$ open in $S$ and $\phi$ a diffeomorphism from $U$ onto an open set of
$\mathbb{R}^k$; $\phi^{-1}$ is a *parameterization*. Two charts $(U, \phi)$, $(V, \psi)$ are
**$C^\infty$-related** if the transition maps $\psi \circ \phi^{-1}$ and $\phi \circ \psi^{-1}$
are smooth on the images of $U \cap V$. A family of pairwise $C^\infty$-related charts whose
domains cover $S$ is an **atlas**, also called the *differentiable structure*; $S$ with an atlas is a
**differentiable manifold**.
</KeyIdea>

The configuration spaces this book plans in are all differentiable manifolds, and the
parameterizations of Chapter 4 — "the configuration of the arm is $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$" — were
charts without the name. When no single chart covers the space, as for the torus, there are three
options (Choset §3.5): use one chart anyway and suffer its seam; use an atlas; or *embed* the space
in a higher-dimensional Euclidean space with constraints — the unit circle in $\mathbb{R}^2$, the
doughnut in $\mathbb{R}^3$, rotation matrices in $\mathbb{R}^9$. The code in this chapter uses all
three, each where it is best.

<Derivation title="S¹ needs at least two charts, and Choset's four suffice" result="\{(U_i, \phi_i)\}_{i=1}^4 \text{ is an atlas}">
**Statement.** No single chart covers $S^1$. The charts $U_1 = \{x_2 > 0\}, \phi_1 = x_1$;
$U_2 = \{x_2 < 0\}, \phi_2 = x_1$; $U_3 = \{x_1 > 0\}, \phi_3 = x_2$; $U_4 = \{x_1 < 0\},
\phi_4 = x_2$, with parameterizations $\phi_1^{-1}(z) = (z, \sqrt{1 - z^2})$,
$\phi_2^{-1}(z) = (z, -\sqrt{1 - z^2})$, $\phi_3^{-1}(z) = (\sqrt{1 - z^2}, z)$,
$\phi_4^{-1}(z) = (-\sqrt{1 - z^2}, z)$, form an atlas.

**Step 1 — one chart is impossible.** A single chart would be a homeomorphism from $S^1$ onto an
open subset of $\mathbb{R}$, which is a union of open intervals; being the image of a connected set
it is one interval. Removing a point from an open interval disconnects it; removing a point from
$S^1$ does not. A homeomorphism preserves connectedness of the complement of a point, so none
exists.

**Step 2 — cover.** Every point of $S^1$ has $x_1 \ne 0$ or $x_2 \ne 0$, so lies in some $U_i$.
Each $U_i$ is open, each $\phi_i$ is the restriction of a coordinate projection, a diffeomorphism
onto $(-1, 1)$ with the inverse shown.

**Step 3 — the transition maps.** $U_1 \cap U_2 = U_3 \cap U_4 = \emptyset$, so there are four
overlaps to check, each a quarter-circle. On $U_1 \cap U_3$, $\phi_1 \circ \phi_3^{-1}(z) =
\sqrt{1 - z^2}$ for $z \in (0, 1)$ and $\phi_3 \circ \phi_1^{-1}(z) = \sqrt{1 - z^2}$ for
$z \in (0, 1)$. The other three overlaps give $\pm\sqrt{1 - z^2}$ on $(-1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$.

**Step 4 — smoothness.** $\sqrt{1 - z^2}$ is smooth on any open subinterval of $(-1, 1)$ — the
only non-smooth points, $z = \pm 1$, are excluded because the overlaps are open quarter-circles.
Hence the charts are pairwise $C^\infty$-related and the four form an atlas. $\blacksquare$

*Two charts suffice* (Choset problem 3.9): $V_1 = S^1 \setminus \{(-1, 0)\}$ with the angle from
$+x$ in $(-\pi, \pi)$, and $V_2 = S^1 \setminus \{(1, 0)\}$ with the angle from $-x$. Their
transition map is $z \mapsto z \pm \pi$ on each component of the overlap: a translation, with
derivative $1$. This is the atlas `S1` uses, and the derivative-one fact is what makes the Jacobian
chart-independent in the last derivation of this chapter.
</Derivation>

### Connectedness and compactness

A manifold is **connected** if any two of its points are joined by a path. $\mathbb{R}^n$, $S^n$,
$T^n$ are; so are $\SOthree$ and $\SEtwo$. Obstacles break $\Qfree$ into **connected components**,
its maximal connected subsets, and the first fact every planner must respect is that there is no
solution when $\qstart$ and $\qgoal$ lie in different components. The Torus Unwrapper's component
count is this definition evaluated on a raster.

A space is **compact** if it is a closed and bounded subset of some $\mathbb{R}^n$. $[0, 1]$ is
compact and $[0, 1)$ is not; $S^n$, $T^n$ and $\SOthree$ are compact; $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\SEtwo$
are not. Products of compact spaces are compact, and a non-compact product $M_1 \times M_2$ with
$M_1$ compact has $M_1$ as its *compact factor* — $\SEtwo \cong \mathbb{R}^2 \times S^1$ has $S^1$.
Two facts matter for planning: compact and non-compact spaces are never diffeomorphic, which is the
invariant that separates $T^2$ from $\mathbb{R}^2$; and a sampler can be uniform on a compact space
but needs a box on a non-compact one, which is why `Se2::sample` takes bounds and `S1::sample` does
not.

<Derivation title="Joint limits change the topology" result="T^2 \not\cong \mathbb{R}^2,\quad (\theta^\ell, \theta^u)^2 \cong \mathbb{R}^2">
**Statement.** The two-joint arm's configuration space $T^2$ is not diffeomorphic to
$\mathbb{R}^2$; with strict joint limits it becomes an open rectangle, which is.

**Step 1.** Continuous images of compact sets are compact. $T^2$ is compact (a closed, bounded
subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$ via the doughnut embedding) and $\mathbb{R}^2$ is unbounded, so no
continuous bijection carries one onto the other — let alone a diffeomorphism.

**Step 2.** With limits $\theta_i^\ell < \theta_i < \theta_i^u$, each joint ranges over an open
interval and the space is the open rectangle $(\theta_1^\ell, \theta_1^u) \times (\theta_2^\ell,
\theta_2^u)$.

**Step 3.** $t \mapsto \tan\!\big(\pi \tfrac{t - \theta^\ell}{\theta^u - \theta^\ell} - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)$
is a diffeomorphism from $(\theta^\ell, \theta^u)$ onto $\mathbb{R}$, and a product of
diffeomorphisms is a diffeomorphism, so the rectangle is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$.
$\blacksquare$

*Closed limits* $\theta_i^\ell \le \theta_i \le \theta_i^u$ give a compact space again — but a
manifold *with boundary*, which Choset's §3.4.4 warns is not a manifold in the sense above. And some
one-degree-of-freedom parallel mechanisms have a configuration space shaped like a figure eight,
which is no manifold at all: at the crossing there are two distinct motion directions. "If you
cannot show it to be a manifold, it may not be."
</Derivation>

### Metric spaces, and the contract for `dist`

<KeyIdea title="Definition C.2.1 — metric">
A **metric** on a set $M$ is a function $d : M \times M \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ with, for all
$m_1, m_2, m_3$: (1) *definiteness*, $d(m_1, m_2) = 0$ iff $m_1 = m_2$; (2) *symmetry*,
$d(m_1, m_2) = d(m_2, m_1)$; (3) the *triangle inequality*, $d(m_1, m_3) \le d(m_1, m_2) +
d(m_2, m_3)$. The open balls of a metric generate a topology, and two metrics are equivalent when
each ball of one contains a ball of the other.
</KeyIdea>

Non-negativity is not an axiom; it follows: $0 = d(m_1, m_1) \le d(m_1, m_2) + d(m_2, m_1) =
2 d(m_1, m_2)$. A function $f : S \to T$ between metric spaces is **continuous** (Definition C.4.1)
when preimages of open sets are open, equivalently when for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is a
$\delta > 0$ with $d(x, s) < \delta \Rightarrow d(f(x), f(s)) < \epsilon$. A path is a $C^0$ map
$c : [0, 1] \to \Qfree$; a trajectory must be $C^k$ for $k \ge 1$ so that velocity exists.

Here are the four metrics this chapter ships, with the chart's wrong answer written beside each
right one. The seam example from the hook: angles $a = 0.1$ and $b = 6.2$ radians.

<LinkedMath>
$$
d_{S^1}(a, b) = \min\big(|a - b|,\; 2\pi - |a - b|\big)
\qquad
d_{S^1}(0.1, 6.2) = 2\pi - 6.1 = \htmlClass{term-path}{0.1832}
\quad\text{(chart: } |a - b| = 6.1\text{)}
$$
</LinkedMath>

The geodesic midpoint `interpolate(a, b, 0.5)` goes the short way, *downward* from $0.1$ by half of
$0.1832$: it lands at $\htmlClass{term-path}{0.0084}$ after wrapping, not at the chart midpoint
$3.15$. On the torus, with $p = (0.1, 3.0)$ and $q = (6.2, 3.5)$:

$$
d_{T^2}(p, q) = \sqrt{d_{S^1}(0.1, 6.2)^2 + d_{S^1}(3.0, 3.5)^2} = \sqrt{0.1832^2 + 0.5^2} = \mathbf{0.5325}.
$$

On $\SEtwo$ the metric needs an *exchange rate* $w$ between metres and radians, and with
$w = 0.5\ \mathrm{m/rad}$ the distance from $(0, 0, 0)$ to $(1, 0, \pi)$ is

$$
d^{w}_{SE(2)} = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + w^2\, d_{S^1}(\theta, \theta')^2}
= \sqrt{1 + 0.25\pi^2} = \mathbf{1.8621}.
$$

On $\SOthree$ the distance between two rotations is the angle of the rotation that carries one to
the other, $d_{SO(3)}(R, R') = \arccos\big((\operatorname{tr}(R^{\mathsf T} R') - 1)/2\big)$, which
is also the axis–angle $\theta$ of $R^{\mathsf T} R'$ (App. E.3) and, for unit quaternions,
$2\arccos|\langle Q, Q' \rangle|$.

<Derivation title="The chapter's metrics are metrics, and interpolate follows geodesics" result="d_{S^1}, d_{T^2}, d^w_{SE(2)}, d_{SO(3)} \text{ satisfy C.2.1}">
**Statement.** Each of the four functions above satisfies Definition C.2.1, and `interpolate(t)`
moves along the shortest arc at constant speed: $d(a, \gamma(t)) = t\, d(a, b)$.

**Step 1 — $S^1$ is a quotient.** Identify $S^1$ with $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. For classes
$[a], [b]$ define $d([a], [b]) = \min_{k} |a - b + 2\pi k|$, the shortest representative of the
difference — which is exactly $\min(|a-b|, 2\pi - |a-b|)$ for representatives in a window of width
$2\pi$. Definiteness and symmetry are inherited from $|\cdot|$. For the triangle inequality pick
$k_1, k_2$ attaining the two minima on the right; then $a - c + 2\pi(k_1 + k_2)$ is *some*
representative of $[a] - [c]$, so $d([a],[c]) \le |a - b + 2\pi k_1| + |b - c + 2\pi k_2|$.

**Step 2 — products.** For metrics $d_1, d_2$, the function $\sqrt{d_1^2 + d_2^2}$ is a metric:
definiteness and symmetry are immediate, and the triangle inequality is Minkowski's inequality for
the Euclidean norm applied to the vectors $(d_1(a,b), d_2(a,b))$ and $(d_1(b,c), d_2(b,c))$, using
the triangle inequalities of $d_1$ and $d_2$ componentwise. This gives $d_{T^2}$, $d_{T^n}$, and
$d^w_{SE(2)}$ for any $w > 0$ (a positive scalar multiple of a metric is a metric).

**Step 3 — the weight has no canonical value.** Scaling $w$ changes which pose is "nearer": with
$w = 0.5$ a half-turn in place costs $1.57$ m-equivalents, with $w = 2$ it costs $6.28$. Both are
metrics; neither is right. Chapter 11 measures what a PRM does as $w$ varies, and this book never
hides $w$ in a default.

**Step 4 — $\SOthree$.** $d(R, R')$ is the angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ of the relative rotation
$R^{\mathsf T}R'$. Definiteness: $\theta = 0$ iff $R^{\mathsf T}R' = I$. Symmetry: $R'^{\mathsf T}R$
is the inverse rotation, same angle. Triangle inequality: composing a rotation by $\alpha$ with a
rotation by $\beta$ yields a rotation by at most $\alpha + \beta$ (the angle is the geodesic
distance on the unit 3-sphere of quaternions, folded by the double cover, and great-circle distance
on a sphere satisfies the triangle inequality). The quaternion form $2\arccos|\langle Q, Q'\rangle|$
is the same number because $\langle Q, Q' \rangle = \cos(\theta/2)$ up to sign, and the absolute
value is what identifies $Q$ with $-Q$.

**Step 5 — geodesics.** On $S^1$, $\gamma(t) = a + t\,\operatorname{wrap}(b - a)$ moves along the
shorter arc with $d(a, \gamma(t)) = t |\operatorname{wrap}(b-a)| = t\,d(a,b)$. On a product, the
componentwise geodesic is the geodesic of the $\ell^2$ product metric because the factors do not
interact. On $\SOthree$, spherical linear interpolation between $Q$ and the sign-flipped $\pm Q'$
nearer to $Q$ traverses the shorter great-circle arc at constant speed. $\blacksquare$

*The antipodal ambiguity.* At $d_{S^1} = \pi$ exactly, or $\theta = \pi$ on $\SOthree$, the
geodesic is not unique. `S1::interpolate` takes the representative $+\pi$ that `wrap` returns —
counter-clockwise — and `So3::interpolate` the sign-flipped endpoint; both deterministic, both
documented, neither hidden.
</Derivation>

### Embeddings: $SO(n)$ and $SE(n)$

The alternative to an atlas is an embedding with constraints. A rotation in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a
$3 \times 3$ matrix whose columns are the body axes $\tilde x, \tilde y, \tilde z$ expressed in a
stationary frame: nine numbers, six constraints (three unit lengths, three orthogonalities), three
degrees of freedom.

<KeyIdea title="§3.5.1 — the matrix groups">
$$
SO(n) = \{R \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} : R R^{\mathsf T} = I,\ \det R = 1\},
\qquad
SE(n) = \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} : R \in SO(n),\ p \in \mathbb{R}^n \right\}.
$$
Both are groups under matrix multiplication and smooth submanifolds of $\mathbb{R}^{m^2}$ — Lie
groups, in Choset's footnote: the group operation and inversion are $C^\infty$. $\dim SO(2) = 1$,
$\dim SO(3) = 3$, $\dim SE(2) = 3$, $\dim SE(3) = 6$. $R^{\mathsf T} = R^{-1}$.
</KeyIdea>

A matrix in $SE(n)$ is used three ways (§3.5.1): to *represent* a configuration — then it is a
*frame*; to *change the reference frame* of a configuration or a point; and to *displace* one —
then it is a *transform*. The three frames of the Frame Composer are Choset's figure 3.17 restricted
to the plane, with $T_{AB}$ having $R = \mathrm{rot}(\pi)$, $p = (-2, 0)$ and $T_{BC}$ having
$R = \mathrm{rot}(-\pi/2)$, $p = (-4, -1)$.

<Derivation title="SE(n) composes by subscript cancellation; body versus world transformations" result="T_{AB}T_{BC} = T_{AC},\quad T_{AB}T_1 \ne T_1 T_{AB}">
**Statement.** $T_{AB}T_{BC} = T_{AC}$ changes the frame in which $C$ is expressed; $w_A = T_{AB}
w_B$ changes a point's frame; $T_{AB} w_A$ *displaces* the point by the motion that carries $A$ to
$B$; and for a transform $T_1$, $T_{AB}T_1$ acts in $B$'s frame (body) while $T_1 T_{AB}$ acts in
$A$'s (world), with different results.

**Step 1 — block multiply.**
$$
\begin{bmatrix} R_1 & p_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} R_2 & p_2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix} R_1 R_2 & R_1 p_2 + p_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
$R_1 R_2 \in SO(n)$ since $(R_1R_2)(R_1R_2)^{\mathsf T} = I$ and determinants multiply, so $SE(n)$
is closed under the product. The inverse is
$T^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & -R^{\mathsf T}p \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, as one checks by
multiplying. With the identity, $SE(n)$ is a group.

**Step 2 — the constraint count.** $R \in SO(3)$ has $9$ entries and $6$ independent constraints,
so $\dim SO(3) = 3$; adding $p$ gives $\dim SE(3) = 6$, the six degrees of freedom of a rigid body
in space. In the plane, $\dim SO(2) = 1$ and $\dim SE(2) = 3$.

**Step 3 — the micro-example.** With $T_{AB} = (x, y, \theta) = (-2, 0, \pi)$ and
$T_{BC} = (-4, -1, -\pi/2)$:
$R_{AC} = \mathrm{rot}(\pi)\,\mathrm{rot}(-\pi/2) = \mathrm{rot}(\pi/2)$ and
$p_{AC} = R_{AB}\,p_{BC} + p_{AB} = (4, 1) + (-2, 0) = (2, 1)$, so
$T_{AC} = (2, 1, \pi/2)$, Choset's $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The
point $w_C = (-2, 1)$: $w_B = R_{BC} w_C + p_{BC} = (1, 2) + (-4, -1) = (-3, 1)$ and
$w_A = R_{AB} w_B + p_{AB} = (3, -1) + (-2, 0) = (1, -1)$, both as in his text. Displacing instead:
$T_{AB} w_A = (-3, 1)$ — the subscripts do not cancel, and the result is a *new* point in $A$'s
frame, rotated about $A$'s origin by $R_{AB}$ and translated by $p_{AB}$ (figure 3.18).

**Step 4 — the two orderings.** With $T_1 = (0, 1, \pi)$:
$T_{AB} T_1 = \begin{bmatrix} R_{AB}R_1 & R_{AB}p_1 + p_{AB} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = (-2, -1, 0)$
rotates $B$ about *its own* origin by $R_1$ then translates by $p_1$ *in the original $B$ frame*;
$T_1 T_{AB} = \begin{bmatrix} R_1 R_{AB} & R_1 p_{AB} + p_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = (2, 1, 0)$
rotates $B$ about *$A$'s* origin and translates in $A$. They differ because matrix multiplication
does not commute. $n$ body-frame transformations stack on the right, $T_{AB}T_1 T_2 \cdots T_n$;
world-frame ones on the left, $T_n \cdots T_2 T_1 T_{AB}$. $\blacksquare$

*Choset problem 3.23.* $\SEtwo$ and $\mathbb{R}^2 \times SO(2)$ with the product operation
$(x_1, R_1)(x_2, R_2) = (x_1 + x_2, R_1 R_2)$ are homeomorphic as spaces — the same three
coordinates — but not isomorphic as groups: the product group is commutative and $\SEtwo$ is not.
`Pose2::compose` is $\SEtwo$'s product, with the $R_1 p_2$ coupling; the commutative one is what you
get by adding poses componentwise, which is wrong for a robot.
</Derivation>

### $D\varphi$ is chart-independent; the Jacobian is its matrix

Chapter 4 computed Reach's Jacobian $J(q) = \partial\varphi/\partial q$ for the forward map
$\varphi : T^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$. Appendix C.5 calls the same matrix the **differential** $D\varphi$
and adds the one footnote that matters here: the differential is really a linear map from the
tangent space of the domain to the tangent space of the range, $D\varphi_q : T_q\Q \to
T_{\varphi(q)}M$, and the matrix is its expression in charts. Along a curve $c(t)$ in $\Q$,
$\tfrac{d}{dt}\varphi(c(t)) = D\varphi_{c(t)}\,\dot c(t)$ — velocities are columns, pushed forward
by $J$. Forces live in the cotangent space and are rows, pulled back by $J^{\mathsf T}$ (Choset's
remark on p. 486), which is why Chapter 7 will write $\tau = J^{\mathsf T} F$.

<Derivation title="Dφ is chart-independent; the Jacobian is its matrix" result="D(\varphi \circ h) = D\varphi \cdot Dh,\quad Dh = I \text{ for the angle atlas}">
**Statement.** Under a change of chart $h$, the matrix of $D\varphi$ transforms by the chain rule
$D(\varphi \circ h) = D\varphi \cdot Dh$. For the torus's angle atlas the transition maps are
translations by $2\pi$, $Dh = I$, and Chapter 4's $J$ is the same matrix in every chart.

**Step 1 — the differential along curves.** Let $c : \mathbb{R} \to \Q$ be smooth and set
$x(t) = \varphi(c(t))$. Then $\dot x = \tfrac{\partial \varphi}{\partial q}\big|_{c(t)}\,\dot c =
D\varphi_{c(t)}\,\dot c(t)$: $D\varphi$ is the linear map that takes the velocity of the
configuration to the velocity of the image.

**Step 2 — the chain rule (C.5).** If the curve is given in $y$-coordinates and
$h$ maps $y$-coordinates to $x$-coordinates, $\tfrac{d}{dt}(\varphi \circ h \circ c) =
D_h\varphi \cdot D_c h \cdot \dot c$. Choset's Example C.5.1 is the worked check: $c(t) = (2, 2\pi t)$
in polar coordinates, $h$ polar-to-Cartesian, $g(x) = x_1$, and the chain rule gives
$[1\ 0]\begin{bmatrix}\cos 2\pi t & -2\sin 2\pi t \\ \sin 2\pi t & 2\cos 2\pi t\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2\pi \end{bmatrix} = -4\pi \sin 2\pi t$, which direct differentiation of
$2\cos 2\pi t$ confirms.

**Step 3 — the torus.** The transition maps of the angle atlas are $h(\theta_1, \theta_2) =
(\theta_1 \pm 2\pi, \theta_2)$ and the like, with $Dh = I$. So the matrix of $D\varphi_q$ is the
same $J(q)$ whichever chart the configuration is read in; shifting $\theta_1$ by a full turn changes
nothing — not the entries, not the singularities. At Choset's $q = (\pi/4, \pi/2)$ with
$L_1 = L_2 = 1$ and $\dot q = (1, 0)$:
$$
J(q)\dot q = \begin{bmatrix} -\sqrt2 & -\sqrt2/2 \\ 0 & -\sqrt2/2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
= \begin{bmatrix} -\sqrt 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1.4142 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},
$$
and `dphi_chart_independent` asserts the matrix is unchanged, to machine precision, under
$\theta_1 \mapsto \theta_1 + 2\pi$. $\blacksquare$
</Derivation>

### Rotations in three dimensions, briefly

Everything above generalizes from $SO(2)$ to $\SOthree$ except the coordinates. Nine matrix entries
with six constraints leave three degrees of freedom, so $\SOthree$ is a three-dimensional manifold
and can be *locally* parameterized by three numbers — Euler angles — but, just as for the circle,
not globally.

Choset's choice is **Z-Y-Z Euler angles** $(\phi, \theta, \psi)$ (§3.6): rotate about $z$ by
$\phi$, then about the new $y$ by $\theta$, then about the new $z$ by $\psi$, so that

$$
R = R_{z,\phi} R_{y,\theta} R_{z,\psi} =
\begin{bmatrix}
c_\phi c_\theta c_\psi - s_\phi s_\psi & -c_\phi c_\theta s_\psi - s_\phi c_\psi & c_\phi s_\theta \\
s_\phi c_\theta c_\psi + c_\phi s_\psi & -s_\phi c_\theta s_\psi + c_\phi c_\psi & s_\phi s_\theta \\
-s_\theta c_\psi & s_\theta s_\psi & c_\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad (3.6\text{–}3.8).
$$

**Statement.** The Z-Y-Z map is a chart only on $\{R : R_{33} \ne \pm 1\}$. At $R_{33} = 1$ only
$\phi + \psi$ is determined (Choset eq. E.2, E.9). Unit quaternions $Q = (\cos\tfrac\theta2,
n\sin\tfrac\theta2)$ cover $\SOthree$ with four charts $U_i = \{|q_i| \text{ largest}\}$.

**Step 1 — the collapse.** Put $\theta = 0$ in (3.8). Then $c_\theta = 1$, $s_\theta = 0$ and
$$
R = \begin{bmatrix} c_{\phi+\psi} & -s_{\phi+\psi} & 0 \\ s_{\phi+\psi} & c_{\phi+\psi} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\qquad (\mathrm{E.2}),
$$
a rotation about $z$ by $\phi + \psi$: infinitely many $(\phi, \psi)$ pairs give the same $R$, and
only $\phi + \psi = \operatorname{atan2}(R_{21}, R_{11})$ is defined (E.9). The same happens at
$R_{33} = -1$, where $\theta = \pi$ and only $\phi - \psi$ survives (E.10–E.11). Off those two
rotations, $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(\sqrt{1 - R_{33}^2}, R_{33})$,
$\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(R_{23}, R_{13})$, $\psi = \operatorname{atan2}(R_{32}, -R_{31})$
(E.3, E.5, E.6) invert the map smoothly, so it is a chart there. *Gimbal lock* is the name for
having driven off this chart; nothing mechanical is involved.

**Step 2 — unit quaternions.** For a rotation by $\theta$ about a unit axis $n$,
$Q = (\cos\tfrac\theta2, n_x\sin\tfrac\theta2, n_y\sin\tfrac\theta2, n_z\sin\tfrac\theta2)$ (E.26)
has $\|Q\|^2 = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2 + \sin^2\tfrac\theta2\,(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2) = 1$ (E.27), and
the matrix $R(Q)$ of (E.28) is a rotation; $Q$ and $-Q$ give the same $R$ — the double cover — and
quaternion multiplication is rotation composition.

**Step 3 — four charts cover.** Some component of a unit quaternion has the largest magnitude, at
least $\tfrac12$. On $U_i = \{|q_i| \ge |q_j| \ \forall j\}$ (interiors, to make them open) the map
$\varphi_i(Q) = (q_j / |q_i|)_{j \ne i}$ is a smooth bijection onto an open subset of
$\mathbb{R}^3$, and the $U_i$ cover. Where an Euler chart ends, the quaternion simply moves to a
different $U_i$ — which is what the Rotation Zoo's smooth quaternion curve shows while $\phi$ and
$\psi$ swing. $\blacksquare$

*Kept for Appendix B:* the full inverse (E.3–E.11), roll–pitch–yaw (E.12) and its lock at pitch
$\pm\pi/2$, the axis–angle matrix (E.18) and the chart $\varphi(R) = \theta k$ on
$\{\operatorname{tr} R \ne \pm 1\}$ (E.20–E.21), the quaternion product and conjugate (E.31–E.35).
The exponential and logarithm maps that turn axis–angle into a Lie-group coordinate, and the
$\boxplus/\boxminus$ operators estimators need, are the sister book's
[Chapter 3](https://prvr.shannon.id/chapters/ch03-geometry-of-motion),
whose `So3` the Rust `exp`/`log` of Exercise 6 reuses.
</Derivation>

One more honesty item about $\SOthree$, because Chapter 11's samplers depend on it. *Uniform on
$\SOthree$* means uniform with respect to the Haar measure, the one invariant under rotation. A
normalized Gaussian 4-vector is uniform on the quaternion 3-sphere and therefore Haar-uniform on
$\SOthree$. Uniform *Euler angles* are not: the rotation angle of a Haar-uniform rotation has
density $(1 - \cos\theta)/\pi$ on $[0, \pi]$, mean $\pi/2 + 2/\pi = 2.2074$, and only
$(0.5 - \sin 0.5)/\pi = 0.65\%$ of rotations lie within half a radian of the identity; drawing
$(\phi, \theta, \psi)$ uniformly puts about three times that mass near the identity. `So3::sample`
is the Gaussian version, and a check pins both numbers.

## The algorithm

This chapter's algorithms are operations, not planners: the three methods a manifold must
provide, and the first generic planner-shaped function built on them.

<Algorithm
  name="GEODESIC-PATH(M, a, b, n)"
  inputs="a manifold M with dist and interpolate, endpoints a, b ∈ M, a step count n"
  outputs="n + 1 configurations along the shortest geodesic from a to b"
  complexity="O(n · dim M)"
>
1. for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$ do
2. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;$t \leftarrow i / n$
3. &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;$q_i \leftarrow M.\mathrm{interpolate}(a, b, t)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— the short way, by the metric's own geodesic
4. return $(q_0, q_1, \dots, q_n)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— with $q_0 = a$, $q_n = b$, and $M.\mathrm{dist}(a, q_i) = t_i\, M.\mathrm{dist}(a, b)$
</Algorithm>

The per-manifold operations, in the order the Rust implements them:

<Algorithm
  name="S1 · dist, interpolate, sample"
  inputs="angles a, b ∈ (−π, π]; a parameter t ∈ [0, 1]; a seeded Rng"
  outputs="a distance in [0, π]; an angle on the short arc; a uniform angle"
  complexity="O(1)"
>
1. $\mathrm{dist}(a, b) \leftarrow |\operatorname{wrap}(a - b)|$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— $= \min(|a-b|,\, 2\pi - |a-b|)$
2. $\mathrm{interpolate}(a, b, t) \leftarrow \operatorname{wrap}\big(a + t \cdot \operatorname{wrap}(b - a)\big)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— antipodal tie: $\operatorname{wrap}$ returns $+\pi$, counter-clockwise
3. $\mathrm{sample}() \leftarrow \mathrm{Uniform}(-\pi, \pi)$
4. $T^n$: apply 1–3 componentwise; $\mathrm{dist} \leftarrow \sqrt{\sum_i d_{S^1}(a_i, b_i)^2}$
5. $\SEtwo$ with weight $w$: $\mathrm{dist} \leftarrow \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + w^2 d_{S^1}(\theta, \theta')^2}$; straight in $(x, y)$, short way in $\theta$; sample a box for $(x, y)$ and $\mathrm{Uniform}(-\pi, \pi)$ for $\theta$
</Algorithm>

<Algorithm
  name="SO3 · dist, slerp, Haar sample"
  inputs="unit quaternions Q, Q′; t ∈ [0, 1]; a seeded Rng"
  outputs="the rotation angle between them; the geodesic rotation; a Haar-uniform rotation"
  complexity="O(1)"
>
1. $d \leftarrow \langle Q, Q' \rangle$; if $d < 0$ then $Q' \leftarrow -Q'$, $d \leftarrow -d$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— the double cover: $-Q'$ is the same rotation, nearer on $S^3$
2. $\mathrm{dist} \leftarrow 2\arccos d$, computed as $4\,\operatorname{atan2}(\|Q - Q'\|, \|Q + Q'\|)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— $\arccos$ near 1 loses eight digits
3. $\omega \leftarrow \arccos d$; $\mathrm{slerp}(t) \leftarrow \dfrac{\sin((1-t)\omega)}{\sin\omega}Q + \dfrac{\sin(t\omega)}{\sin\omega}Q'$, normalized; linear blend when $\omega \approx 0$
4. $\mathrm{sample}() \leftarrow (g_0, g_1, g_2, g_3)/\|g\|$ with $g_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$ &nbsp;&nbsp;— uniform on $S^3$, hence Haar on $\SOthree$
5. Euler Z-Y-Z: if $|R_{33}| = 1$ return $\mathrm{Err}(\mathrm{GimbalLock})$ carrying $\phi \pm \psi$; else (E.3, E.5, E.6)
</Algorithm>

Line 2 of the $\SOthree$ box is a numerical honesty item worth a sentence: the first implementation
of `dist` used $2\arccos d$ directly, and `dist(Q, Q)` came out as $6 \times 10^{-8}$, which failed
the definiteness check. The half-angle identity $\arccos x = 2\operatorname{atan2}(\sqrt{1-x},
\sqrt{1+x})$ gives the same number to full precision, and a metric that is not definite to machine
precision breaks every equality test built on it.

## Implementation in Rust

The trait is the chapter. Everything else is four implementations of it, a product, and one
generic function.

```rust title="crates/manifold/src/lib.rs"
use prob::Rng;

/// THE OWNED ARTIFACT. Every planner from Chapter 6 on is generic over this trait
/// and never touches coordinates directly. `DIM` is the manifold dimension — the
/// number of local coordinates — not the embedding dimension (3 for SO(3), not 9).
pub trait Manifold: Copy + PartialEq + core::fmt::Debug {
    const DIM: usize;
    /// Sampling box for non-compact factors; `()` for compact spaces, which have
    /// a uniform distribution of their own and need no box.
    type Bounds;

    /// A metric in the sense of Definition C.2.1 — property-tested, not assumed.
    fn dist(&self, other: &Self) -> f64;
    /// The geodesic from `self` to `other` at `t ∈ [0, 1]`, taken the short way.
    fn interpolate(&self, other: &Self, t: f64) -> Self;
    /// Uniform with respect to the natural measure: Lebesgue on boxes, Haar on groups.
    fn sample(rng: &mut Rng, bounds: &Self::Bounds) -> Self;
}

/// The first generic planner-shaped function in the book: a geodesic local path.
/// Chapter 11's `trait Steer` defaults to exactly this.
pub fn geodesic_path<M: Manifold>(a: &M, b: &M, n: usize) -> Vec<M> {
    (0..=n).map(|i| a.interpolate(b, i as f64 / n as f64)).collect()
}
```

`S1` is a newtype around an `f64`, and the newtype is the point: the constructor wraps, so no
value outside $(-\pi, \pi]$ can exist, and the metric and interpolation never see a seam.

```rust title="crates/manifold/src/s1.rs" {12-14}
use core::f64::consts::{PI, TAU};

/// S¹ as a wrapped angle. The chart is (−π, π]; the type guarantees the representative.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq)]
pub struct S1(f64);

impl S1 {
    /// Wrap into (−π, π]. This is the only way to build an S1, so the invariant holds everywhere.
    pub fn new(theta: f64) -> Self {
        let mut x = (theta + PI).rem_euclid(TAU);
        if x <= 0.0 { x += TAU; }
        S1(x - PI)
    }
    pub fn angle(self) -> f64 { self.0 }
}

impl Manifold for S1 {
    const DIM: usize = 1;
    type Bounds = ();

    /// min(|a − b|, 2π − |a − b|): the quotient metric of ℝ by 2πℤ.
    fn dist(&self, o: &Self) -> f64 {
        let d = (self.0 - o.0).abs();
        d.min(TAU - d)
    }

    /// Travel the short way. At the antipode wrap() returns +π: deterministic, documented.
    fn interpolate(&self, o: &Self, t: f64) -> Self {
        S1::new(self.0 + t * S1::new(o.0 - self.0).0)
    }

    fn sample(rng: &mut Rng, _: &()) -> Self {
        S1::new(rng.uniform(-PI, PI))
    }
}
```

The torus is a product of circles, and $\SEtwo$ is the ported `Pose2` with a weight attached. Note
what `Se2` is *not*: it does not redefine the pose type. `Pose2::compose`, `::inverse` and `::act`

```rust title="crates/manifold/src/{torus.rs, se2.rs}" {25-27}
/// Tⁿ as an array of circles, with the ℓ² product metric. Reach's `T2` from Chapter 2
/// is `Torus<2>` through a `From` impl, so no code in `cspace` changes.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq)]
pub struct Torus<const N: usize>(pub [S1; N]);

impl<const N: usize> Manifold for Torus<N> {
    const DIM: usize = N;
    type Bounds = ();
    fn dist(&self, o: &Self) -> f64 {
        self.0.iter().zip(&o.0).map(|(a, b)| a.dist(b).powi(2)).sum::<f64>().sqrt()
    }
    fn interpolate(&self, o: &Self, t: f64) -> Self {
        let mut out = *self;
        for i in 0..N { out.0[i] = self.0[i].interpolate(&o.0[i], t); }
        out
    }
    fn sample(rng: &mut Rng, _: &()) -> Self {
        Torus(core::array::from_fn(|_| S1::sample(rng, &())))
    }
}

/// SE(2) is the ported Pose2. The metric needs an exchange rate between metres and
/// radians, and that rate has no canonical value — so it is a field, never a default.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq)]
pub struct Se2 { pub pose: Pose2, pub w: f64 }

impl Manifold for Se2 {
    const DIM: usize = 3;
    type Bounds = Aabb; // sampling box for (x, y); θ is uniform on S¹

    fn dist(&self, o: &Self) -> f64 {
        let dth = S1::new(self.pose.theta).dist(&S1::new(o.pose.theta));
        (self.pose.x - o.pose.x).hypot(self.pose.y - o.pose.y).hypot(self.w * dth)
    }
    fn interpolate(&self, o: &Self, t: f64) -> Self {
        let th = S1::new(self.pose.theta).interpolate(&S1::new(o.pose.theta), t);
        Se2 { w: self.w, pose: Pose2 {
            x: self.pose.x + t * (o.pose.x - self.pose.x),
            y: self.pose.y + t * (o.pose.y - self.pose.y),
            theta: th.angle(),
        } }
    }
    fn sample(rng: &mut Rng, b: &Aabb) -> Self {
        Se2 { w: 0.0, pose: Pose2 { x: rng.uniform(b.min.x, b.max.x), y: rng.uniform(b.min.y, b.max.y),
                                    theta: rng.uniform(-PI, PI) } }
    }
}
```

`So3` wraps `nalgebra`'s `UnitQuaternion`, which already gives the product, the conjugate, and

```rust title="crates/manifold/src/so3.rs" {14-21,34-40}
use nalgebra::{Quaternion, SVector, UnitQuaternion};

#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq)]
pub struct So3(pub UnitQuaternion<f64>);

#[derive(Debug, Clone, Copy, PartialEq)]
pub struct GimbalLock { /// The one quantity that survives at R₃₃ = ±1: φ + ψ (or φ − ψ).
                        pub sum: f64 }

impl So3 {
    /// Z-Y-Z Euler angles, eqs. E.3, E.5, E.6, on the chart {R₃₃ ≠ ±1}. Off the chart
    /// there is no answer, and the type says so.
    pub fn euler_zyz(&self) -> Result<[f64; 3], GimbalLock> {
        let r = self.0.to_rotation_matrix();
        let r33 = r[(2, 2)].clamp(-1.0, 1.0);
        if 1.0 - r33.abs() < 1e-9 {
            let sum = if r33 > 0.0 { r[(1, 0)].atan2(r[(0, 0)]) }        // E.9: φ + ψ
                      else { (-r[(0, 1)]).atan2(-r[(0, 0)]) };          // E.11: φ − ψ
            return Err(GimbalLock { sum });
        }
        let theta = (1.0 - r33 * r33).sqrt().atan2(r33);
        Ok([r[(1, 2)].atan2(r[(0, 2)]), theta, r[(2, 1)].atan2(-r[(2, 0)])])
    }

    /// Eqs. E.19–E.21: axis and angle, θ ∈ [0, π], with the sign fixed by w ≥ 0.
    pub fn axis_angle(&self) -> (SVector<f64, 3>, f64) {
        let q = if self.0.w < 0.0 { -self.0.into_inner() } else { self.0.into_inner() };
        let angle = 2.0 * q.w.clamp(-1.0, 1.0).acos();
        let v = q.imag();
        let n = v.norm();
        (if n < 1e-12 { SVector::z() } else { v / n }, angle)
    }
}

impl Manifold for So3 {
    const DIM: usize = 3;
    type Bounds = ();

    /// 2·acos|⟨q, q′⟩|, via 4·atan2(‖q − q′‖, ‖q + q′‖) after a sign flip: the same angle,
    /// but acos near 1 loses eight digits and a metric must be definite to machine precision.
    fn dist(&self, o: &Self) -> f64 {
        let (a, mut b) = (self.0.into_inner(), o.0.into_inner());
        if a.dot(&b) < 0.0 { b = -b; }
        4.0 * (a - b).norm().atan2((a + b).norm())
    }
    /// Spherical linear interpolation toward the nearer of ±q′ — the short way on S³.
    fn interpolate(&self, o: &Self, t: f64) -> Self {
        let b = if self.0.dot(&o.0) < 0.0 { UnitQuaternion::new_unchecked(-o.0.into_inner()) } else { o.0 };
        So3(self.0.slerp(&b, t))
    }
    /// Haar-uniform: a normalized Gaussian 4-vector is uniform on S³.
    fn sample(rng: &mut Rng, _: &()) -> Self {
        let q = Quaternion::new(rng.normal(), rng.normal(), rng.normal(), rng.normal());
        So3(UnitQuaternion::from_quaternion(q))
    }
}

/// A × B with the ℓ² product metric — SE(2) × S¹ for Hitch, Chapter 14's composites.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, PartialEq)]
pub struct Product<A: Manifold, B: Manifold>(pub A, pub B);
```

### The type system knows that $T^2 \ne SE(2)$

The compile error is a feature. Choset's §3.7 lists the configuration spaces of common robots and
then a list of inequalities — $S^1 \times S^1 \times S^1 \ne SO(3)$, $SE(2) \ne \mathbb{R}^3$,
$SE(3) \ne \mathbb{R}^6$ — that a planner written against coordinates cannot see. One written
against `Manifold` cannot *fail* to see them:

```rust title="crates/manifold/tests/ui/mix_spaces.rs (checked by trybuild: must NOT compile)"
use manifold::{geodesic_path, Se2, Torus};

fn main() {
    let q_reach: Torus<2> = reach_home();          // Reach lives on T²
    let rusty: Se2 = Se2 { pose: rusty_pose(), w: 0.5 }; // Rusty lives on SE(2)
    let _path = geodesic_path(&q_reach, &rusty, 10);
    //                                  ^^^^^^ error[E0308]: mismatched types
    //                                  expected `&Torus<2>`, found `&Se2`
}
```

`trybuild` asserts that this file fails to compile with exactly that message. There is no runtime
check anywhere in the planners for "are these two configurations in the same space?", because
there does not need to be.

<Callout type="info">
Why the weight $w$ is a field and not a constant: with $w = 0.5$ the two poses in the example are
$1.86$ apart; with $w = 2$ they are $6.36$ apart and a half-turn in place costs more than driving
six metres. Both are metrics. Which one a PRM should use depends on the robot and the task, and
Chapter 11 measures the consequences. Hiding $w$ in a default would hide a modelling decision.
</Callout>

## Putting it together: components on the square and on the torus

The integration lab turns §3.4.3 into a measured quantity. Take Chapter 4's raster of Reach's
C-space on the Workbench — here a $48 \times 48$ grid over $[-\pi, \pi]^2$ for the arm among three
disc obstacles, built inside this chapter's module because the general raster lands with Chapter 4 —
and count connected components of the free cells twice: once as a square, with 4-connectivity and
no wrapping, and once as a torus, with the opposite edges identified.

The square has **2** components; the torus has **1**. One of the discs lies within reach of the
first link, and a first-link collision does not care about $\theta_2$, so its C-obstacle is a band
across the whole chart. The band cuts the square in two. On the torus the two halves meet through
the seam at $\theta_1 = \pm\pi$, and `seam_connection` finds the closest pair of cells that the
square separates and the torus joins: two cells at $d_{T^2} = 0.2618$ — two raster cells apart —
whose chart distance is $6.0214$. That is the purple path of the hook, measured.

Monotonicity is the invariant the lab asserts: identifying edges can only *merge* components, never
split them, so the torus count is at most the square count, on every raster. With joint limits
$|\theta_i| < 3.0$ the outermost ring of cells is forbidden, the seam becomes a wall, and both
counts are **2**: the topology changed, and the count noticed.

The last thread to tie is Chapter 4's Jacobian. At $q = (\pi/4, \pi/2)$ the matrix of
$D\varphi_q$ sends $\dot q = (1, 0)$ to $(-\sqrt2, 0) = (-1.4142, 0)$, Choset's Example 3.8.1, and
shifting $\theta_1$'s chart by $2\pi$ changes no entry by more than $10^{-15}$. The Jacobian is the
matrix of a chart-independent linear map between tangent spaces; Chapter 20 makes tangent spaces
formal and Chapter 7 uses $J^{\mathsf T}$ to pull forces back from the workspace to the joints.

Everything downstream is now generic. Chapter 6 builds graphs whose edge costs are `dist`; Chapter 7
needs the tangent spaces this chapter named informally; Chapter 11's kd-tree splits on
`Chart::coords` with `periodic` axes and its samplers call `sample`; Chapters 12–14's tree planners
and composite spaces are generic over `Manifold`; Chapter 17 needs $\SOthree$'s inertia; Chapters
20–21 live in $\SEtwo$ as the car's group. None of them will do arithmetic on coordinates.
